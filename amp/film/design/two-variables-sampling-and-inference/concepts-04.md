#### `model-residual-conclusion` — Read the residuals; choose the conclusion they support.

**Standard.** HSS.ID.B.6b: "Informally assess the fit of a function by plotting and analyzing residuals." The extrapolation case also leans on 6a's "use functions fitted to data to solve problems in the context of the data": the context is where the data are, and x = 30 is not in it.

**Three kinds under one name**, so three screens: the misses show a bend (try a curve); the misses show nothing (the line is fine, for these x's); the misses are fine but the question asks about an x far outside them (the residuals are silent). The first is the full example because its "why" is a motion; the other two are quick, each on its own screen, because their choices are different sentences.

**Foundations first** (drawn live on the first data set, before its question is asked). *Predicted* is what the line says at this x; *observed* is what was measured; the *residual* is the miss, observed minus predicted, and its sign is the dot's side of the line: above, positive. The *residual plot* is every miss re-hung from a level zero line at its own x, so the line is taken out and only the misses remain. Two facts said plainly: every fitted line misses, since real measurements never sit on a line, and a fitted line's misses always add to about zero, as much above as below, because that is how the line was placed. So "there are misses," "the misses add to zero," and "some are positive, some negative" are true of *every* line and tell this line from no other. What does tell: the *order* of the signs along x, and the *size* of the misses against the measurements. A fit leaves a shapeless band of small misses switching sign with no run. A line through a bend is too high at both ends and too low in the middle (or the reverse), so its misses run one sign, then the other, then back. And misses were measured only at the x's in the data; they say nothing about any other x.

**Read.** Name x and y with units (hours; km). The wall of text is one list: write the seven residuals' signs in a row, left to right, and their sizes. The deciding word in the question goes gold: "conclusion the residuals support," or "x = 30" against "x = 1 to 7." Each choice is two halves, a fact about the residuals and a conclusion; both must hold. At a glance: a run of one sign, then the other, then back is a bend; switching with no run and all misses small against the data is a fit; an x asked outside the data's range is an extrapolation whatever the residuals look like.

**The example.** "A linear model ŷ = 5x + 3 predicts the distance a cyclist has ridden (km) after x hours. ... Which conclusion do the residuals support?" (1) The data restated as a table beside the plot, filling row by row: x, observed, predicted; the residual column held back. (2) The cloud: axes "Hours ridden" 0 to 8 and "Distance (km)" 0 to 40, to scale; dots drop at (1, 2), (2, 12), (3, 21), (4, 27), (5, 31), (6, 32), (7, 36); the blue line draws through (0, 3) and (7, 38). Before any arithmetic the eye sees it: the end dots sit under the line, the middle dots over it. (3) The residual at x = 1: walk up to the line, ŷ = 8; the dot is at 2, below the line, so negative; a rust stick from dot to line, −6. At x = 3: the line says 18, the dot is at 21, above: +3. The remaining five sticks are read the same way in a quick count, each sign said from the dot's side, the table's residual column filling in step: −6, −1, +3, +4, +3, −1, −2. (4) Hang: every stick slides down into the strip under the plot, hung from the zero line at its own x, and its sign is written under it: − − + + + − −. (5) Read the row like a word: below, below, above, above, above, below, below. The line is too high at the ends and too low in the middle; the dots bow up through the middle, as a tiring cyclist's distance does. (6) The proof: a gentle curve draws through the cloud; the sticks re-hang against it and come out small, switching sign with no run. The straight line was the wrong shape. (7) The choices: three dim together with one reason, "each of these is true of every line's misses, so it cannot tell this line from any other" (not exactly on the line; add to zero; some positive and some negative). The one left is read in halves against the strip: negative, then positive, then negative: yes. A curved model should be tried: yes.

**Quick example, no pattern** (snow depth, ŷ = 6x + 12): the same build, fast: sticks −1, +2, −2, +1, +1, −2, +1 hung; signs − + − + + − +; every miss within 2 cm of depths from 17 to 55 cm; a shapeless band. Reasonable, *for these values of x*; the muted bracket under the x axis marks 1 to 7 as the half of the sentence that matters. Dim: "not all 0" (no line's are), "accurately at any x including 100" (far past the bracket), "poor because mixed signs" (every line's).

**Quick example, extrapolation** (seedling, ŷ = 6x + 13, "predict at x = 30"): the strip of small, patternless misses lives over x = 1 to 7. The walk to x = 30 goes muted at the bracket's edge and carries "beyond the data"; the card pulls back until the data are a sliver near the axis and the line reads 193 cm. A seedling two meters tall at week 30, from seven weeks of measurements: nothing was measured there, so the residuals say nothing there. Dim: "no prediction can ever be made" (an absolute), "reliable because the model is a straight line" (a shape does not make data appear), "reliable because the residuals for 1 to 7 are small" (the bracket). The one left says it is an extrapolation and the residuals do not show it reliable.

**Fast.** Write the signs in order. One run, then the other, then back: a curve. Switching with no run, misses small against the measurements: a fit, for those x's. Any x past the data: the residuals are silent.

**Sense.** The sign row must match the picture: a bend in the dots against the line for one, a band for the other. For the extrapolation, the context itself objects (a 193 cm seedling).

**Check.** Recompute two residuals as observed minus predicted; each sign matches its dot's side; the seven add to about zero (a check on the subtraction, never on the fit); the asked x against the data's range.

**Rule out.** Anything true of every fitted line's misses (there are misses; they add to zero; mixed signs; not all zero) dims together. Any absolute ("any x," "ever," "accurately at 100"). "Reliable" for an x outside the bracket.

**Line.** "A good line leaves no pattern in its misses. And misses speak only where the dots are."

**Trap.** Two real ones. Students expect a good model's residuals to be zero, so any miss means the model, or the data, is wrong ("a curve is needed because the residuals are not all 0"; "the data must contain mistakes"). The band strip of a good fit, misses and all, is the first thing they see, and the foundation says aloud that every line misses. And students take small misses over 1 to 7 as a license for x = 30. The pull-back that shrinks the data to a sliver beside a two-meter seedling ends it. If the cast is used, this is its place: the student says "the misses aren't zero, so the line is wrong?" and the teacher answers "every line misses; the question is whether the misses have a shape."

**Kit.** `scatter` as in the family design: `data` to scale with named axes and the muted `s.range` bracket, `line` in blue, `stick`, `residuals` (the hung strip), `walk` with `"view": true`. Gains: a `signs` row under the strip, each sign written as its stick lands; `model` with a second equation (the writer fits a quadratic to the data) that draws the curve and re-hangs the sticks against it; and the problem's data restated as a `table` beside the plot filling row by row (exists). The long problem card must wrap (exists).

**Writer.** The strongest writer: a judgment type whose insight (every decoy is true of every line) and whose "why" (the curve re-hung) must be staged, not described, across three cases.
