# Two variables, sampling and inference, part 3: cause and design, inference, reports

Ten problem types across `causal-claims` (A1), `study-design` (A2), `statistical-inference`, `simulation-model-checks`, `sampling-margin-error`, `randomization-tests` and `statistical-report-critique` (A2). Based on the family design: per type, how to read it, the one example, the sure way, sense, check, the realistic trap, the kit, the writer.

Two shared foundations run under these ten and are built once, then named in a line.

**Who put each person in their group?** (foundations 9 and 10). The crowd; the sample pulled into a frame; then the split. A coin flips above each dot and it slides teal or rust to its side: the researcher assigned by chance, an *experiment*, and since chance has no favorites the two groups are alike in everything but the treatment, so a difference can be credited to it. Or the dots arrive already colored and sort themselves: the people were already in their groups, an *observational study*, and whatever else makes them different travels with them, so a difference is an *association* only. Separately: who picked the participants? Scattered across the whole crowd from a complete list: the result can be *generalized* to that crowd. One corner, or the ones who raised a hand: these participants only. Two randomnesses, two conclusions, a two-by-two table on the board.

**What would chance alone draw?** (foundations 3, 11, 12). Results stacking on a number line, one square cell each, countable. Ten classmates' counts in grade 7; a hundred simulated sets of twenty; two hundred reshuffled differences. The pile's middle is the estimate; its half-width is the margin of error; its tails say whether an observed result is rare. Every Algebra 2 lesson here opens by building the pile in front of the learner before it reads the problem's finished chart.

### Algebra 1: causal-claims (HSS.ID.C.9; the three types also serve HSS.IC.B.3 and B.5, which the catalog places in Algebra 2)

**`study-design-choice` — Choose and read study designs.** A mixed type: three questions under one name (classify and state what it can show; pick the design that can show cause; read a results table and say whether cause is justified). The lesson teaches reading; one example per kind, each on its own screen. Filed in A1 under HSS.ID.C.9; two of its three kinds practice HSS.IC.B.3 ("differences among ... experiments, and observational studies; explain how randomization relates to each") and the table kind practices HSS.IC.B.5 ("use data from a randomized experiment to compare two treatments"). Say so in the alignment list; the lesson stands either way.
Foundations first: the coin split and the self-sorted split, drawn on the crowd; the words experiment, observational study, treatment, random assignment, comparison group; the line from family 32: groups of different sizes are compared by percents. Two `term` cards: experiment, observational study.
Read: find the verb that says how the groups were formed, gold: "randomly assigned," "flips a coin" against "asked whether they had," "signed up," "chooses," "already." For a table, "of each group" is the total to divide by. At a glance: a choice saying "without random assignment" when the problem says "randomly assigned" is false on its face, and the reverse.
Example (the table kind, full): "Researchers randomly assigned 180 students either to use a vocabulary app or to study as usual. The groups had 60 and 120 students." Table: app 48 passed, 12 not, 60; no app 72 passed, 48 not, 120. (1) The crowd of 180; the coin flips above each dot; 60 slide teal, 120 rust: the researcher did it, by chance. (2) The band lights the app row: 48 of 60; the percent writes beside it, 80%. (3) The band moves to the no-app row: 72 of 120, 60%. (4) Mosaic: two slabs, 60 and 120 tall, colored 80% and 60%: a step of twenty points. The app group did better. (5) Back to the coin: random assignment made the groups alike, so the step is evidence of a cause. Rule out as the choices come: "about equally well" dims (a twenty-point step); "72 against 48" dims (counts from a group twice the size; the mosaic's slab is taller but its colored share is smaller); "without random assignment" dims (the problem said randomly assigned). Prove the one left.
Quick case (the classify kind): "asked 30 students whether they had used a vocabulary app": the dots arrive already colored; observational; association only. Quick case (the design kind): four designs on four lines; for each, who put each member in a group: "text every member, compare with last month" has no comparison group at all (the month changed too); "signed up" and "each member chooses" let the people sort themselves; "randomly assign" is the one where the researcher did it by chance. That one can show cause.
Fast: the verb. Researcher by chance: experiment, cause allowed. People themselves: observational, association only. Table: cell over its own row.
Sense: a conclusion about cause needs both a real step in the percents and a coin; one without the other is not enough.
Check: read the chosen sentence in two halves (the fact, the conclusion) and test each against the picture.
Line: "Who put each person in their group?"
Trap: "They compared two groups, so it is an experiment." The runner variant (a coach asked who had stretched) compared two groups and is observational: the dots sorted themselves. And "72 against 48": the mosaic ends it.
Kit: `crowd.assign` with `how: coin` and `how: self`; `table.percent` writing the share beside the band; mosaic exists.
Writer: strongest. Three kinds, each decided by one sentence found in a story, and a cast's first wrong read ("more passed without the app") is the lesson.

**`correlation-causation-choice` — Direction of a correlation, and whether it shows cause.** Badly worded, first: "neither causes the other" is a claim the data cannot make either; the honest answer, which `causal-claim-choice` already uses, is "a third factor explains it; these data cannot show a cause." Fix the choices. Also, the "one causes the other" variants (miles and gasoline, height and air pressure, winter temperature and heating oil, generator hours and fuel) are right only because the mechanism is known from how the world works, not from the correlation; the lesson must say that aloud or it teaches the opposite of HSS.ID.C.9.
Standard: HSS.ID.C.9, "Distinguish between correlation and causation."
Foundations first: positive and negative correlation as the cloud's lean, one line from family 32 (an oval tilting up, tilting down). Then the three-box diagram: two measured things side by side; a third thing above with an arrow to each. A *lurking variable* is a third thing that pushes both. The question that decides: can you point at the link from one to the other (more miles burn more gasoline), or is there something that moves both?
Read: two halves, direction then cause; both must hold. At a glance: when both amounts rise together, the two "negative" choices are gone before thinking about cause.
Example (full): firefighters sent and damage done. (1) Direction: bigger fires get more firefighters and do more damage; both rise together: positive. The two negative choices dim. (2) The tempting read, said by the student: "so the firefighters cause the damage?" (3) The diagram: "size of the fire" appears above, arrows to "firefighters sent" and "damage." The fire pushes both. Take the fire away and the firefighters do nothing to the damage. (4) Positive correlation; a third factor explains it; these data cannot show a cause.
Quick cases: miles driven and gasoline used: both rise; the link is direct, each mile burns fuel: positive, one causes the other. Generator hours and fuel left: as hours rise, fuel falls: negative; each hour burns fuel: one causes the other. Three cases because sign and mechanism each change.
Fast: direction first, by asking "as this goes up, does that go up or down?"; then "what would make both move?" If the answer is a third thing, cause is not shown.
Sense: a sentence beginning "sending fewer firefighters would reduce damage" is absurd; absurdity means a lurking variable.
Check: name the third thing out loud, or name the direct link. One or the other must exist.
Line: "Goes with is not causes."
Trap: reading cause from "they rise together." The firefighters case is the realistic one, and the three-box diagram is what makes it impossible.
Kit: none beyond labels, segments and `vector` arrows for the diagram.
Writer: strongest. Every answer is a judgment about the world, and the right answers are right for two different reasons that must be kept apart.

**`study-design-classify` — Experiment or observational, with the reason.**
Standard: HSS.IC.B.3, "differences among ... experiments, and observational studies; explain how randomization relates to each." The reason is half the answer, so the decoys are wrong reasons.
Foundations first: the coin split and the self-sorted split, named; then why the other two reasons are never reasons: every study measures a response (the doctor measured blood pressure in the records too), and no study's subjects are all alike (that is what random assignment copes with, not what it removes).
Read: the verb, gold: "reviews the records," "already take," "already use" against "flips a coin," "randomly assigns," "randomly picks half to receive." At a glance: two choices are gone whatever the study is.
Example (full): "A doctor reviews the records of 60 patients, some of whom already take a vitamin, and compares their blood pressure." (1) Sixty dots arrive already colored, teal takers and rust non-takers, and sort themselves; the doctor's hand only writes. (2) Whatever made these patients choose the vitamin travels with them into their group. (3) Observational, because the researcher only recorded values for groups that already existed. Rule out: "measured a response variable" and "subjects were not all alike" dim together (true of every study, so reasons for nothing); "assigned who received each treatment" dims: nobody assigned.
Quick case: "flips a coin for each of 60 students": the coin above each dot; experiment, because the researcher assigned.
Fast: who put each subject in their group. Researcher, by chance: experiment. Already there: observational.
Sense: a study you could do from a filing cabinet is observational.
Check: say the reason aloud and ask whether it names who assigned.
Line: "Who put each person in their group?"
Trap: "a doctor doing a study on 60 patients is an experiment." The records picture: nobody was given anything. And "randomly picks half of 80 plots" read as random selection; it is assignment, because the picking decides the treatment.
Kit: `crowd.assign` with `how: self` and `how: coin`.
Writer: a capable cheaper model, from this concept, if the two always-wrong reasons are dimmed exactly as written; the simplest of the five design types and a fair blind-test candidate.

### Algebra 2: study-design (HSS.IC.B.3)

**`scope-of-inference-choice` — Which conclusions does the design support?** Flag first: the problem text is cut off mid-word at about 400 characters in two variants ("outcome diffe", "for the par"); the card shows a broken sentence. And the type asks two different questions (generalizing only; the pair) with choice sets of different shapes. Settle one format; the lesson teaches the two-by-two so either wording maps onto it.
Standards: HSS.IC.B.3 ("explain how randomization relates to each"), HSS.IC.A.1 ("inferences about population parameters based on a random sample from that population").
Foundations first: two randomnesses, drawn on the crowd. Selection: 120 dots light scattered over all 900 ("from a complete list, every set the same chance"), or 60 light in one corner ("easiest to reach"), or 100 light where hands go up ("volunteer"). Assignment: the coin, or the dots sorting themselves ("each participant chooses"). Then the two-by-two on the board: rows random pick yes/no, columns random assign yes/no; a check for "generalize to the population named" in the random-pick row, a check for "cause, for these participants" in the random-assign column. "With sampling uncertainty" rides with generalizing; "exact results for every one" is never written.
Read: two sentences decide, both gold: the one about how participants entered, the one about who assigned. The population is named once ("all 900 students at Cedar School"); anything bigger ("the district," "the region") is outside it. At a glance: "exact results" is nonsense; "throughout the district" is a bigger crowd than was sampled.
Example (the pair kind, full): "120 participants are drawn at random without replacement from a complete list of all 900 students at Cedar School ... Each participant chooses whether to use a diagram-based or a text-only sheet." (1) Crowd of 900; 120 light scattered everywhere: random reaches everywhere; the pick row gets its check: generalize to the 900, with uncertainty. (2) The 120 pulled into a frame; they arrive already colored and sort themselves; no coin; the assign column gets no check: the better scores may belong to the kind of student who chooses diagrams. (3) Supports generalization; does not support causal inference. Rule out: the two "does not support generalization" choices dim together (the pick was random from the complete list); of the two left, "supports causal" dims (nobody assigned).
Quick case (the selection kind): "60 of the 600 students who are easiest to reach ... the researcher randomly assigns half": one corner lights; the coin flips; cause for these 60, yes; generalize, no. "Exact results" and "all students throughout the district" dim at once; "with sampling uncertainty" belongs to a random pick and there was none; the design alone does not justify generalizing beyond the participants.
Fast: two check boxes. Random pick from the whole? Random assign? Each earns exactly its own conclusion.
Sense: a conclusion can reach no further than the randomness that was used.
Check: point at the sentence in the problem that earned each check, and at the missing one.
Line: "Random pick: generalize. Random assign: cause."
Trap: the word "randomly" appearing anywhere earning both checks. In the quick case "randomly assigns" sits beside "easiest to reach"; the two-by-two keeps them in separate cells. And "120 is plenty": size is not randomness.
Kit: `crowd.pick` with `how: random`, `how: region`, and a `how: volunteer` (dots light scattered but self-chosen, marked with a raised halo); `crowd.assign`; the two-by-two as a `table` with `fill` of a check glyph.
Writer: strongest. The deciding sentences hide in a long story, and the realistic misreads are exactly the decoys.

### Algebra 2: statistical-inference (HSS.IC.A.1)

**`margin-of-error-interval` — The interval from an estimate and its margin.**
Standards: HSS.IC.B.4's first clause, "use data from a sample survey to estimate a population mean or proportion"; HSS.IC.A.1. The margin is given, never developed (the second clause of B.4 has no type; see `sample-size-margin`).
Foundations first: the pile. A hundred samples of 1420 residents, each giving a percent, drop onto a line from 40 to 60 and stack: almost all within about 5 of the middle. One sample alone misses by about that much. So the truth is very likely within 5 of our 49: that stretch is the *interval*, and 5 is the *margin of error*. "95% confidence" is said once as "very likely." One `term` card: margin of error. Then the words: "percentage points" means add and subtract on the percent scale (1 point on 41% is 40 to 42; it is not 1% of 41).
Read: the estimate and the margin, both gold, in the same units. At a glance: the interval is centered on the estimate and twice the margin wide; an interval with the estimate at one end is wrong before any arithmetic.
Example (full): estimate 49%, margin 5 percentage points. (1) Number line 35 to 65; 49 drops as a dot. (2) Arms of 5 extend each way, labeled −5 and +5. (3) The stretch 44 to 54 shades, both ends filled. (4) From 44% to 54%. Rule out: "39% to 49%" and "49% to 59%" dim together (the estimate is an endpoint, so the interval reaches only one way); "46.5% to 51.5%" dims (five wide in all; the margin is five each way).
Quick case: a mean of 104 liters with a margin of 8 liters: 96 to 112 liters; the same arms in the problem's units.
Fast: estimate minus margin, estimate plus margin.
Sense: the estimate sits dead center; the two ends are two margins apart.
Check: the midpoint of the chosen interval is the estimate; each end is one margin from it.
Line: "Estimate, plus or minus the margin."
Trap: one-sided ("add the margin": 49 to 59) and half-margin ("plus or minus 5 means 5 wide": 46.5 to 51.5). Both are among the decoys; the arms drawn both ways make the first impossible and their labels the second.
Kit: `numberline` with `interval` (both ends filled) and the arms; `pile` with a hundred listed results for the opening.
Writer: a capable cheaper model; one method, one picture, the arithmetic checked by `--dry`.

**`sample-mean-estimate` — A population mean or total from a sample mean.** Off grade, first: this is grade-7 arithmetic (7.SP.A.2) sitting in Algebra 2, and its "estimate the population mean" variant is "write the sample mean again." Keep both as base cases; neither is a lesson's main example on its own, and the type leaves HSS.IC.B.4's "develop a margin of error through simulation" unpracticed. Standards: HSS.IC.B.4's first clause, HSS.IC.A.1, 7.SP.A.2.
Foundations first: the sample mean stands in for the population mean (the pile's middle, one line); "about," because another sample would differ. The total is every plant's height added, and we know only that the average plant is about 15, so the total is about 15 for each of the 3,800: the mean times the population, never the sample.
Read: which is asked, the mean or the total, gold; the population size and the sample size, both named, and only one of them belongs in a total. At a glance: a total is far larger than any plant; 15 × 50 is 750 centimeters, the height of a tall tree, nowhere near 3,800 plants.
Example (full): 50 plants of 3,800, mean height 15 cm; estimate the sum of all heights. (1) The crowd of 3,800 as a labeled block, too many to draw; 50 dots pulled into a frame; their leveled mean bar at 15 (family 32). (2) "On average each plant is about 15 cm, and there are 3,800 plants": 15 × 3,800. (3) Stacked: 57,000. (4) About 57,000 cm. Quick case: "estimate the population mean height" from a sample mean of 13: about 13; nothing to compute; another sample would say a little differently.
Fast: mean times population size for a total; the mean itself for a mean.
Sense: 3,800 is about 4,000, times 15 is about 60,000.
Check: 57,000 ÷ 3,800 = 15.
Line: "The sample's mean stands in for everyone's; a total is that mean, once for every member."
Trap: multiplying by the sample size, 15 × 50 = 750. The block of 3,800 beside the frame of 50 makes the choice of multiplier visible.
Kit: `crowd` block form with count; `stack`.
Writer: a capable cheaper model; one computation and a base case.

### Algebra 2: simulation-model-checks (HSS.IC.A.2)

**`simulation-consistency-check` — Is the model consistent with the result?** Two cases that are different acts: one tail when a suspicion names a direction; both tails when the question says "either direction." The cutoff differs by problem (5%, 2%, 12%) and one variant lands on the boundary (2 of 100 against "fewer than 2%"). Pilot type.
Standard: HSS.IC.A.2, "Decide if a specified model is consistent with results from a given data-generating process, e.g., using simulation."
Foundations first: the claim is a *model*: "each shot goes in with probability one half." To *simulate* is to let chance run the claim: twenty coin tosses stand for twenty shots. Three sets of twenty are tossed and counted on screen (say 11, 9, 12), each count dropping as a cell onto the pile; then a hundred in a burst, the total ticking. The pile is tall near 10 and thin at the ends: this is what chance alone draws when the claim is true. One `term` card: consistent ("chance alone could easily have drawn this"). "At least as extreme" is a region of the pile, from the observed result outward, never one column; the observed column is inside it. The cutoff is read from the problem, and "fewer than" is strict.
Read: the observed result (16), the direction ("you suspect ... greater," so one side), the region ("16 or more"), the cutoff ("fewer than 5%"), all gold in turn. At a glance: 16 is far from 10 on a pile that is tall in the middle, so the tail must be small; 99 of 100 and 50 of 100 are the other side of the pile.
Example (full, one tail): the shooter, 16 of 20, suspicion greater, cutoff 5%, on the problem's own chart. (1) The gold mark at 16. (2) The columns at 16 and beyond fill rust one at a time, the count writing as they fill: 1 at 16, none at 17, 18, 19, 20: 1 of 100. (3) 1%; the board writes 1% < 5%. (4) Rare: if she really made half her shots, chance would draw 16 or more about once in a hundred tries. So the result is not consistent with the model; chance is an unlikely explanation. Said twice, the second time plainly: rare means not chance. Rule out: "99 of 100" dims (the wrong side of the mark); "0 of 100" dims (left out the observed column, and 16 or more includes 16); "1 of 100: consistent" dims (1% is below the cutoff, so the conclusion runs the wrong way).
Example (full, both tails, boundary): the coin, 4 heads of 20, "at least as far from 10 as 4 is, in either direction," cutoff "fewer than 2%." Why a second: the mirror and the strict boundary are two things the first never shows. (1) Mark at 4; the center 10; the mirror mark at 16. (2) Both tails fill: 0 through 4 on the left, 16 through 20 on the right, the count adding across both: 2 of 100. (3) 2%, against "fewer than 2%": 2 is not fewer than 2. Consistent with the model; chance could explain it.
Fast: mark, shade outward (one side, or both mirrored about the center), count, percent, compare with the cutoff using its exact words.
Sense: a far-out result has a small tail; a result near the center has a large one. A percent above 50 is the wrong side.
Check: recount the tail from the far end of the pile back to the mark; reread the cutoff's words and the conclusion's direction together.
Line: "Ask what chance alone would do. Rare means not chance."
Trap: "exactly 16" instead of the region; forgetting the second tail; comparing the count 16 to the cutoff 5; and the inversion, "it is rare, so it is surprising, so ... consistent." The tail filled column by column kills the first three; saying the direction twice kills the fourth. The boundary is a trap too, and the second example is built around it.
Kit: `pile` with `drop`, `mark`, `tail` (`side: upper`, `side: both` with `center`), `tailcount`; the same `mark` and `tail` as scene actions on the app's histogram so the count happens on the problem's chart.
Writer: strongest. The inversion, two tail shapes and a strict boundary are reasoning a weak author describes instead of staging.

### Algebra 2: sampling-margin-error (no linked standard; serves HSS.IC.B.4)

**`sample-size-margin` — A bigger comparable sample, a smaller margin.** Flag first: the type has no standard and is the whole of a skill whose description says "simulate sampling margins of error," yet nothing is simulated, and there is one variant. It serves HSS.IC.B.4's "margin of error ... for random sampling" in spirit. The pile built here would carry the missing simulation type at once.
Foundations first: the pile twice. A hundred random samples of 200 from the same population drop and stack: wide, bracketed about 7 points each way. Beside it, a hundred samples of 800: narrow, about 3 or 4 each way. The margin of error is the pile's half-width; more people, tighter pile. Then the caution: no pile is a point; no sample size makes an estimate exactly right. And "comparable": same population, same random method, same question, same confidence; only then does size alone decide.
Read: the sizes, gold: 200 and 800; "same population ... same question and confidence level ... comparable": the conditions that make size the only difference. At a glance: two choices promise certainty ("must be identical," "guaranteed to have an estimate equal to") and statistics never does.
Example (the one problem, full): (1) The two piles built side by side in a burst each, brackets drawn. (2) "Must be identical" and "guaranteed equal to the population proportion" dim together: no pile is a point, and the two piles are plainly different widths. (3) Survey B's pile is the narrow one: Survey B generally has a smaller margin of error. "Generally" because any single sample can miss by more; the pile has ends.
Fast: comparable conditions, larger sample, smaller margin.
Sense: four times the people gives about half the margin (the wide pile is about twice the narrow one); the lesson shows it, does not derive it.
Check: the chosen sentence names the larger sample and says "generally," not "must."
Line: "More people, tighter pile. Nothing makes it a point."
Trap: choosing the confident wording ("guaranteed") over the hedged true one, because certainty sounds like knowledge. The two piles with visible ends make "guaranteed" absurd.
Kit: two `pile`s with listed results and `spread` brackets.
Writer: strongest, as the family design says: a short lesson whose every decoy is a belief students hold; or a cheaper model under strong review if the blind test on `study-design-classify` passes.

### Algebra 2: randomization-tests (HSS.IC.B.5)

**`randomization-test-decision` — Is the difference between two treatments significant?** Flag first: the sprint variant says "3 tenths of a second," then "a difference of 3 or more"; the chart is in tenths and the text slips units. Say "3 tenths" both times. Two cases: both tails ("whether the groups differ") and one tail ("whether A gave a higher mean"); one variant sits on the boundary (22 of 200 = 11% against "less than 11%").
Standard: HSS.IC.B.5, "Use data from a randomized experiment to compare two treatments; use simulations to decide if differences between parameters are significant."
Foundations first: volunteers randomly assigned (the coin, one line) to plant food A and B; two dot plots at real growth values, teal and rust; each mean bar; the difference bracketed, A minus B. Then the idea that is the lesson: if the plant food made no difference, the labels A and B are arbitrary, so lift the colors off, shuffle them, deal them back while every plant keeps its height, recompute the two means and their difference. Three reshuffles by hand, each new difference dropping onto a pile centered at 0; then two hundred in a burst. The pile is what chance alone does to the difference. *Significant*: the observed difference is one chance would rarely give. One `term` card: significant.
Read: the observed difference and its order (A minus B), the direction asked ("whether the groups differ": both tails; "whether A gave a higher mean": one), the region ("6 or more"), the cutoff ("less than 5%"), gold in turn. At a glance: a 6 cm difference far out on a pile centered at 0 has a tiny tail; "199 of 200" is the other side.
Example (full, one tail): plant food, observed 6 cm, "whether A gave a higher mean growth," cutoff 5%, on the problem's chart. (1) Mark at 6. (2) Columns at 6 and beyond fill rust, the count writing: 1 of 200. (3) 0.5% < 5%: significant; chance is an unlikely explanation. (4) And because the assignment was by coin, the groups were alike in everything else, so the difference is evidence that plant food A worked. Rule out: "199 of 200" dims (wrong side); "1 of 200: not significant" dims (0.5% is below the cutoff; the direction runs wrong); "2 of 200" dims (does not match the shaded columns).
Example (full, both tails, boundary): plant food, observed 4 cm, "whether the groups differ," cutoff "less than 11%." Why a second: the mirror about 0 and the strict boundary. (1) Marks at 4 and at −4. (2) Both tails fill: 4 and above, −4 and below, the count adding: 22 of 200. (3) 11%, against "less than 11%": not less. Not significant; chance could explain it. A difference this size turns up about one reshuffle in nine with the labels arbitrary.
Fast: mark the observed difference (and its mirror when the question says "differ"), shade outward, count, percent, compare with the cutoff's exact words.
Sense: far from zero, small tail; near zero, large tail; a percent over 50 is the wrong side.
Check: recount from the far end; reread "less than" and the conclusion together; for a significant result, say why cause is allowed (the coin).
Line: "If the treatment did nothing, the labels are arbitrary. Rare means not chance."
Trap: one tail when the question says "differ"; comparing 22 to 11; the inversion; and "significant means the plant food helped a lot": significance says the difference is unlikely to be chance, not that it is large. The reshuffle built live is what makes "the labels are arbitrary" believable.
Kit: `groups` with `mean`, `reshuffle`, `send`; `pile` with `mark`, `tail` (`side: both`, `center: 0`); the scene actions on the app's chart.
Writer: strongest. The reshuffle is the lesson, and staging it is the whole difference from describing it.

### Algebra 2: statistical-report-critique (HSS.IC.B.6)

**`report-flaw-critique` — What weakens the headline?** A mixed type (one flaw, or two) that teaches reading. Flag first: a voluntary-response report that states a margin of error has a flaw the choices never offer, since a margin assumes random sampling; the lesson says that in one honest sentence and then answers what is asked. Also "most weakens" when a report has several flaws: the single-flaw variants are built so only one check fails; confirm the generator keeps it so.
Standards: HSS.IC.B.6, "Evaluate reports based on data"; HSS.IC.A.1 (a random sample from that population); HSN.Q.A.1, "choose and interpret the scale and the origin in graphs and data displays."
Foundations first: three questions, in order, each with its picture. Who answered: the crowd with a scattered pick from a complete list (good), one corner (convenience), or hands raised (voluntary response); size never cures a lean, so 1,300 replies from people who chose to reply are still one kind of person; and when the sample truly is tiny (20 parents) the pile is wide. How sure: the interval around the estimate; a lead smaller than about two margins can be nothing; a headline with no margin pretends to certainty. Is the picture honest: a bar chart drawn from 0, then its axis slides up to 33% and the bars stretch and the gap yawns, then slides back. Two `term` cards: voluntary response, margin of error.
Read: the sentence about how people were counted, the sentence about the margin (present or absent), the sentence about the axis, each gold in turn; the headline last. At a glance: each check passes or fails on one phrase, so each decoy dies on one phrase.
Example (full, two flaws): the cafe posted the question on its website and counted 1,300 replies from customers who chose to answer; 65% to 35%; margin ±3 stated; axis starts at 33%. (1) Who answered: hands raised across the crowd; those who feel strongly reply; voluntary response; 1,300 does not help, they are one kind of customer. "Sample too small" dims. (2) How sure: a margin is stated, so "no margin of error" dims (the honest aside: a margin from volunteers is not worth much, but the report did state one).
