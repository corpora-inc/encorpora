# Two variables, sampling and inference: part 2

Ten problem types across `residuals-correlation`, `categorical-association`, `regression-interpretation` and `causal-claims`, all Algebra 1. Every concept stands on the family design's foundations 6 to 9 and uses the cloud and the table. Where a type holds several problems under one name, the lesson works one of each kind on its own screen, and the flag says so first.

**Shared openings.** Every film in the table strand (`relative-frequency-percent`, `conditional-frequency-choice`, `two-way-share`) opens the same way, about thirty seconds, drawn live: three people are tallied into an empty two-way table on the words of their answers; the row totals, column totals and grand total are written; then the reading rule: "'of the' names the total you divide by," with the gold band landing on a row, then a column, then the whole table as each kind of "of" is said. Every film in the cloud strand (`correlation-choice`, `residual-function-fit`, `regression-coefficient-meaning`, `regression-line-predict`, `nonlinear-data-model`, `regression-prediction-select`) opens with the cloud built from its own data: "each dot is one student, over to the hours, up to the score," the axes named with units, the line drawn in blue, and one sentence on what the line is for: "it sums up the lean, so we can predict." Nothing re-taught; the picture is the same one the learner has met since grade 8.

### `correlation-choice` — Estimate, rank and interpret r

**Flag first.** Three problems live under one name: estimate r from a cloud (four wordings), rank given r values by strength (one), and a true-statement choice about an r near 0 on curved data (two). The description also promises residual plots that no variant shows; `residual-function-fit` owns those. Split, or keep one lesson with three screens as below. No variant computes r, so HSS.ID.C.8's "compute (using technology)" goes unpracticed, acceptably.

**Standard.** HSS.ID.C.8: "interpret the correlation coefficient of a linear fit."

**Foundations first.** The cloud and its line. Then r, defined by what it does, not a formula: a number from −1 to 1 that measures how tightly the cloud hugs a straight line. Its sign is the direction of the lean. Its size is the tightness, not the steepness. Drawn live with **tighten**: a loose falling cloud with r reading −0.6, the dots slide toward the line while r counts to −0.95, and the line never moves. Beside it, two tight clouds, one steep and one shallow, both reading 0.95. Then the r line: a number line from −1 to 1 with 0 in the middle; strength is distance from 0 in either direction. Last, the thing r cannot see: a cloud that rises then falls (a ball's height over time). The **sweep**'s average height goes up, then down; the lean cancels; r reads near 0 even though the dots sit on a perfect curve. "r measures only straight-line lean."

**Read.** For a cloud: which way does it lean, and how tight? For a list: which r is farthest from 0? For a statement: two halves, a fact about the dots and a claim about r; both must hold. At a glance: a falling cloud kills every positive choice; a cloud with any visible lean kills 0.

**The example** (estimate r). "The scatter plot shows the price of a cup of lemonade and the cups sold on each of 12 days. Which value is closest to r? 0 | 0.6 | −0.95 | 0.95." Picture: the problem's twelve dots, price on x in dollars, cups on y, a tight falling cloud. (1) Sweep: the marker falls steadily across. Negative: 0.6 and 0.95 dim together ("the cloud falls"). (2) It leans, so 0 dims. (3) Tightness: the line is drawn and every dot sits close to it; a loose cloud at −0.6 is shown ghosted beside for comparison. (4) −0.95.

**Second screen** (rank). "Which shows the strongest linear association? −0.05 | −0.9 | 0.42 | 0.77." The four values drop onto the r line; brackets measure each distance from 0; −0.9 is farthest. Sign ignored. "−0.9 is stronger than 0.77: the minus is direction, not weakness."

**Third screen** (statement). "Speed and fuel economy on 8 test drives; r = 0.06. Which statement is true?" The dots rise then fall. Sweep: up, then down. "No relationship" dims (the picture shows a strong one); "strong positive linear" dims (it falls as much as it rises); "a line fits well" dims (a flat line misses every dot). The remaining statement is proved by the sweep: a clear curve; r near 0 because r sees only straight-line lean.

**Fast.** Sign from the lean; size from the hug; strongest means largest size either sign; near 0 means either no lean or a lean that cancels, so look at the picture.
**Sense.** Choices here are far apart (0, ±0.6, ±0.95): the lesson teaches the three looks, shapeless, loose lean, tight lean, never fine estimation.
**Check.** Cover the line with your hand: can you still see where it goes? Tight. Does the right half sit lower than the left? Negative.
**Rule out.** Wrong sign dims first with one reason; 0 dims when a lean is visible; in the list, every value nearer 0 than another dims.
**Line.** "r is tightness, not steepness. The sign is the direction."
**Trap.** Choosing 0.77 over −0.9 because negative sounds weak; calling a falling cloud "no association"; reading r = 0 as "no relationship." The r line and the curved cloud make each one visible.
**Kit.** `scatter` gains an r readout (`"r": true`) on `tighten`; a ghosted second cloud for comparison (`"ghost": {...}`); `numberline` from −1 to 1 with dropped marks and distance brackets.
**Writer.** Strongest: two of the three screens are judgment, and the curved-cloud case must be staged, not described.

### `relative-frequency-percent` — A percent from a two-way table

**Standard.** HSS.ID.B.5: "Interpret relative frequencies in the context of the data (including joint, marginal, and conditional relative frequencies)."

**Foundations first.** The shared opening. Then the three wholes, named on the one table: a total over everyone is a *marginal* share; a cell over everyone is a *joint* share (two things at once); a cell over its own row or column is a *conditional* share (of that group only). Each name is said once as the band lands where its whole is. The reading rule is the lesson: "of the" names the total you divide by; "of the 250 surveyed" or "of everyone" is the grand total.

**Read.** Find the phrase after "of." That is the whole. Find the count that fits the rest of the sentence. At a glance: the answer is between 0 and 100, and the two shares of any one whole add to 100.

**The example.** The table (the generator's own numbers are not given, so the lesson builds one; the film must draw the problem's table): rows Eleventh (bus 25, another way 95, total 120) and Twelfth (bus 65, another way 65, total 130); columns Bus 90, Another way 160; all 250. "What percent of the twelfth graders ride the bus?" (1) "Of the twelfth graders" goes gold; the band lands on the Twelfth row; its total, 130, is the whole. (2) "Ride the bus" picks the cell: 65. (3) 65 ÷ 130 = 0.5 → 50%, written beside the band. (4) The mosaic slab for Twelfth is half colored. 50.

**Quick second case** (marginal, a different whole). "What percent of the 250 students surveyed get to school another way?" The whole table lights; 160 ÷ 250 = 64%. **Quick third case** (joint). "What percent of the 250 are twelfth graders who ride the bus?" Whole table; one cell, 65 ÷ 250 = 26%. Three questions, one table, three wholes: that is the lesson.

**Fast.** Band on the "of"; cell over band total; times 100.
**Sense.** 65 is exactly half of 130, so 50; 160 is well over half of 250, so more than 50.
**Check.** The other share of the same whole: bus 90 ÷ 250 = 36%, and 36 + 64 = 100.
**Line.** "'Of the' names the total you divide by."
**Trap.** Dividing by the grand total when the question says "of the twelfth graders" (65 ÷ 250 = 26%). The band shrinks the table to one row before any division is written.
**Kit.** `table` `percent` action (writes `65/130 = 50\%` beside the band and labels the slab); nothing else.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, one table, the picture and three cases are fixed here, and `--dry` checks every percent.

### `conditional-frequency-choice` — Conditional percents and association, multiple choice

**Flag first.** The app's method says "percents 20 or more points apart count as associated." That is a convention, not mathematics; the lesson says "a real step" against "about the same" and leans on the fact that the generated tables are never borderline (50 against 50; 80 against 20). Confirm no variant lands between. The type holds three kinds: a conditional percent in either direction, an association yes/no, and "what does this calculation describe."

**Standard.** HSS.ID.B.5, both clauses: "conditional relative frequencies" and "Recognize possible associations and trends in the data."

**Foundations first.** The shared opening and the reading rule. Then the direction of "of the": the band on a row for "of the adults under 30," on a column for "of the adults who prefer streaming." Then association in a table, built with the **mosaic**: slabs for the two age groups, proportional to their sizes, each colored by its theater share; the same share in both makes one straight edge; a step means association. "Compare percents, never counts."

**Read.** For a percent: the group after "of" is the whole, row or column. For association: the same conditional share in each group; is there a step? For "what does this describe": find both numbers in the table; the divisor is a total and names the whole; the dividend is the part.

**The example.** One table for all three screens: Under 30: theater 48, streaming 72, total 120; 30 and over: theater 12, streaming 18, total 30; totals theater 60, streaming 90, all 150. "Is there an association between the age group and whether they prefer a theater?" Cast: the student's first read, "48 under 30 go to the theater and only 12 over 30, so yes." Teacher: "More, yes; but there are 120 of them and only 30 of the others. Compare the shares." (1) Band on Under 30: 48 ÷ 120 = 40%. (2) Band on 30 and over: 12 ÷ 30 = 40%. (3) The mosaic: two slabs, one straight edge. (4) Rule out: "Yes: of those who prefer a theater, 80% are under 30, 20% are 30 and over" dims (that is the age mix of theater-goers, which only says there are more young adults in the survey); "Yes: 32% of everyone are under 30 who prefer a theater, 8% are 30 and over who do" dims (joint shares; same reason); "Yes: 40% but only 40%" dims (it contradicts itself). (5) No: 40% and 40%, about the same.

**Quick second screen** (direction). "Of the adults who prefer streaming, what percent are 30 and over?" Band on the Streaming column; 18 ÷ 90 = 20%. The row read, 18 ÷ 30 = 60%, is the decoy and is named.

**Quick third screen** (describe). "One calculation is 72 ÷ 120 = 60%. What does it describe?" 120 is the Under 30 row total, so the whole is the under 30; 72 is their streaming cell: 60% of adults under 30 prefer streaming. Decoys with "of everyone" (120 ÷ 150 is 80%) and "of those who prefer streaming" (72 ÷ 90 is 80%) dim together: the divisor was 120.

**Fast.** Band on the "of"; cell over band total. For association, the same share in both groups, then look for a step.
**Sense.** A share for a smaller group can be larger; a conditional percent and its complement in the same group add to 100 (40 + 60).
**Check.** Re-read the chosen sentence and test each half against the table; for association, compute the other share (streaming 60% and 60%) and get the same verdict.
**Rule out.** Any "yes" built on counts or on joint shares dims with one reason: "different-sized groups." Any sentence whose whole is not the divisor dims.
**Line.** "Compare percents, not counts. 'Of the' names the total you divide by."
**Trap.** "More under 30 go to the theater, so yes": the cast's first read, untangled by the mosaic's straight edge. And the band on the wrong line for a column "of": the band motion makes the choice of total visible before any number is written.
**Kit.** `table` `percent`; `mosaic` sliced by rows and, for the column question, by columns (exists).
**Writer.** Strongest: a judgment type with a cast, a direction trap and decoys that are each a specific misconception.

### `two-way-share` — The right total, rounded to the nearest percent

**Flag first.** This is `relative-frequency-percent` with four choices, rounding and larger counts; two types for one idea in one skill. Fine to keep as the multiple-choice version; the lesson is shorter because the foundations are the same opening.

**Standard.** HSS.ID.B.5: "Interpret relative frequencies ... (including joint, marginal, and conditional relative frequencies)."

**Foundations first.** The shared opening; the direction of "of the"; and rounding a percent: divide, multiply by 100, look at the tenths digit (family 2's rule, named in one line).

**Read.** The group after "of" is the whole; the rest of the sentence is the cell. At a glance: is the cell more or less than half its whole? That alone dims two choices.

**The example.** "The table shows the counts for 162 customers. What percent of the teens bought in store? Round to the nearest percent." The lesson's table (the generator's is not given; the film draws the problem's): Teens: in store 31, online 41, total 72; Adults: in store 51, online 39, total 90; totals 82, 80, 162. Choices 43% | 19% | 38% | 57% (the lesson's decoys are the real misreads: 31 ÷ 162, 31 ÷ 82, 41 ÷ 72). (1) "Of the teens" gold; band on the Teens row; whole 72. (2) "Bought in store": 31. (3) Half of 72 is 36; 31 is under half, so the answer is under 50%: 57% dims. 31 is far more than a fifth of 72: 19% dims. (4) 31 ÷ 72 = 0.4305...; tenths digit after the point is 0, so 43%. (5) 43%.

**Quick second case** (column direction). "The table shows the counts for 157 commuters. Of the commuters who take a train, what percent are city commuters?" City: train 25, drive 50, total 75; Suburb: 36, 46, 82; totals 61, 96, 157. Band on the Train column; 25 ÷ 61 = 0.4098 → 41%. The row read, 25 ÷ 75 = 33%, is named as the decoy.

**Fast.** Band, cell, divide; before dividing, decide which side of 50 the answer is on.
**Sense.** 31 of 72 is a bit under half; 43% is a bit under 50.
**Check.** The row's other cell: 41 ÷ 72 = 57%; 43 + 57 = 100.
**Rule out.** The wrong side of half dims at once; the choice equal to cell ÷ grand total dims as "the whole was the teens, not everyone."
**Line.** "Decide which side of half before you divide."
**Trap.** The grand total (31 ÷ 162 = 19%) and the wrong direction (31 ÷ 82 = 38%). Both appear as choices and are dimmed by name.
**Kit.** Nothing beyond `table` `percent`.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, arithmetic `--dry` can check, decoys fixed by the concept.

### `residual-function-fit` — Residuals from a model, and reading a residual plot

**Flag first.** Misfiled: under `regression-interpretation` (HSS.ID.C.7) but it serves HSS.ID.B.6b. Three problems under one name: (a) a residual from a curved model, as a number or as an "over/underestimates by" sentence; (b) a residual-plot conclusion, where both shown variants answer "keep the linear model" and the curved cases the description promises never appear (confirm the generator produces them); (c) classifying a table of exact values by first differences, second differences and ratios, whose only variant answers "none of these." Case (c) is HSF.LE.A.1 content and belongs in family 16 or 13; recommend moving it. The lesson below works (a) and (b) fully and (c) briefly so every variant is covered.

**Standard.** HSS.ID.B.6b: "Informally assess the fit of a function by plotting and analyzing residuals"; B.6a for predictions from a given function.

**Foundations first.** The cloud with a curved model drawn through it. Predicted: the model's height at x. Observed: the dot. Residual: the miss, observed minus predicted, drawn as the **stick** from dot to curve; above the curve positive, below negative. "Overestimates" means the model is above the dot, so the residual is negative. Then **hang**: every stick slides into the strip beneath and hangs from a level zero line. A shapeless band means the model fits; a bend (positive, negative, positive) means the data curve more than the model; the sizes of the sticks say nothing about the shape.

**Read.** For a residual: which model, which x, which observed value; the answer is a miss, small compared with the values, with a sign. For a plot: left to right, is there a pattern? At a glance: a residual problem's two big numbers (721, 729) are never the answer.

**The example** (residual from a model). "A model predicts ŷ = 3·3^x. At x = 5 the observed value is 721. What is the residual? 721 | −8 | 676 | 729." (1) Predict: 3^5 = 243; 3 × 243 = 729; the walk up to the curve reads 729. (2) The dot at (5, 721) sits just below the curve; the stick is short and points down. (3) 721 − 729 = −8. (4) In words: the model overestimates by 8. Choices 721 and 729 dim together ("those are the values, not the miss"); 676 dims ("a miss this size would be a stick as long as the curve is tall"). −8.

**Second screen** (the plot). "A line was fit to daily sales against advertising spending. Which conclusion does the residual plot support?" The cloud and line are drawn; the sticks hang into the strip; the strip is the problem's own residual plot. No pattern: keep the linear model. Rule out: "steeper slope, because some residuals are far from 0" dims (size is not pattern; a steeper line would push every right-hand stick down); "a different line, because some are positive and some negative" dims (a good line always has both; the misses add to zero). Then one quick beat on the case the learner will meet: a cloud that bends up, the same line, sticks positive, negative, positive: switch to a model that bends upward.

**Third screen** (the table, brief). x 0, 1, 2, 3 with y 1, 2, 4, 9: first differences 1, 2, 5 (not equal, so not linear); second differences 1, 3 (not equal, so not quadratic); ratios 2, 2, 2.25 (not equal, so not exponential): none of these. Three rows written under the table, each test said as one line.

**Fast.** Predict, then observed minus predicted, sign read off the picture. For a plot: look for a bend; ignore sizes.
**Sense.** A residual is small beside the values; an "overestimates" sentence must carry a negative residual.
**Check.** Observed = predicted + residual: 729 + (−8) = 721.
**Rule out.** Values themselves dim; the wrong direction word dims against the picture; "steeper" and "different line" dim with their reasons.
**Line.** "The residual is the miss: observed minus predicted. A good model leaves no pattern in its misses."
**Trap.** Predicted minus observed, which flips the sign; and reading large residuals as a bad fit. The stick is drawn from the dot, so its direction is seen before it is computed.
**Kit.** `scatter` `model` by equation (curves), `stick` to a curve, `residuals` strip (in the family's list); a table with difference rows for the third screen can be board lines.
**Writer.** Strongest: the plot screens are read as patterns and the decoys are beliefs about residuals; the numeric screen alone could go cheaper.

### `regression-coefficient-meaning` — What the slope and the intercept mean

**Standard.** HSS.ID.C.7: "Interpret the slope (rate of change) and the intercept (constant term) of a linear model in the context of the data." HSN.Q.A.1 for "choose and interpret units," HSF.LE.B.5 for "interpret the parameters."

**Foundations first.** The cloud and its line. Slope as rise over run on the **triangle** between two points of the line, with units on both legs: run +1 hour, rise +4.5 points; the slope is 4.5 points per hour. Its sentence always starts with x: "each additional hour of practice goes with a predicted score 4.5 points higher." "Goes with," never "causes." The intercept is the **walk** to x = 0, meaningful only when x = 0 is a place the data could be and the predicted value is possible. "Trust the line where the dots are."

**Read.** Which is asked, slope or intercept, and in what form (sentence, units, "does it have a meaning")? At a glance: a slope sentence begins with x's unit and ends with y's; a positive slope says "higher"; the intercept question is settled by where the data's bracket sits relative to x = 0.

**The example** (slope). "Hours of practice and test score of 9 students, ŷ = 4.5x + 40. What does the slope mean?" (1) The triangle: run one hour, rise 4.5 points, labeled. (2) "4.5 points lower" dims: the line rises. (3) "Each additional point goes with 4.5 more hours" dims: the slope's units are points per hour, read off the legs, not hours per point. (4) "0 hours has a predicted score of 4.5" dims: at x = 0 the walk reads 40; that is the intercept, and it is 40, not 4.5. (5) The sentence is read against the triangle: each additional hour of practice goes with a predicted score 4.5 points higher. **Quick beat** (units variant): rise over run is points over hours: points per hour.

**Second example** (the intercept; a different judgment). "High temperature and cups of lemonade on 12 days, ŷ = 3x − 149. Does the y-intercept have a meaning?" The data sit between 75 and 95 degrees; the bracket under the axis shows it. (1) The walk to x = 0 goes muted past the bracket, carrying "beyond the data," and the view pulls back until the line is seen diving to −149 cups. (2) "Yes: −149 cups on a 0-degree day" dims: negative cups. (3) "Each degree goes with 149 fewer cups" dims: 149 is not even the slope. (4) "An intercept never has a meaning" dims against the first example: 40 at 0 hours of practice was a real prediction, because 0 hours is inside the data. (5) No: 0 degrees is far outside the data, and −149 cups is impossible.

**Fast.** Slope: read the legs with their units; write the sentence x first. Intercept: is 0 inside the data's bracket, and is the value possible? Both yes: it means something. Either no: it does not.
**Sense.** A positive slope's sentence says "higher"; an intercept's meaning depends on the data, so the blanket "never" is a tell.
**Check.** Step the line one unit of x and watch y change by the slope; walk to x = 0 and read the intercept.
**Rule out.** Wrong sign word dims; swapped units dim; intercept stated as slope dims; "never" dims against the counterexample.
**Line.** "One more of x goes with about this much y. Trust the line where the dots are."
**Trap.** Swapping x and y in the sentence; taking the intercept literally at −149 cups; the overcorrection "intercepts never mean anything." Each is a choice, each is dimmed on the picture.
**Kit.** `triangle` with unit labels; `walk` with `"view": true` and the "beyond the data" tag; `s.range` bracket (in the family's list).
**Writer.** Strongest: every choice is a specific misconception and the intercept judgment turns on the picture.

### `regression-line-predict` — Predict with a least-squares line

**Standard.** HSS.ID.B.6a: "use functions fitted to data to solve problems in the context of the data." HSA.REI.B.3 ("solve linear equations ... in one variable") holds: the "for how many hours does the line predict a score of 45" variant exists, so the link stands.

**Foundations first.** The cloud and its line; predicted (the line's height, ŷ) against observed (a dot); "use the line" means read the line, never the nearest dot. The **walk**: up from x to the line, across to y. The same walk backward: across from a given ŷ to the line, down to x.

**Read.** Three asks under one name: a prediction at x (substitute), the x for a given prediction (solve), the gap between two predictions (slope times the gap in x). At a glance: a prediction inside the data lands among the dots' heights; the x for a given ŷ lands under the dots near that height.

**The example** (predict). "Hours of practice and test score of 10 students, ŷ = 4x + 46. Predict the score of a student who practices 6 hours." (1) The walk rises from 6 to the line, across to y. (2) On the board: ŷ = 4(6) + 46 = 24 + 46 = 70. (3) The readout on the walk says 70, among the dots near 6 hours. 70.

**Quick second case** (solve). "ŷ = 3x + 39; for how many hours does the line predict a score of 45?" The walk runs backward from 45. 3x + 39 = 45; subtract 39: 3x = 6; divide by 3: x = 2. The margin notes are the balance. **Quick third case** (difference). "ŷ = 2.5x + 40; two students practice 1 hour and 7 hours. By how many points do their predicted scores differ?" The triangle from x = 1 to x = 7: run 6 hours, rise 2.5 × 6 = 15 points. Shown once the other way: 57.5 − 42.5 = 15; the intercept cancels.

**Fast.** Substitute; or solve; or slope times the x gap. All three are the one line read three ways.
**Sense.** 70 is a bit above the middle of the scores; 2 hours is a small amount for a score near the intercept; 15 is 2.5 taken six times.
**Check.** Put the pair back on the line: 4(6) + 46 = 70; 3(2) + 39 = 45; 2.5(7) + 40 − (2.5(1) + 40) = 15.
**Line.** "Read the line, not the nearest dot."
**Trap.** Predicting from the nearest dot; for the gap, subtracting only the hours (6) or adding the intercept in. The walk and the triangle make the line the only thing read.
**Kit.** `walk` in both directions (a `"y": 45` form that goes across then down); `triangle` with labeled legs.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one line, three reads, every number checkable.

### `nonlinear-data-model` — The model with the smallest sum of squared residuals

**Flag first.** One variant; four models times four points is heavy arithmetic for a standard whose intent is "choose a function suggested by the context." Teach shape first, compute only the finalists; the lesson should not be sixteen residuals.

**Standard.** HSS.ID.B.6a: "Use given functions or choose a function suggested by the context. Emphasize linear, quadratic, and exponential models"; B.6b: "assess the fit of a function by ... analyzing residuals."

**Foundations first.** The four points plotted. Residual as the stick; *squared* residual as a literal square drawn on the stick (its side is the miss), so "least squares" is the model whose squares cover the least area. A far-off model has a huge square; a model that fits has small ones. Then the shapes: a line climbs steadily, a parabola climbs faster and faster, an exponential explodes, a constant is flat.

**Read.** "Smallest sum of squared residuals": the model that stays closest to all the points together. At a glance: any model that leaves the picture, or sits far above every point, is out before any arithmetic.

**The example.** "(0, 4), (1, 4), (2, 8), (3, 8). Which model gives the smallest sum of squared residuals? 2x + 3 | 2(3)^x | 21 | 2x² + 3." (1) The points, between 4 and 8, rising slowly. (2) All four models drawn: y = 21 is a flat line far above every point; 2·3^x reaches 54 at x = 3, off the card. Both dim together: "nowhere near the points." (3) Finalists. 2x + 3 predicts 3, 5, 7, 9; residuals 1, −1, 1, −1; squares 1, 1, 1, 1; sum 4, drawn as four small squares. (4) 2x² + 3 predicts 3, 5, 11, 21; residuals 1, −1, −3, −13; squares 1, 1, 9, 169; sum 180, the last square swallowing the card. (5) Every point used, four squares each. y = 2x + 3.

**Fast.** Sketch the points and see the shape; dim the models whose shape is wrong; square the misses only for what remains.
**Sense.** The data rise about 2 per step on average; the line with slope 2 should win; the parabola is already 13 off at x = 3.
**Check.** Count the squares: four per finalist; the sums 4 and 180 are far apart, so no rounding doubt.
**Rule out.** A constant above all the points and an exponential off the card dim in one beat.
**Line.** "See the shape first; square the misses only for the finalists."
**Trap.** Adding residuals without squaring (2x + 3's add to 0, which looks perfect for the wrong reason); or skipping a point. Squares drawn on the sticks make both visible.
**Kit.** `scatter` `model` by equation for several models at once; `stick` to a curve; `squares` on sticks (a square whose side is the residual, to scale).
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model from this concept once `squares` exists; the shape reasoning is written here.

### `regression-prediction-select` — Select the true statements about a fitted line

**Flag first.** Two of the three variants pair a context with the wrong sign (more practice, fewer free throws; more study, a lower score). A lesson cannot say those with a straight face; the generator must match sign to story. The example below uses the sensible one. Also note "the data have x between 0 and 10," so here x = 0 is inside the data and the intercept statement is true; the contrast with `regression-coefficient-meaning`'s lemonade line is worth one sentence.

**Standard.** HSS.ID.C.7 ("interpret the slope ... and the intercept ... in the context of the data") and B.6a ("use functions fitted to data to solve problems").

**Foundations first.** The line drawn over the data's stretch 0 to 10. The three kinds of statement, each pointing at a part of the picture: a prediction at an x (a walk), a change per unit (the slope triangle), a value at 0 (the intercept). Then the habit for select-many: sort every statement into its kind before computing anything, and count how many must be chosen.

**Read.** Five statements, three kinds. At a glance: a change statement quoting the intercept (44.0) or a zero statement quoting the slope (−2.2) is wrong on sight; a "greater for 9 than 4" statement is settled by the slope's sign alone.

**The example.** "ŷ = 2.1x + 34.8, minutes of exercise and calories burned, x between 0 and 10. Select the 3 true statements." (1) Sort: (a) greater for 9 than 4: a slope-sign statement; (b), (c), (d): predictions; (e) at 0: the intercept. (2) The slope is positive, so the line rises: (a) true; the triangle shows it. (3) Walks: x = 2: 4.2 + 34.8 = 39.0, not 41.1: (c) false; x = 4: 8.4 + 34.8 = 43.2: (d) true; x = 8: 16.8 + 34.8 = 51.6, not 18.0: (b) false. (4) x = 0: 34.8, and 0 minutes is inside the data: (e) true. (5) Three chosen: a, d, e. Count matches.

**Fast.** Sort into kinds; sign statements by the slope's sign; zero statements by the intercept; predictions by substitution; stop when the count is met, but check the rest when time allows.
**Sense.** Predictions at larger x are larger for a positive slope; every prediction for x in 0 to 10 lies between 34.8 and 55.8.
**Check.** Exactly three chosen; each one's number sits on the line in the picture.
**Rule out.** A change quoting the intercept, a zero quoting the slope, and a prediction that forgot the intercept (−2.2 × 8 = −17.6 in the first variant) dim together as "the wrong part of the line."
**Line.** "Three kinds of statement, three parts of the line: the walk, the triangle, the start."
**Trap.** Dropping the intercept when predicting (−17.6 free throws); reading the intercept as the change per hour. The walk and the triangle keep them apart.
**Kit.** `walk` at several x on one line; nothing new.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: a sorting habit and arithmetic `--dry` checks.

### `causal-claim-choice` — What an association can and cannot show

**Standard.** HSS.ID.C.9: "Distinguish between correlation and causation."

**Foundations first.** Association restated in one line from the cloud: more of this tends to go with more of that. Then the question that decides everything: who put each day, city or person in its group? Here no one did; the data were recorded as they came (an *observational study*), so the data can show association only. Three reasons an association is not a cause, each drawn on the **study diagram**: a *lurking variable*, a third thing that pushes both (a box above the two measured things with an arrow to each); *reverse cause*, the arrow pointing the other way; and the overcorrection that is also wrong: the association is real, it is the reason that is unknown.

**Read.** Three asks: name a lurking variable; pick the conclusion the data support; pick the explanation that reverses cause and effect. At a glance: a lurking variable must be a third thing, not one of the two measured; the supported conclusion is the one that says "associated, but cannot show a cause"; a reversal names the measured y as the pusher of the measured x.

**The example** (lurking variable). "On 30 summer days, cones sold and sunburns treated; more cones went with more sunburns. Which is a lurking variable?" (1) Cast: the student, "ice cream causes sunburn? That can't be right, but the data say so." Teacher: "The data say they rise together. Ask what else was different about those days." (2) The diagram: Cones and Sunburns side by side; "How hot and sunny the day was" appears above with an arrow to each. (3) Rule out: "the number of people treated for sunburn" dims (it is one of the two measured things, not a third); "the color of the cones" and "the flavors on sale" dim together (nothing about a cone's color pushes sunburns). (4) How hot and sunny each day was.

**Second screen** (the conclusion). "40 house fires: more firefighters went with more damage. Which conclusion do the data support?" "Sending fewer would reduce the damage" dims (advice built on a cause the data cannot show); "the data show more firefighters cause more damage" dims (no one assigned the firefighters); "the number and the damage are not related" dims (the association is real; the diagram shows the fire's size pushing both). Associated, but these data cannot show a cause.

**Third screen** (reversal). "25 cities: more police went with more reported crimes. Which explanation reverses cause and effect?" "Police cause crime" is the forward reading; "bigger cities have both" is a lurking variable, true but not what was asked; "it happened by chance" dismisses a real pattern. "Cities with more crime hire more police": the arrow turned around.

**Fast.** Lurking: the one choice that could push both. Conclusion: the one with "associated" and "cannot show a cause." Reversal: y pushing x.
**Sense.** No study that only watched can support "causes," "would reduce," or "requiring everyone to." A choice that denies the association is as wrong as one that claims a cause.
**Check.** Say the chosen sentence and ask: does it name a third thing (lurking), or turn the arrow (reversal), or claim exactly what observed data can claim (association)?
**Rule out.** One of the two measured variables offered as "lurking" dims; anything with "causes," "would," "requiring" dims; "not related" dims as the overcorrection.
**Line.** "Goes with is not causes. Who put each one in its group?"
**Trap.** The overcorrection: "association is not causation, so they are not related." Say it twice: the association is real; what the data cannot say is why. And confusing a lurking variable with a reversal when both are offered: the diagram's arrows differ.
**Kit.** The study diagram: three labels and arrows (`label`, `vector`; exists), with a `lurk` layout that places the third box above the pair.
**Writer.** Strongest: pure judgment, a cast that carries the realistic first thought, and decoys that are each a belief students hold.
