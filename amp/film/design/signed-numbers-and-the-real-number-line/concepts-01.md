# Signed numbers and the real number line, part 1: concepts for ten problem types

Written from the family design. Every lesson in grade 6 opens the same way, drawn live in about twenty seconds, exactly as every decimal-fractions film opens with the flat cut into tenths: a plain number line with its labeled ticks, then the line extended left of zero, the zero tick drawn tall with the dashed mirror through it, one negative number dropped with "the sign says which side; the digits say how far." After that opening, each lesson defines only the words it needs. Grade 7 opens by naming that line in one sentence and adding the first walk.

Two flags before the concepts, each a small app fix that no lesson should inherit: `check-register-balance` is misfiled (nothing in it is signed), and `staar-number-sets` and `number-set-venn` are two drawings of one picture and must become the same drawing.

---

## Grade 6: signed-numbers (6.NS.C.5, 6.NS.C.6)

### `opposite-on-line` — Read a point and find its opposite

**Standard.** 6.NS.C.6a, exactly: "Recognize opposite signs of numbers as indicating locations on opposite sides of 0 on the number line; recognize that the opposite of the opposite of a number is the number itself, e.g., –(–3) = 3, and that 0 is its own opposite." Reading the point is 6.NS.C.6c, "find and position integers … on a horizontal or vertical number line diagram." Foundations 1 and 2; the lesson where the family's picture is born, so it carries the family's definitions.

**Foundations first.** (1) The line the learner has drawn since grade 1, labels and the step on an arc, then: it keeps going left of zero. Zero is a place, not nothing. (2) *Negative number*: a number on the left side of zero, written with its sign. Three is three jumps right of zero; negative three is three jumps left. "The sign says which side; the digits say how far." (3) Zero drawn as the mirror: the tall tick and the dashed line. (4) *Opposite*: the number the same distance from zero on the other side. Shown once on 2 and −2 with two equal blue brackets before any problem.

**Read.** "What is the opposite of the number at P?" Two jobs: read P, then cross the mirror. At a glance: P is on the positive side, so the answer is negative, and it has the same digits as P.

**The example.** A line from −10 to 10, a tick every unit, labels at −10, −5, 0, 5, 10, P three ticks right of zero. (1) Find the step: 0 to 5 is five jumps for 5, so one jump is 1; the arc says +1. (2) Count jumps from zero to P: one, two, three; P is 3, teal. (3) The opposite: the dot lifts, swings across the dashed zero line and lands three jumps left; two equal blue brackets, 3 and 3, from zero. The landing dot is rust, labeled −3. (4) Answer −3. (5) One beat on the words: "the opposite of three is negative three"; the sign changed, the digits did not.

**Quick second case** (a different question, two beats): "the opposite of the opposite." P at 4: swing to −4, swing again: it lands back on itself. The opposite of the opposite is the number: 4. One more beat: a dot at 0 swings and lands where it was. Zero is its own opposite. The wording "point R, the same distance from 0 on the other side" is the definition said in words; the lesson says so once.

**Fast.** Read P from zero; change the sign, keep the digits. Twice: back to the start.
**Sense.** The answer sits on the other side of zero, as far from zero as P.
**Check.** Count the jumps from zero to each dot: equal.
**Line.** "Same distance, other side."
**Trap.** Counting ticks instead of jumps when the labels are every 5 (every tick read as 5). The step is computed on screen and written on the arc every time. For the double opposite, children stop after one flip and answer −4; the dot visibly swings twice.
**Kit.** `numberline` with `mirror`, `measure`, `drop`, arcs. `drop` of a point from a scene figure if `facts.ts` prints the app's line.
**Writer.** Strongest. The pilot's first lesson; the swing and the brackets must be staged, not described, and every later lesson copies them.

### `signed-quantity` — Write a signed number for a story amount

**Standard.** 6.NS.C.5, exactly: "use positive and negative numbers to represent quantities in real-world contexts, explaining the meaning of 0 in each situation." The problems never ask the explaining clause; the lesson says what zero means aloud in every example, because the standard does.

**Foundations first.** The line extended left of zero (the shared opening). Then the one new idea: every story chooses what zero means and which way is positive. Three quick lines drawn: horizontal, zero at the start of the play, "gained" arrow right; vertical, zero at sea level, up positive; vertical, zero at no change, "money added" up. The number is where the story lands.

**Read.** The problem says what is positive; the question says which way the amount goes. At a glance: if the amount goes the positive way, the number is the amount written plainly; if it goes the other way, it is the amount with a minus sign. The digits are already in the problem; only the sign is being asked.

**The example.** "On one play, a football team loses 20 yards. Yards gained are positive. What number represents the team's change in position?" (1) What is zero: no change, the spot where the play began. The tall tick. (2) Which way is positive: gained, teal arrow to the right. (3) Loses goes the other way: a rust arc of 20 to the left of zero. (4) The dot lands at −20. (5) Read it back: "negative twenty: twenty yards, the other way from gaining." Horizontal because yards run along the ground.

**Quick second case** (the other shape of the question, three beats): "A temperature 23 degrees below zero is written as −23. What number represents 23 degrees above zero?" A vertical thermometer line; −23 is already given; the question asks for its mirror. The dot swings up across zero: 23, written plainly, no sign. A withdrawal of 111 dollars is the first shape again (money added is positive; a withdrawal goes the other way: −111); covered by the method.

**Fast.** Two questions: which way is positive? does this amount go that way? Yes: write it. No: write it with a minus sign.
**Sense.** A loss, a drop, a withdrawal, a depth: negative. A gain, a climb, a deposit, a height: positive.
**Check.** Say the number in the story's words and ask if it is true: "negative twenty: twenty yards lost. Yes."
**Line.** "The sign says which side; the digits say how far."
**Trap.** Copying a sign that appears in the problem: writing −23 for "above zero" because "−23" is on the page, or 20 for a loss because "20 yards" is positive-looking. The mirror pair (the given negative and the asked positive on one thermometer) makes the two sides two different numbers.
**Kit.** `numberline` with `vertical`, `drop`, `mirror`; a short teal arrow labeled with the positive direction's word.
**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept. One method; the kit's `vertical` and `mirror` are named actions.

### `staar-number-sets` — Whole numbers, integers, rational numbers in nested sets

**Standard.** Filed under 6.NS.C.6; its honest home is the Texas standard on classifying numbers with a Venn diagram, which Common Core has no clause for. It practices 6.NS.C.6 ("understand a rational number as a point on the number line") only through the picture that builds the rings from the line, and 7.NS.A.2d's "a fraction bar means divide." Say so in the alignment list. Flag: the problem text describes the diagram in words; if no figure is drawn, the lesson's figure is the diagram, and it must be the same drawing as `number-set-venn` uses.

**Foundations first.** Build the rings from the line, each ring growing as the line gains points. *Whole numbers*: 0, 1, 2, 3, … (the counting dots and zero), innermost. *Integers*: the whole numbers and their opposites (the mirror adds −1, −2, −3, …), the ring around them. *Rational numbers*: any number that can be written as a fraction of two integers, so every fraction, and every decimal that ends or repeats (the line fills in between), the outer ring. Nested means every number in an inner ring is also in the outer ones: 8 is a whole number, an integer and a rational number. The *region* for "integers that are not whole numbers" is the band between the first two rings: the negative integers. One more word: a fraction bar means divide, so a number written as a fraction is a number waiting to be worked out.

**Read.** Three wordings: "which belongs in the region for …", "which does NOT belong in …" (gold NOT), and "a student put X in …; which statement fixes it" (where does X really go). At a glance: a choice written as a fraction or a division is not placed yet; anything with a decimal or fraction part cannot be in the integer or whole rings; anything negative cannot be in the whole ring.

**The example.** "Which number belongs in the region for integers that are not whole numbers?" Choices −20/5, 40/5, 8, 0.6. (1) The region goes gold: the band of negative integers. (2) Each choice is a card; each simplifies before it drops. −20/5: 20 ÷ 5 = 4, and the sign puts it on the left of zero: −4, no fraction part, left of zero. It drops into the gold band. (3) 40/5 = 8 and 8: whole numbers, innermost ring. (4) 0.6: six tenths, a part left over, not a whole count of anything: the outer band. (5) The only card in the gold band: −20/5.

**Quick second case** (statement choices, three beats): "A student put 3/4 in the region for integers that are not whole numbers." 3/4 = 0.75, a part left over: it belongs in the outer band. "Move it to the region for rational numbers that are not integers." The other statements are checked against the picture in one beat each.

**Fast.** Simplify. Then two questions: a part left over? outer band. No part: left of zero? integer band. Else: whole numbers.
**Sense.** A number lands in the innermost ring that fits; the rings further out also contain it.
**Check.** Say the sentence: "negative four is an integer; it is not a whole number because it is left of zero."
**Rule out.** For "integers, not whole": anything positive dims, anything with a fraction part dims. For "whole numbers": any negative, any fraction part. For NOT: the three that fit dim together, the one left is the answer.
**Line.** "Simplify first, then place."
**Trap.** Placing −20/5 among the rationals that are not integers because it looks like a fraction, or 36 ÷ 6 outside the whole numbers because it looks like a division. The card simplifies on screen before anything drops.
**Kit.** `rings` with three sets (`whole`, `integer`, `rational`), `card`, `simplify`, `drop`, `highlight` on a region; `dim`.
**Writer.** Cheaper model once `rings` exists; one method with named actions.

### `number-set-venn` — Natural, whole, integer, rational: regions and "every … is a …"

**Standard.** Same as above: no Common Core clause; the alignment list should say so. "Natural numbers" is not a grade-6 Common Core term; the lesson defines it in one line because the state test uses it.

**Foundations first.** The rings grown from the line as in `staar-number-sets`, with one more ring inside: *natural numbers*, the counting numbers 1, 2, 3, … The whole numbers add zero, so the ring B between natural and whole holds exactly one number, 0; the lesson says so, because that is the whole content of two of the statement choices. Regions A to D are the bands between circles, named exactly as the app's figure names them: A natural, B zero, C negative integers, D fractions and decimals with a part left over. "The smallest set that contains 0.5" asks the same question with the set's name instead of its letter.

**Read.** Two kinds. Regions: "which region holds 20/5": simplify, then drop. Statements: "every X is a Y" is true only when X's ring sits inside Y's; "some X are Y" needs one example that is in both; "zero is a natural number" is read off ring B. At a glance for regions: a fraction with a part left over is always D; a negative with no part is always C; B is zero alone.

**The example (regions).** "Which region holds 20/5?" (1) Simplify: 20 ÷ 5 = 4. (2) 4 is a counting number: it drops into A. Two quick drops on the same picture: −7 (no part, left of zero): C; −3/10 (a part left over; renaming to −0.3 is not needed, a fraction of integers is already rational): D. One beat: "the smallest set containing 0.5" is the set whose band 0.5 lands in: rational numbers.

**The second example (statements), fully worked because the reading is different.** "Which statement is true?" (1) "Some negative integers are whole numbers": the negative integers are band C, outside the whole-number circle; none is inside. False, dims. (2) "Zero is a natural number": zero sits in B, outside the natural circle. False. (3) "Every whole number is a natural number": the whole circle is outside the natural circle; zero is a whole number that is not natural. False. (4) "Every integer is a rational number": the integer circle sits inside the rational circle. True.

**Fast.** Regions: simplify, then part? negative? zero? counting. Statements: "every X is a Y" is true when X's circle is inside Y's.
**Sense.** Inward nests outward: an inner number is in every outer set; an outer number is in no inner set.
**Check.** Say the number's sentence; for a statement, name one number that breaks it (zero breaks "every whole number is natural").
**Rule out.** Regions: anything with a part left over is not A, B or C; anything negative is not A or B. Statements: a claim that an outer band is inside an inner circle dims at once.
**Line.** "Every inner ring is inside every outer one."
**Trap.** Zero: children put it in A, or decide "zero is a natural number" from the word "natural." The lesson shows ring B holding zero alone, and says it.
**Kit.** `rings` with four sets, `letters`, `examples`; `card`, `simplify`, `drop`; the app's scene if `facts.ts` prints one. Make `staar-number-sets` and this one draw the identical figure.
**Writer.** Cheaper model from this concept.

### `check-register-balance` — Running balance and a missing amount

**Misfiled.** Nothing in it is signed: balances never cross zero and amounts carry no sign. It practices 6.NS.B.3 ("fluently add, subtract … multi-digit decimals using the standard algorithm") and belongs under money (family 21) or decimal algorithms (family 2). Either move it, or let one register overdraw so the balance goes negative and it earns 6.NS.C.5. Designed below as it is, so the lesson can ship either way.

**Foundations first.** The register, built live: a `table` with Entry | Amount | Balance, the first balance written at the top. Define each entry by what it does to the money: a *deposit* puts money in (the amount colored teal, the balance goes up); a *check*, a *transfer out* and a *fee* take money out (rust, the balance goes down). The *running balance*: each row's balance is the balance above it, plus a deposit or minus everything else. Decimal addition and subtraction are named, not re-taught: "line up the point, then it is whole numbers," family 2's stack with headers, drawn beside the table for each step.

**Read.** Two questions. "Balance after the last entry": run every row. "The amount in the row X is missing": the amount is the gap between the balance above and the balance in that row. At a glance: the final balance is the start plus the deposits minus everything else, so an estimate is available before any row (182 + 125 − 58 is about 249); a missing deposit is the rise between two balances, a missing check the drop.

**The example.** Start 182.47. Rows: Deposit 125.00; Check 101 41.70; Fee 3.25; Transfer 13.50. (1) Deposit: 182.47 + 125.00 in the stack, hundredths first: 307.47; the balance cell fills. (2) Check 101: 307.47 − 41.70; tenths 4 − 7 needs a trade, one one becomes ten tenths: 265.77. (3) Fee: 265.77 − 3.25 = 262.52. (4) Transfer: 262.52 − 13.50 = 249.02. (5) Answer 249.02, with the estimate beside it.

**Second case, fully worked because it reverses the move.** The same register with the Check 101 amount blank and both balances shown, 307.47 above and 265.77 in the row. The balance fell, so money went out. The gap: 307.47 − 265.77 = 41.70, stacked. Answer 41.70, entered without a sign as the problem asks.

**Fast.** Deposits add, everything else subtracts, one stack per row. A missing amount: subtract the two balances around it.
**Sense.** Each deposit row ends higher than the row above; every other row ends lower. The final balance is near the estimate.
**Check.** Final balance: start + deposits − payments recomputed in one line (182.47 + 125.00 − 58.45 = 249.02). Missing amount: subtract it from the balance above and land on the balance below.
**Line.** "Money in goes up; money out goes down; each balance comes from the one above."
**Trap.** Adding a check or fee because the register is a list of numbers (children add everything). The entry column is read before the arithmetic and the amount takes its color on the word. The decimal alignment slip (3.25 under 262.52 by last digit) is place value's trap, killed by the headed stack.
**Kit.** `table` with the `running` column, `run` row by row, a `?` cell and `solve`; `stack` with headers beside the table; no number line until balances can go negative.
**Writer.** Cheaper model from this concept; one method, and the stack does the arithmetic.

## Grade 6: absolute-value (6.NS.C.7)

### `signed-context-order` — Size against position, and ordered lists

**Standard.** 6.NS.C.7b: "Write, interpret, and explain statements of order for rational numbers in real-world contexts"; 6.NS.C.7c: "interpret absolute value as magnitude for a positive or negative quantity in a real-world situation"; 6.NS.C.7d, exactly: "Distinguish comparisons of absolute value from statements about order." The list wording practices 7a and 6c. The deepest grade-6 lesson and the misconception the tests aim at.

**Foundations first.** The shared opening, then two words. *Left is less*: order is position; on the line, the number farther left is less, whatever its digits. *Absolute value*: the distance from zero, written |x|, drawn as a blue bracket from zero to the dot; distance has no sign. Then the key line, said and shown before any problem: a number has a position and a size, and they are two different questions. Words that ask position: greater, less, higher, warmer, colder, greater balance. Words that ask size: owes more, bigger debt, deeper, farther from sea level, farther from zero.

**Read.** For the story kind, every choice is a reason; both questions must be answered on the picture before the choices are read. For the list kind, the direction word is gold. At a glance: both balances are negative, so both are debts, and the one farther left is the smaller balance and the bigger debt.

**The example (story).** Student A −53.25 dollars, Student B −37.25 dollars. A line from −60 to 10, labels every 10, ticks every 5, both dots rust with names above. (1) Position: the gold scan from the left meets Student A first. Student A is farther left: −53.25 < −37.25. Student B has the greater balance. (2) Size: two blue brackets from zero. Student A's is longer: |−53.25| = 53.25, |−37.25| = 37.25. Student A owes more. (3) Now the choices. "Student A owes more, because |−53.25| = 53.25 is greater than 37.25": a size question answered with sizes. True. "Student A has the greater balance, because 53.25 is greater than 37.25": greater balance is position, and Student A is farther left. False. "They owe the same, because both are negative": two different brackets. False. "Student B owes more, because −37.25 is greater than −53.25": owing is size; −37.25 being greater makes Student B's the greater balance, the smaller debt. False. The three dim together. (4) Answer the first.

**The second example (list), fully worked because it is a different act.** Order −4/5, 9/10, −2/5, −0.3 from least to greatest. (1) Rename each to a decimal (families 2 and 9): −0.8, 0.9, −0.4, −0.3; each label morphs. (2) Signs sort first: one positive, three negatives; 9/10 is last. (3) Drop on a line from −1 to 1 in tenths. (4) Scan left to right, writing the order: −4/5, −2/5, −0.3, 9/10. (5) The matching list. The temperature wording (Monday −3, Tuesday −7) is the story kind with one question only: colder is farther left, so −7 < −3; one beat on reading the sign aloud, since two choices differ only by the symbol.

**Fast.** Story: name the question's word, position or size, then read the line (scan for position, brackets for size). List: rename, signs first, negatives by size with the biggest size farthest left.
**Sense.** A bigger debt is a longer bracket on the left; the account that is "lower" owes more.
**Check.** Read the chosen statement aloud with its symbol and ask if it is true on the line; for a list, read it left to right and watch each step go right.
**Rule out.** A statement whose reason uses sizes to settle a position word, or positions to settle a size word, dims. "Owe the same" with unequal brackets dims. For lists: any list whose first entry is not the leftmost dot dims at once; any list with a negative after a positive dims.
**Line.** "Owing more is a longer bracket, not a bigger number. Left is less."
**Trap.** "Student A has the greater balance because 53.25 > 37.25", and "−7 > −3 because 7 > 3." Both die on one picture that answers position and size as two separate questions before the choices appear.
**Kit.** `numberline` with named points, `measure`, `scan`, `rename`, `drop`; wrapped sentence choices (exists); `dim`.
**Writer.** Strongest. The choices are misconceptions stated as reasons, and the lesson must hold two questions on one picture without blurring them.

### `number-line-distance` — Units between two marked points

**Standard.** Filed under 7.NS.A.1 (clause c, "the distance between two rational numbers on the number line is the absolute value of their difference"). At grade 6 it serves 6.NS.C.7c, distance from zero, and counting units across zero. Flag: the variants must put the points on opposite sides of zero, or this is a grade-2 count.

**Foundations first.** The shared opening; distance as the number of unit jumps between two points, counted on arcs, never ticks; zero as the landmark: the distance from zero to a dot is its absolute value, the blue bracket.

**Read.** "What is the distance between the two marked points? Each tick is one unit." At a glance: one point is left of zero and one is right, so the distance is the two distances from zero added, and it is bigger than either number's size.

**The example.** Points at −4 and 5, ticks every unit, labels every 2 or 5. (1) Read each point by counting jumps from zero: −4 is four jumps left; 5 is five jumps right. (2) A blue bracket from −4 to 5; at zero it shows its two parts, 4 and 5. (3) 4 + 5 = 9. (4) Answer 9. (5) One beat: the jumps counted straight through, one to nine, agree.

**Quick second case** only if the generator produces it: both points on the same side (−7 and −2). The brackets from zero overlap; the distance is the longer minus the shorter: 7 − 2 = 5. Three beats. The grade-7 form 5 − (−4) is left to the subtraction lesson, where "turn back" earns it.

**Fast.** Across zero: add the two distances from zero. Same side: subtract them.
**Sense.** Across zero, the distance is more than either number's size.
**Check.** Count the unit jumps straight through from one dot to the other.
**Line.** "Count the jumps to zero, then the jumps past it."
**Trap.** Counting ticks or dots instead of jumps (10 or 8 for 9); and 5 − 4 = 1, subtracting the digits as if both were positive. The bracket split at zero shows two parts that add.
**Kit.** `measure` between two points with its two parts shown across zero.
**Writer.** Cheaper model from this concept.

### `order-rationals-slots` — Order signed fractions and decimals into slots

**Standard.** 6.NS.C.7a: "interpret statements of inequality as statements about the relative position of two numbers on a number line diagram"; 6.NS.C.6c: "find and position integers and other rational numbers on a horizontal … number line diagram."

**Foundations first.** The shared opening; left is less; a fraction's decimal is how it is placed (named from families 2 and 9: 3/5 is 0.6, 3/2 is 1.5), with the negative side the mirror of the positive, so −0.8 sits as far left as 0.8 sits right.

**Read.** The direction word goes gold. At a glance: the negatives fill the first slots for least to greatest; among negatives the biggest size goes farthest left; the positive is last.

**The example.** Least to greatest: 3/2, −1/5, −0.8, −3/5. (1) Rename: 1.5, −0.2, −0.8, −0.6; each label morphs, the original fraction kept in small type. (2) Signs sort first: three negatives, then 3/2. (3) Drop each on a line from −1 to 2 in tenths. (4) Scan left to right; as each dot is met, its original form fills the next slot: −0.8, −3/5, −1/5, 3/2. (5) Read the finished list along the line.

**Quick second case** (the other direction, three beats): greatest to least −0.2, −0.6, 3/2, 0.75: the same drops, the scan runs right to left: 3/2, 0.75, −0.2, −0.6. Five numbers change nothing.

**Fast.** Rename to decimals; signs first; negatives with the biggest size farthest left; positives by size; then fill the slots in the asked direction.
**Sense.** Least to greatest starts with a negative and ends with the positive.
**Check.** Read the slots in order and watch each dot sit to the right of the last (or left, for greatest to least).
**Line.** "Signs first, then size. Left is less."
**Trap.** Ordering negatives by their digits (−0.2 before −0.6 as "less"); and filling least-to-greatest when greatest-to-least was asked. The scan direction is tied to the gold word, and the brackets make the longer one sit farther left.
**Kit.** `rename`, `drop`, `scan` with `write` into the problem's slots.
**Writer.** Cheaper model from this concept.

### `inequality-number-blanks` — Which number makes each inequality true

**Standard.** Filed under 6.EE.B.5, "which values from a specified set … make the … inequality true? Use substitution." It equally practices 6.NS.C.7a. Flag: only one wording was produced and the brief does not show the two inequalities or the four numbers; the exact layout (two boxes, one set of four numbers) must be confirmed from `facts.ts` before the screenplay is written. The example below assumes it.

**Foundations first.** The shared opening; left is less; the symbols < and > read as positions: "□ < t" means the box is a number left of t; "□ > t" a number right of t (place value's "small end to the small number" named once). The shaded stretch: every number left of t makes □ < t true, drawn as a hollow dot at t and shade to the left arrow.

**Read.** Two boxes, each with its own bound; exactly one of the four numbers fits each. At a glance: for □ < t with t negative, only a number farther left than t fits, so any positive is out at once; for □ > t with t positive, any negative is out.

**The example.** Numbers −1/4, −0.9, 0.2, 0.75; make true □ < −1/2 and □ > 1/2. (1) Rename: −0.25, −0.9, 0.2, 0.75; the bounds −0.5 and 0.5. (2) Drop all six on a line from −1 to 1 in tenths, the bounds as hollow dots. (3) Shade left of −0.5: only −0.9 is inside: −0.9 < −1/2. (4) Shade right of 0.5: only 0.75 is inside: 0.75 > 1/2. (5) Substitute each chosen number back and read the sentence aloud; the two statements are true on the line.

**Fast.** Rename, drop, shade the side the symbol names, take the one dot inside.
**Sense.** The number chosen for □ < t is farther left than t; for □ > t farther right.
**Check.** Put the number in and read the statement with the symbol in words.
**Line.** "Less than is left of; greater than is right of."
**Trap.** Choosing −1/4 for □ < −1/2 because 1/4 < 1/2: sizes, not positions. The shaded stretch left of −0.5 does not contain −0.25.
**Kit.** `shade` with an open bound; `rename`, `drop`.
**Writer.** Cheaper model, after the layout is confirmed.

## Grade 7: signed-addition (7.NS.A.1)

### `integer-addition` — Add two integers

**Standard.** 7.NS.A.1b, exactly: "Understand p + q as the number located a distance |q| from p, in the positive or negative direction depending on whether q is positive or negative. Show that a number and its opposite have a sum of 0." Also 1d, properties as strategies, and "represent addition … on a horizontal … number line diagram." Foundation 6; the engine for all of grade 7.

**Foundations first.** The grade-6 line named in one sentence (sign is side, digits are distance). Then the new picture, built live: *adding is walking*. Start at the first number; walk the size of the second, right if it is positive, left if it is negative; the sum is where you land. Shown once on 3 + (−5): start at 3, a rust arc of 5 to the left, landing at −2, and the net arc. The size of a number (its distance from zero) named as the length of the walk. The "rules" are not stated yet; they are read off the pictures after the examples.

**Read.** "What is (−12) + (−9)?" At a glance: both negative, both walks go left, so the sum is negative and bigger in size than either. "What is (−2) + 14?" Different signs, the walks fight; the 14 is the longer walk, so the sum is positive, and it is the difference of the sizes.

**The example (same signs).** (−12) + (−9). (1) Start at −12 on a line from −25 to 5. (2) Walk 9 to the left: a rust arc labeled −9, landing at −21. (3) The net arc from zero's side: the two walks go the same way, so the distances add, 12 + 9 = 21, and the landing is on the negative side. (4) Answer −21. (5) The rule read off the picture: same signs, add the sizes, keep the sign.

**The second example (different signs, across zero), fully worked.** (−2) + 14. (1) Start at −2. (2) Walk 14 to the right, teal arc; it crosses the mirror: two jumps bring it to zero, twelve more carry it on. (3) Land at 12. (4) The rule read off the picture: the walks fight; the longer one wins by the difference, 14 − 2 = 12, and the sum takes the longer walk's sign. One quick beat on 9 + (−9): start at 9, walk 9 left, land on zero: a number and its opposite sum to zero. 14 + (−9) is the second case again with the walk not crossing zero; covered by the method. 4 + 6 is the grade-2 half; never the worked example.

**Fast.** Sign first: same signs keep it; different signs, the longer walk's sign. Then size: same signs add, different signs subtract.
**Sense.** A walk left from a negative ends more negative; a walk across zero ends on the far side; the sum of opposites is zero.
**Check.** Walk back: from the landing, walk the second number's size the other way and land on the start (from −21, 9 right is −12). Or sizes: 12 + 9 = 21; 14 − 2 = 12.
**Line.** "Adding is walking: right for positive, left for negative."
**Trap.** (−12) + (−9) = 21, "two negatives make a positive," borrowed from multiplication before it is learned; and (−2) + 14 = −16 or −12, adding or subtracting the sizes and keeping the first number's sign. Two rust arcs the same way, and a teal arc that visibly crosses zero, make both impossible.
**Kit.** `numberline` `walk` (chained, arcs that arch higher over covered ground), `net`; `reverse` is not needed until subtraction.
**Writer.** Strongest. The pilot lesson that gives grade 7 its picture; the walk across zero and the moment the rules are read off the pictures must be staged exactly.
