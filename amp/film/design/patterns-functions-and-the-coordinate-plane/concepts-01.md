# Patterns, functions and the coordinate plane: per-type concepts, part 1 (grades 3 to 4)

Ten problem types, two skills. Every one of them stands on foundation 1 (a rule is an arrow: a number goes in, one number comes out, same in gives same out) or foundation 2 (a pattern is a rule run step after step from a start; the position counts the jumps). The pictures are the **table with its rule arrow** and the **strip with its arcs**, drawn as in the family design, plus the array from family 7 where the standard asks for the why. Voice for these grades: "the number that goes in", "the number that comes out", "jump", "step"; nothing is struck out; the right move is shown instead.

A note that applies to the whole skill `arithmetic-patterns` (grade 3): of its four types, only `times-table-pattern` and `neighbor-product-change` practice 3.OA.D.9 ("identify arithmetic patterns, including patterns in the ... multiplication table, and explain them using properties of operations"). `situation-pair-table` is a word-problem type and `repeating-shape-pattern` has no Common Core standard. Both are noted below.

### `situation-pair-table` — Use a real-life rule to fill a table: forward, backward or the rate

**Misfiled.** Filed under 3.OA.D.9; it practices 3.OA.A.3 ("use multiplication and division within 100 to solve word problems in situations involving equal groups"), 3.OA.A.4 ("determine the unknown whole number in a multiplication or division equation") and, for the age tables, the additive comparison of 2.OA.A.1 ("comparing, with unknowns in all positions"). It is a family 8 reading type that rides here because its picture is the table. The lesson is **mixed**: four stories, no shared recipe; it teaches reading.

**Foundations first.** A table (two columns, a header on each, one row per pair). A rule: what turns the left number into the right number, drawn as the teal arrow from a left cell to its right cell with the operation riding it. Two kinds of rule live in these stories and the film names both before any question: **equal groups** ("each box holds 7": the right number is the left number times 7, and the array of 8 rows of 7 is named from family 7 in one line) and **a fixed gap** ("4 years older": the right number is the left number plus 4, the same gap in every row because both people age together). The film says once: find the rule on a row you can see, then test it on another row before trusting it.

**Read.** Three questions hide under one name, and the asked column decides. "How many pencils for 8 boxes": the right column is missing, run the arrow forward. "How many buses give 32 students": the left column is missing, run the arrow backward. "How many apples for one bag": the rule itself is missing; find it. For ages, "older than" (gold) means an add rule, never a times rule. At a glance: forward times gives a bigger number; backward gives a smaller one; one bag's worth is less than any total in the table; the younger person's age is the smaller one in every row.

**Example A, forward (full).** Boxes 2, 3, 5, 8; pencils 14, 21, 35, ?. (1) Read the story; "each box holds 7" is equal groups. (2) Draw the arrow from 2 to 14 labeled × 7; test it on the row 3 → 21: it holds. (3) The gold "?" cell: 8 slides along the arrow, × 7 is read off it, 56 is written on the word. (4) 56 pencils. Sense: more pencils than boxes, and 8 boxes is a bit more than 5 boxes (35), so a bit more than 35. Check: 56 ÷ 7 = 8.

**Example B, backward (full).** Buses 2, 5, ?; students 8, 20, 32. (1) "Each bus seats 4": × 4, tested on 5 → 20. (2) The missing number is on the left, so the arrow turns around and its label becomes ÷ 4: "to find the number that went in, undo." (3) 32 slides back along it: 32 ÷ 4 = 8 buses. Sense: fewer buses than students. Check: 8 × 4 = 32, run forward.

**Example C, the rate (full).** Bags 2, 3, 6, 1; apples 10, 15, 30, ?. (1) "The same number in every bag" but the number is not given: the arrow has no label yet. (2) Pick a row with a bag count that is not 0 or 1: 3 bags, 15 apples; 15 ÷ 3 = 5, so the label is × 5. (3) Test on 6 → 30: it holds. (4) One bag: 1 × 5 = 5 apples. Sense: less than any row's apples. Check: the rule works on every row.

**Example D, the gap, backward (quick).** Student C 6, 9, 12, ?; Student B 10, 13, 16, 14. "Student B is 4 years older": + 4, tested on 9 → 13. Student B's age 14 is given, so undo: 14 − 4 = 10. Student C is 10. Sense: Student C is younger in every row. Check: 10 + 4 = 14.

**Fast.** Find the rule on one row, test it on a second; forward means do it, backward means undo it (divide undoes times, subtract undoes add), and the rate is one row's right number divided by its left number.
**Line.** "Find the rule on a row you can see. Forward: do it. Backward: undo it."
**Trap.** Realistic and common: going backward with the rule still forward: 32 students × 4 = 128 buses, or Student C = 14 + 4 = 18 when Student B is 14. The arrow turned around with the undo written on it, and the sense line ("fewer buses than students", "Student C is younger in every row") make it impossible. Also "4 years older" multiplied (4 × 14): the film names it a gap, the same +4 in every row.
**Kit.** The family's `table` with `rule`, `run`, `reverse` and `fill` on a gold "?" cell. Nothing more.
**Writer.** Strongest writer: a mixed reading type where the choice of story and the words that decide each kind are the whole lesson.

### `times-table-pattern` — A missing product in part of a multiplication table

**Standard.** 3.OA.D.9: "identify arithmetic patterns (including patterns in the ... multiplication table), and explain them using properties of operations"; 3.OA.C.7 for the multiply-back check. Shared with family 7.

**Foundations first.** What a multiplication table is: the cell where row 6 meets column 5 holds 6 × 5, and 6 × 5 is 6 rows of 5 squares. Built live: the part of the table on the board, the gold "?" cell, and beside it the array for that cell drawn as a grid of unit squares, 6 across... no: 6 rows tall, 5 wide, to scale. Every pattern in the table is a way of cutting that array; that is the "properties of operations" the standard asks for, and the film shows three cuts on one array so the child sees they are the same 30.

**Read.** The question names the cell (row 6, column 5) and often names a pattern to use ("the sum of the two products above it", "6 more than the one before", "double the row for 3"). The pattern is a way to the answer; row × column is what the cell means. At a glance: the answer sits between its row neighbors (24 and 36) and ends in 0 or 5 because it is in the column for 5.

**Example (full).** Rows 2, 3, 4, 6; columns 4, 5, 6; the "?" at row 6, column 5. (1) Name the cell: 6 × 5, six rows of five; the array appears, 6 rows of 5 squares. (2) Cut one: the row pattern. The cell to the left is 6 × 4 = 24; one more column adds one square to each of the 6 rows: a rust column of 6 slides on; 24 + 6 = 30. "Along the row for 6, each step adds 6, one more in every row." (3) Cut two: double the row for 3. A line splits the array into 3 rows and 3 rows: 15 and 15; 30. (4) Cut three: the rows for 2 and 4 above it. The split moves: 2 rows (10) and 4 rows (20); 30. (5) Three cuts, one array, one answer: 30. Check: 6 × 5 = 30 from memory.

**Quick second case (a different reading).** "This part of a multiplication table has one empty cell": row 4, column 4, no pattern named. Name it, 4 × 4 = 16; the pattern is the check: 4 × 3 = 12, one more column of 4 makes 16.

**Fast.** Row times column, always. Use the named pattern as the check, or the check as the pattern.
**Sense.** The answer is between the neighbors in its row and in its column.
**Check.** Multiply row by column; or walk one step along the row and add the row number.
**Line.** "Along a row, each step adds the row number: one more in every row."
**Trap.** Along the row for 6, adding the column number instead of the row number (24 + 5 = 29). The rust column that arrives is visibly one square in each of six rows. Also 6 + 5 = 11: the array has thirty squares, not eleven.
**Kit.** `grid` and `seg` for the array and its cut lines exist; the lesson needs a column of cells to arrive in rust (a `counters` or `grid` show on a column) and the table part with a gold "?" cell. The split line moving from after row 3 to after row 2 is a `move` of a `seg`.
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one array, three cuts fully specified here, and the math check verifies every number.

### `neighbor-product-change` — An array gains one row; how many squares were added

**Standard.** 3.OA.A.1 ("interpret 5 × 7 as the total number of objects in 5 groups of 7 objects each") and 3.OA.D.9 (the why: 7 × 5 is 6 × 5 plus one more 5). Shared with family 7. One variant; a short lesson.

**Foundations first.** An array: rows of equal length; each row is one group; 6 rows of 5 is 6 × 5 = 30, drawn to scale with the count under it. A new row is one more group.

**Read.** "Grows from 6 rows to 7 rows, 5 per row. How many squares were added?" The question asks for what was added, not the new total. At a glance: one row was added, so the answer is one row's worth: the number per row.

**Example (full).** (1) The 6 × 5 array, teal, count 30 under it. (2) A rust row of 5 slides on underneath: "one more row is one more group of five"; the count ticks 31 ... 35. (3) The new array is 7 × 5 = 35; the rust row is what was added: 5. (4) On the board: 7 × 5 = 6 × 5 + 5. Sense: what was added is small, one row, far less than the array. Check: 35 − 30 = 5.

**Fast.** The number of squares in one row.
**Line.** "One more row is one more group."
**Trap.** Answering 35 (the new total) or 7 (the new row count). Realistic: "how many" pulls the eye to the whole. The rust row stays rust after it lands, and the question's "added" goes gold; the answer is the rust count.
**Kit.** Exists: `grid`, a rust row shown with a count label. Nothing new.
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model.

### `repeating-shape-pattern` — Continue or complete a repeating pattern of shapes

**Off grade and unstandardized.** Repeating patterns have no Common Core standard; the type is filed under 4.OA.C.5 at grade 3 and belongs to K to 2 in states that assess it. Keep the film short.

**Foundations first.** The repeating part: the shortest run of shapes that comes again, in the same order, over and over. Shown by a bracket over the first copy, labeled "repeats", and the next copies bracketed in turn so the child sees the pattern is copies of one unit. The check that the unit is right: the next shapes match it in order.

**Read.** "Which two shapes go in the empty boxes?" The boxes are at the end, or in the middle ("from left to right"). At a glance: a shape that never appears in the pattern cannot be an answer; after the unit is found, the boxes are just the unit continued.

**Example A, boxes at the end (full).** circle, circle, triangle, circle, circle, triangle, circle, circle, □, □; choices: circle, triangle | circle, square | triangle, circle | circle, circle. (1) Find the unit: circle, circle, triangle; bracket it. (2) Check it comes again: the next three are circle, circle, triangle; bracket; then circle, circle; the third bracket is unfinished. (3) The unfinished copy needs its third shape: triangle. Then a new copy starts: circle. (4) The unit is stamped forward into the boxes: triangle, circle. Rule out: "circle, square" dims at once (no square anywhere); "circle, circle" and "circle, triangle" would make the third copy circle, circle, circle, circle, which is not the unit. Check: read the whole row aloud in threes.

**Example B, boxes in the middle (quick).** circle, □, □, circle, circle, triangle, circle, circle, triangle. The unit shows plainly in the copies after the gap: circle, circle, triangle. Stamp it onto the first copy: the gap is its second and third shapes: circle, triangle.

**Fast.** Bracket the unit, stamp it along; the boxes take whatever lands in them.
**Line.** "Find the part that repeats, then copy it along."
**Trap.** Taking a unit that is too short (seeing circle, circle as the unit, or circle, triangle) because the first two shapes feel like the pattern. The film checks the unit against the next copy before stamping; a wrong unit fails the check on screen.
**Kit.** `counters` with `shape: "triangle"` (and square), a `bracket` over a run of grid cells with a label, and `stamp` to copy the unit into the next cells (family design 4.5).
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model.

### `rule-pattern-term` — A later term from the start and the rule *(pilot)*

**Standard.** 4.OA.C.5: "Generate a number or shape pattern that follows a given rule."

**Foundations first.** Pattern: numbers made one after another by a rule. Term: one number of the pattern. Position: first, second, third ... which one it is. Rule: what you do to a term to get the next one, drawn as an arc from each term to the next with the step written on it, the same arc the number line uses for hops. The strip is built live: positions 1, 2, 3 ... in blue across the top; the start, 19, under position 1; an arc labeled + 11 to the next term. Then the one thing this lesson exists to say: **term 1 has had no jumps.** The arcs are what we count, never the terms.

**Read.** "Starts at 19. Rule: add 11. What is the 10th number?" (gold on "10th" and on "add 11"). The answer is a term, not a position. At a glance: the pattern grows, so the answer is well past 19: about nine elevens past it, somewhere near 120, and its ones digit comes from 9 + nine 1s, so it ends in 8.

**Example (full).** (1) The strip to position 10; 19 under 1. (2) Arcs appear one at a time on the counting words, each labeled + 11, the terms written under as they are reached: 30, 41, 52, 63, 74, 85, 96, 107, 118. "One jump, two jumps ... nine jumps." (3) Nine arcs reach position 10. The term under the last arc goes gold: 118. (4) The fast way, read off the strip: nine jumps of 11 is 99; 19 + 99 = 118, the same number. "The tenth term is nine jumps."

**Example B, a multiply rule (full but brief: a different motion).** Start 3, multiply by 2, 6th term. The arcs read × 2; there is no "five twos added" shortcut, so each term is written: 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96. Five arcs for the 6th term. 96. Sense: doubling grows fast; 96 is far past 3 after only five jumps. Check: count the five arcs.

**Quick beat.** Subtract 2 from 28, 6th term: five arcs down, 28 − 10 = 18; the same count, downhill.

**Fast.** Add or subtract rule: start + (position − 1) × step, where position − 1 is the number of arcs. Multiply rule: write every term along the arcs.
**Sense.** The answer is on the far side of the start in the rule's direction, about (position − 1) steps away.
**Check.** Count the arcs one by one and land on the term; for add rules, the ones digit of the shortcut matches the written term.
**Line.** "The tenth term is nine jumps."
**Trap.** 19 + 10 × 11 = 129: counting terms, not jumps. The strip makes it impossible: position 10 sits under exactly nine arcs, and arcs are the only things ever counted aloud. The lesson shows the tenth arc would reach position 11.
**Kit.** The family's `table` strip: `orient: "rows"`, inks blue and rust, `arcs` drawn per counted word with a live count of arcs, `fill` on the gold cell. This lesson is where the strip is born.
**Writer.** Strongest writer: the picture is born here and every later pattern and sequence lesson points back to it.

### `rule-pattern-feature` — A feature every term of the pattern shares

**Standard.** 4.OA.C.5: "Identify apparent features of the pattern that were not explicit in the rule itself ... Explain informally why the numbers will continue in this way." Multiple choice cannot ask the explanation; the film gives it anyway, because the explanation is what makes the answer sure.

**Foundations first.** Pattern, term and rule from `rule-pattern-term`, named in one line with the strip. New: a feature, something true of every term that the rule did not say out loud. The habit, said before any statement is judged: write at least three terms first; one term proves nothing; then say why from the rule.

**Read.** "Starts at 89. Rule: add 50. Which statement about the pattern is true?" Choices: every number is even; every number ends in 0; every number ends in 9; the numbers alternate odd and even. At a glance: 89 is odd and ends in 9, so "every number is even" and "every number ends in 0" are false before any adding. Two remain, and 89 alone cannot choose between them.

**Example (full).** (1) Strip: 89, + 50 arcs, 139, 189, 239. (2) The two false-at-once statements dim together: "89 is odd and ends in 9; these two are gone." (3) Test "alternate": 89, 139, 189 are all odd; dim. (4) "Ends in 9": 89, 139, 189, 239 all do. (5) The why, on family 2's chart in one beat: 89 is 8 rods and 9 squares; adding 50 is adding 5 rods; the 9 squares never move. Every term ends in 9 because the rule only ever adds tens. "True for every term, not just the ones we wrote."

**Quick second case (the standard's own).** Start 1, add 3: 1, 4, 7, 10, 13. Odd, even, odd, even. Why: 3 is odd, and adding an odd number to an odd number gives an even one (pairs with one left over, plus pairs with one left over, make all pairs), and to an even one gives an odd one. The alternation is forced by the rule.

**Fast.** Write three or four terms; dim every statement the first term breaks; test the rest on all the terms; then say why from the rule so you know it never stops.
**Sense.** A feature should be visible in the rule: adding tens leaves the ones alone; adding an odd number flips odd and even.
**Check.** One more term (289) still has it.
**Rule out.** Any statement the start itself breaks dims at once; "alternate" needs two terms to kill.
**Line.** "Write three terms, then say why from the rule."
**Trap.** Judging from one term, or from the rule's own number: "add 50, 50 is even, so the numbers are even." Three written terms, all odd, sit on screen before any statement is chosen, and the chart shows the ones digit untouched.
**Kit.** The strip plus the existing `blocks` chart for the one why beat; `dim`.
**Writer.** Strongest writer: the lesson is the explanation, and a weak author will recite the test and skip the why.

### `rule-table-fill-slots` — Use a rule to find outputs; undo it to find inputs

**Off grade in its notation.** The rule is written y = x + 2, y = x − 10, y = 6x; letters standing for numbers are 6.EE.A.2 ("write, read, and evaluate expressions in which letters stand for numbers"), not grade 4. The content is 4.OA.C.5 ("generate a ... pattern that follows a given rule"). The film says once: "x is the number that goes in; y is the number that comes out," and then uses the letters because the problem does.

**Foundations first.** The table with x on top in blue and y beneath in rust (the app's layout), the rule arrow from an x cell to its y cell with the operation on it, and gold "?" cells. Forward: a number goes in, the rule is done to it. Backward: a y is known and the x is missing; the arrow turns around and its label becomes the undo (÷ 6 undoes × 6; + 10 undoes − 10).

**Read.** "Use the rule y = 6x to complete the table." Which cells are empty decides the direction: a missing y is forward; a missing x is backward. At a glance: for × 6 every y is bigger than its x, so a slot choice smaller than the x beside it is nonsense; for − 10 every y is smaller.

**Example (full).** y = 6x; x: 3, 7, ?, ?; y: ?, ?, 54, 30. (1) "6x means six times x": the arrow from 3 labeled × 6; 3 slides along it, 18 is written. 7 → 42. (2) Now a y is known and its x is not. The arrow turns around: ÷ 6. 54 slides back: 9. 30 slides back: 5. (3) Each pair put back through the rule forward: 9 × 6 = 54, 5 × 6 = 30. The slot choices for each cell are shown and the chosen one lit; the others dim with the one reason ("smaller than its x: can't be six times it").

**Quick second case (undoing a subtraction).** y = x − 10; y = 23 given, x missing. Undo subtract 10 by adding 10: 33. Check: 33 − 10 = 23. The trap case: subtracting again gives 13, and 13 − 10 is 3, not 23.

**Fast.** Missing y: do the rule to x. Missing x: undo the rule on y. Put every pair back through the rule.
**Sense.** × 6 makes y bigger than x; − 10 makes y smaller than x; the direction of every pair matches.
**Check.** Run each found pair forward.
**Rule out.** Slot choices on the wrong side of x dim at once.
**Line.** "Forward: do the rule. Backward: undo it."
**Trap.** Doing the rule forward to a known y (54 × 6 = 324; 23 − 10 = 13). The arrow visibly turned around, labeled with the undo, and the sense check (y smaller than x for − 10) make it impossible.
**Kit.** `table` with `orient: "rows"`, `rule`, `run`, `reverse`, `fill`, `dim`.
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one arrow, two directions, every number machine-checkable.

### `growing-shape-pattern` — A later step of a shape pattern that adds the same number of tiles

**Standard.** 4.OA.C.5: "Generate a number or shape pattern that follows a given rule." The goal rightly asks for the rule, not a count.

**Foundations first.** Steps 1, 2 and 3 drawn as the objects the story names (flags, posts, stones, as the KAIZEN note asks; counters that can be anything), the new objects at each step in rust, the count under each step. The counts become the strip from `rule-pattern-term`: 2, 5, 8 with + 3 arcs. "A shape pattern is a number pattern you can see." Step 1 has had no additions.

**Read.** "Starts with 2 flags. Each next step adds 3. Steps 1, 2 and 3 are shown. How many flags in step 6?" (gold on "step 6"). At a glance: step 6 is three steps past step 3, so about 9 more than 8: near 17. And the drawn steps must match the rule before it is trusted.

**Example (full).** (1) Step 1: 2 flags. Step 2: 2 teal, 3 rust: 5. Step 3: 5 teal, 3 rust: 8. The rule holds on every drawn step. (2) The counts drop into the strip: steps 1 to 6 across the top; 2, 5, 8 beneath; arcs + 3. (3) Arcs to step 6 counted: one, two, three, four, five jumps. Step 6 is five jumps from step 1: 5 × 3 = 15; 2 + 15 = 17. (4) The strip fills by hopping: 11, 14, 17; the same number. Sense: three more jumps than step 3's 8, so 8 + 9 = 17. Check: the hop count lands on 17.

No second example: every variant is the same case with other names and numbers (the figure proves the method covers them).

**Fast.** Start + (step − 1) × amount added; step − 1 is the number of jumps.
**Line.** "Step 6 is five jumps."
**Trap.** 2 + 6 × 3 = 20: counting steps, not jumps. The strip shows step 6 under five arcs. A second realistic one: drawing step 4, 5, 6 and counting, which is slow and loses count; the film draws nothing past step 3 and says so ("the rule tells us; we don't have to draw it").
**Kit.** `counters` in a story shape (a flag or post glyph: one generic "object" shape that takes a label is enough), the strip. `rule-pattern-term` must land first.
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model, once the strip exists.

### `pattern-rule-choice` — The rule of a pattern from its terms, or the pattern with the same rule

**Standard.** 3.OA.D.9: "Identify arithmetic patterns ... and explain them using properties of operations." Filed under grade 4's `rule-patterns`; the content is grade 3 to 4 either way.

**Foundations first.** The strip with the terms given and the arcs empty. The arc's label is found by comparing neighbors: subtract to find how much it goes up. If every arc gets the same label, the rule is "add that". If the labels differ, ask instead how many times as big each term is: divide the term by the one before; the same answer each time is a "multiply by" rule. Both labels shown on the arcs, the differences above and the ratios in a second row when needed. The rule is a rule only if it holds on every arc.

**Read.** Case A: "Look at 2, 6, 18, 54, 162. Which pattern follows the same rule?" Case B: "2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Which rule makes this pattern?" At a glance: in A the jumps 2 → 6 → 18 grow fast, not by a fixed amount, so the rule multiplies; a choice whose terms grow by a fixed amount is gone. In B, the differences 2, 4, 8 are not all equal, so "add 8" is gone at once.

**Example A (full).** 2, 6, 18, 54, 162. (1) Arcs labeled with differences: + 4, + 12, + 36: not equal, not an add rule. (2) Ratios: 6 ÷ 2 = 3, 18 ÷ 6 = 3, 54 ÷ 18 = 3, 162 ÷ 54 = 3. The rule is multiply by 3; written on every arc. (3) Choices: 2, 11, 20, 29, 38 (arcs + 9: an add rule) and 3, 7, 12, 18, 25 (arcs + 4, + 5, + 6, + 7: growing adds) dim together: "fixed or growing adds, not multiply." (4) 3, 6, 12, 24, 48: 6 ÷ 3 = 2: multiply by 2; dims. (5) 1, 3, 9, 27, 81: 3 ÷ 1, 9 ÷ 3, 27 ÷ 9, 81 ÷ 27 all 3. The same rule; the arcs match. Check: every arc, not just the first.

**Example B (quick).** 2, 4, 8, 16, 32: differences 2, 4, 8, 16 not equal; "add 8" dims. "Add 2, then 3, then 4": the arcs would read + 2, + 3, + 4; ours read + 2, + 4, + 8; dims. Ratios: all 2. "Multiply by 3" would give 6 after 2; dims. Multiply by 2.

**Fast.** Label the arcs with differences; equal means add. Not equal: label with ratios; equal means multiply. Test every arc of the choice.
**Sense.** A multiply pattern's jumps get bigger and bigger; an add pattern's jumps are all the same size.
**Check.** Make the next term with the rule and compare with the list.
**Rule out.** A choice whose first two arcs disagree with the rule dims at once; choices are dimmed by kind, together.
**Line.** "Same jump every time: add. Jumps that grow by the same times: multiply."
**Trap.** Reading the first arc only ("2 to 6 is add 4") and stopping; the second arc, + 12, is on screen before any rule is named. Also naming "the differences double" as the rule: true, but the choices are written as "multiply by", and the ratio row shows why those say the same thing.
**Kit.** `table` strip with `arcs` labeled per arc and `diff: true` for the row of differences; a second row for ratios (the same `diff` with `"by": "ratio"`); `dim`.
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model.

### `pattern-term-unknown-rule` — A later term when the rule is not given, including changing changes

**Standard.** 3.OA.D.9 for the constant-change case. The shrinking-difference case (15, 22, 28, 33, 37) is past 4.OA.C.5: a stretch item. Keep it, and say in the lesson that the changes are themselves a pattern.

**Foundations first.** The strip with terms given and arcs unlabeled; labels found by comparing neighbors (from `pattern-rule-choice`, one line). New here: the arcs' labels are a list of their own, written in a row above the strip. If that row is all the same number, keep using it. If it changes by the same amount each time, continue it the way it is going, and use each new arc on the last term.

**Read.** "Starts 79, 71, 63, 55. What is the next number?" and "Starts 15, 22, 28, 33, 37. What is the seventh number?" (gold on "seventh"). At a glance: the first goes down by about 8 each time, so next is near 47. The second goes up but by less each time, so it still rises, slowly; the seventh is two arcs past 37, each smaller than 4.

**Example A (full).** 79, 71, 63, 55. Arcs: 79 − 71 = 8, so − 8; 71 − 63: − 8; 63 − 55: − 8. One more arc: 55 − 8 = 47. Check: 47 is 8 below 55 like every neighbor pair.

**Example B (full; a different case).** 15, 22, 28, 33, 37. (1) Arcs: + 7, + 6, + 5, + 4. Not the same. (2) The arc row is its own pattern, going down by 1. (3) Continue it: + 3, then + 2. (4) 37 is the 5th term; the seventh needs two more arcs: 37 + 3 = 40 (6th), 40 + 2 = 42 (7th). Sense: still going up, but each jump is smaller than the last. Check: the arcs read 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2 in order.

**Fast.** Label every arc. All the same: keep going with it. Changing evenly: continue the arc row, then hop.
**Sense.** The pattern keeps going in the direction of the arcs; shrinking arcs mean it rises more and more slowly.
**Check.** Read the arc row of the finished strip; it is one pattern.
**Line.** "If the jumps change, the jumps are the pattern."
**Trap.** Keeping the last jump (+ 4 → 41, 45) because the arcs were never written out; or giving the 6th term (40) for the seventh because positions were not labeled. The arc row and the blue position row are both on screen.
**Kit.** The strip with `arcs` labeled per arc and `diff: true`; nothing new beyond `pattern-rule-choice`'s needs.
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model.
