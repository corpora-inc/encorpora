# Patterns, functions and the coordinate plane, part 4: four quadrants, the definition of a function, story graphs, domain and range

Ten problem types in four skills, grade 6 to Algebra 1, written from the family design. What is off, first:

- `relation-function-choice` and `relation-point-removal` each generate exactly one item; until the generator varies the lists and the points, each is one memorized answer. The lessons below teach the method on a case built in front of the learner and then work the app's item, so they hold once the generator is fixed.
- `relation-function-choice`'s correct list repeats a pair, (−4, 2), (−4, 2). True (the same point written twice is one point) but a thin thing to hang the definition on. The lesson meets it head on rather than hiding it.
- `distance-graph-meaning` is one item whose decoy "the traveler must be standing still" turns on a real but fine distinction: a flat distance graph records distance, not motion. Right, and worth teaching; the template should say the distinction is intended.
- `story-graph-match`'s story-to-description kind never needs its graph. It is the verbal half of 8.F.B.5 and is fine, but the lesson treats it as the same reading run the other way, not a separate recipe.
- HSF.IF.B.5 speaks of the domain only; the range items in both domain-range types rest on HSF.IF.A.1's "range." Fine; noted so the alignment list is honest.
- 6.NS.C.8's distance clause ("coordinates and absolute value to find distances between points with the same first coordinate") is still practiced by no type.

## Grade 6: coordinate-four-quadrants

### `point-on-signed-grid` — Which graph shows the point?

**Standard.** 6.NS.C.6b, "Understand signs of numbers in ordered pairs as indicating locations in quadrants of the coordinate plane," and 6.NS.C.6c, "find and position pairs of integers ... on a coordinate plane." 6.NS.C.8's first clause ("graphing points in all four quadrants") in passing; its distance clause not at all.

**Foundations first.** Start from the grade-5 plane the learner knows (one room, across then up). Extend the x axis left past zero, writing −1, −2, −3 as it grows: zero is the mirror (family 3), and the left side is the negative side. Extend the y axis down the same way. The axes now cut the plane into four rooms; say the word *quadrant* once ("a quadrant is one of the four rooms"). Then the sentence the whole type rests on, said with the picture: *the x number tells how far left or right of the y axis; the y number tells how far above or below the x axis.* Right and up are positive; left and down are negative. Plot one point to show it, (−3, 2): a blue arrow from the origin 3 to the left, counting −1, −2, −3; a rust arrow up 2; the dot lands in the top-left room; the pair written in blue and rust. "Across, then up; the first number always goes across" (from grade 5, one line).

**Read.** "Which graph shows the point that is 2 units to the left of the y-axis and 3 units below the x-axis?" Two gold words, *left* and *below*: each is a direction, and each direction names a sign. At a glance: left means x is negative, below means y is negative, so the point is in the bottom-left room, and every graph with its point in another room is gone before anything is counted. The answer is one graph, and the pair it shows will read (negative, negative).

**Example.** Choices: a point at (−3, −2), at (2, −3), at (−2, 3), at (−2, −3). Drawn as four small planes under the question, to scale.
1. "Left of the y axis" is across, so it is the x number; left is the negative side: x = −2. The blue arrow runs 2 to the left from the origin, ticking −1, −2.
2. "Below the x axis" is up-down, so it is the y number; below is negative: y = −3. The rust arrow drops 3 from the arrow's end, ticking −1, −2, −3.
3. The dot lands; the pair is written (−2, −3), blue then rust. Bottom-left room, both signs negative, as promised.
4. The two graphs whose points sit in other rooms dim together: (2, −3) is right and down, (−2, 3) is left and up. Two remain, both bottom-left.
5. Across first. Ours went 2 across and 3 down. The graph at (−3, −2) went 3 across and 2 down: the right room, the wrong order. The graph at (−2, −3) is the one.
Quick second case (the plain wording, three beats): "Which graph shows (3, 6)?" Both signs positive: top-right room; only one graph has its point there; across 3, up 6, done.

**Fast.** Signs first: they name the room and leave one or two graphs. Then across first.

**Sense.** Two negatives means bottom-left; the chosen graph's point is bottom-left.

**Check.** From the chosen point, drop its shadows: the dashed line hits the y axis at −3 and the x axis at −2, and the voice reads it back as "2 left, 3 below."

**Rule out.** Every graph in the wrong room dims in one beat with one reason ("left and below is the bottom-left room"). If two remain, the swapped one dims with "across first."

**Line.** "Left or right is the first number, even when the wall you measure from is the y axis."

**Trap.** A child hears "2 units from the *y* axis" and changes y: (−3, −2). The lesson draws the arrow starting *at* the y axis and running across, so the distance from the y axis is visibly a left-right distance, which is x. Dropped signs ("left" read as 2) die on the axis numbers the arrow ticks past.

**Kit.** Four-quadrant `axes` with `grid`, axis numbers on both sides of zero, `quadrants`; the `plot` path with counts that tick negative going left or down; `shadow`; `dim`. New: choice pills that hold a small to-scale plane with one dot (the app's choices are graphs, not text), dimmable together.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, every picture a named action, the decoys fully named here.

## Grade 8: function-notation (the definition of a function)

### `relation-function-choice` — Which list is a function?

**Standard.** 8.F.A.1, "a function is a rule that assigns to each input exactly one output," and "The graph of a function is the set of ordered pairs," which the lesson uses to turn the lists into dots.

**Foundations first.** The rule arrow from grade 3, named in one line: a number goes in, one number comes out, same every time. Then the definition, plainly: *a function gives each input exactly one output; an input may not have two different outputs; two inputs may share an output.* Build the picture live: pairs (1, 4), (2, 4), (3, 6) in a table (input column blue, output column rust), each row sliding out to become a dot. Ask the question the definition asks, one input at a time, with the gold vertical line: at x = 1 the line touches one dot, at 2 one dot, at 3 one dot. Outputs 4 and 4 repeat; nothing is wrong, two different inputs just gave the same answer. Then add (2, 7): the dot stacks straight above (2, 4); the line at x = 2 touches two dots; "put 2 in, and two different numbers come out." Not a function. The sweep is the definition drawn: a vertical line is one input, and what it touches is that input's outputs.

**Read.** "Which list of (input, output) pairs represents a function?" The first number of each pair is the input. At a glance: only a repeated *first* number can break a list; a repeated second number never can. So scan first numbers only.

**Example.** Choices: (−4, 2), (−3, 2), (−3, 4); (−4, 2), (−4, 3), (−3, 3); (−4, 2), (−4, 2), (−3, 3).
1. List A: first numbers −4, −3, −3. Input −3 appears twice, with outputs 2 and 4. Plotted, two dots stacked at x = −3; the sweep touches both. Not a function. (The 2 that repeats in (−4, 2), (−3, 2) is an output; said aloud: that is fine.)
2. List B: −4 twice, with outputs 2 and 3. Stacked at x = −4. Not a function.
3. List C: −4 twice, but both times the output is 2. (−4, 2) written twice is the same point twice; plotted, one dot. Then (−3, 3). The sweep touches one dot at a time. A function.
4. A and B dim together: "a repeated input with two different outputs." C is the answer.

**Fast.** Scan the first numbers for a repeat; if it has two different second numbers, that list is out.

**Sense.** In the chosen list, asking "what does −4 give?" has one answer, 2; "what does −3 give?" has one answer, 3.

**Check.** Plot the chosen list and sweep: no vertical line touches two dots.

**Rule out.** Lists with a repeated first number carrying different second numbers dim together.

**Line.** "One input, one output. Two points stacked straight up and it is not a function."

**Trap.** Two real ones: a list is called "not a function" because an *output* repeats, (−4, 2), (−3, 2); and list C is called "not a function" because −4 repeats, without noticing both outputs are 2. The sweep kills the first (two dots at the same height are touched one at a time), and drawing C's repeated pair as a single dot kills the second.

**Kit.** `table` with `link`/`slide` row-to-point; `vline` with `sweep` and `hits`; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest. The definition and the sweep are born here, and every later function lesson points back to it.

### `relation-point-removal` — Which single point could be removed?

**Standard.** 8.F.A.1, "exactly one output," applied to a plotted relation.

**Foundations first.** One line back to the definition lesson: two dots stacked straight up share an input and break the function. Here the graph is given, four lettered dots, two of them stacked. The question is which *one* dot you could take away so nothing is stacked.

**Read.** "Which choice lists every point that could be removed, one at a time, so the remaining relation is a function?" Gold on *one at a time*: each answer is a single removal, tested by itself. At a glance: only the two stacked points can matter; removing any other point leaves the stack where it was. So the answer must name exactly the two stacked points.

**Example.** A(−3, 2), B(2, −1), C(1, 3), D(1, −2); choices A or B, C or D, A or C, B or D.
1. Sweep: the gold line crosses A alone, then at x = 1 touches C and D together; both pulse and stay lit. Those two share the input 1.
2. Take C away: sweep again; every vertical line touches one dot. Removing C works.
3. Put C back, take D away: sweep; clean. Removing D works.
4. Take A away instead: C and D still stand stacked at x = 1; the sweep still hits two. Removing A changes nothing.
5. C or D.

**Fast.** Find the stacked pair; either one of them, alone, is a valid removal; nothing else is.

**Sense.** Both named points are the two that share an x.

**Check.** The remaining three points' first numbers, 1, −3, 2 (or 1, −3, 2 with the other), have no repeat.

**Rule out.** Every choice that names a point outside the stack dims together: "taking away a point that is not stacked leaves the stack."

**Line.** "Only the stacked points matter, and one of them is enough."

**Trap.** Learners think both stacked points must go, or they find the "pair" by matching heights (two dots at the same y) instead of the same x. The sweep finds the pair by x and shows one removal clearing it.

**Kit.** `sweep` with `hits`; `hide`/`show` on lettered dots; `dim`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model: one picture, one motion, four short beats.

### `function-pair-select` — Which pairs can be added?

**Standard.** 8.F.A.1, "exactly one output," and "the set of ordered pairs."

**Foundations first.** One line to the definition. The new idea: every dot on the graph has *taken* its input. Drop a dashed shadow from each dot to the x axis and leave a blue mark there: those x values are used. A new pair can be added only if it does not land on a used mark with a different height; if it does, it stacks.

**Read.** "The graph shows a relation that is a function. Select the 3 ordered pairs that can be added so that it is still a function." Gold on *still*: no new stacks. At a glance: a pair whose x is not among the used marks can always be added; a pair whose x is used must be checked against that dot's y. Exactly three will pass.

**Example.** Graph: (2, 1), (4, 5), (5, 2), (8, 3). Choices: (8, 0), (7, 6), (3, 2), (6, 6), (2, 4).
1. Shadows fall; blue marks at 2, 4, 5, 8: the used inputs.
2. (8, 0): 8 is used, and the graph's point there is (8, 3). A different output for the same input; the dot drops and stacks under (8, 3). Cannot be added.
3. (7, 6): 7 is free. The dot lands alone. Can.
4. (3, 2): 3 is free. Can. (The output 2 already belongs to (5, 2); said aloud: a shared output is fine.)
5. (6, 6): 6 is free. Can.
6. (2, 4): 2 is used by (2, 1); stacks. Cannot.
7. Three chosen, matching the count.

**Fast.** Write the used x values from the graph; a candidate with a new x passes; a candidate with a used x and a different y fails.

**Sense.** Three pass and the question asked for three.

**Check.** Plot the three chosen pairs onto the graph and sweep: no vertical line touches two dots.

**Rule out.** Candidates whose x is already marked dim together: "that input is taken."

**Line.** "Every dot takes its input. A new pair needs a free input."

**Trap.** Learners check the y values ("2 is already used, so (3, 2) can't go") because "no repeats" is half-remembered. The shadows fall only to the x axis, and only those marks are ever compared.

**Kit.** `shadow` to x for a set of points leaving marks; a candidate dot that drops to show a stack; `dim` on select-many pills.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

### `function-missing-input` — Which k breaks it, or keeps it?

**Standard.** 8.F.A.1, "exactly one output."

**Foundations first.** One line to the definition. The table with the input column blue and the output column rust; one input cell holds k, gold-outlined. The lesson says what k is: the input of a pair whose output we already know. The question then runs two ways, and the direction word decides everything.

**Read.** "...has an unknown input k. Which value of k means the relation is *not* a function?" or "...*makes* the relation a function?" The direction word goes gold. At a glance: list the inputs already used. For "not a function," k must be one of them (then that input has two outputs, since k's output is different from theirs). For "makes it a function," k must be a value not on the list. Either way the output beside k is read once and otherwise ignored.

**Example.** {(−3, 5), (5, 5), (k, 6), (−1, 1)}; which k means not a function; choices −3, 3, −2, −6.
1. Used inputs, lit in blue: −3, 5, −1. (Outputs 5 and 5 repeat; one beat: two inputs sharing an output is fine, and this relation is a function as it stands.)
2. k's pair gives 6. To break the function, k must land on an input that already has a different output: −3, 5 or −1.
3. The choices: 3 is not used, −2 is not used, −6 is not used; −3 is. They dim together: "a new input never breaks a function."
4. Try k = −3: the table holds (−3, 5) and (−3, 6); plotted, two dots stacked at x = −3; the sweep hits both. k = −3.
Quick second case, the other direction (four beats): {(k, 3), (0, 0), (5, −1), (−3, −1), (−5, 4)}, which k *makes* it a function; choices 0, 5, 6, −5. Used inputs 0, 5, −3, −5; three of the choices are used and dim ("each would stack"); 6 is new. k = 6.

**Fast.** List the used inputs; "not a function" wants a used one; "function" wants a new one.

**Sense.** The chosen k matches the direction word: used for "not," new for "makes."

**Check.** Rewrite the list with the chosen k and scan the first numbers; the stack is there (or is not) as asked.

**Rule out.** For "not a function," unused choices dim together; for "function," used choices dim together.

**Line.** "Only a used input can break it."

**Trap.** The direction word is missed (a used input chosen when the question asked what keeps it a function); and the number 5, which is an output in (−3, 5) and an input in (5, 5), gets read in the wrong column. The gold direction word and the two column colors make both visible; only the blue column is ever scanned.

**Kit.** `table` with `inks`, a gold `?`-style cell for k filled on the word, `link` to a plane, `sweep`, `dim`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

## Grade 8: qualitative-graphs

### `story-graph-match` — Match the story to the graph

**Standard.** 8.F.B.5, "Describe qualitatively the functional relationship between two quantities by analyzing a graph (e.g., where the function is increasing or decreasing)." The sketching clause is not practiced.

**Foundations first.** A mixed type: three kinds under one name (graph to story, story to shape words, which part changes fastest). The one idea true for all of them is built first, slowly, because the misconception is strong: *a graph is read left to right like a story; the height is the quantity; the graph is not a picture of the path.* Build it with Student A. Across the top of the card a straight lane: home at the left end, the street running right, Student A a dot at home. Below, axes with no numbers, labeled "time" across and "distance from home" up. Student A walks away: the dot moves right along the lane, and the graph rises, its height always equal to her distance (a dashed tie between the dot and the curve's pen). She stops to rest: the lane dot is still, the graph runs flat. She walks back toward home: the lane dot comes left, the graph falls. Then the words on the pieces: *increasing* (up), *constant* (flat), *decreasing* (down). Said once, plainly: the line going down does not mean downhill; it means closer to home. A steeper piece means the distance changes more in the same time: faster.

**Read.** Read the whole story or the whole graph before choosing anything. For a graph, name the pieces in order; for a story, turn each act into a piece. For "most quickly," the question says each part covers the same time, which is what makes steepness fair to compare. Gold on *from left to right*, on the quantity's name, on *most quickly*.

**Examples**, one per kind.
Graph to story (main): a distance graph in three pieces: down, flat, up. The dot glides along it with a readout of distance ticking down, holding, then up; "decreasing" appears beside the first piece as the dot passes, "constant," "increasing." Decreasing is walking toward home; constant is resting; increasing is walking away. Choices: four Student A stories made of the same three acts in different orders. The two that begin with "stops to rest" dim together: the graph begins by falling. "Walks back, rests, walks back" dims: the last piece rises. "Walks back toward home, then stops to rest, then walks away from home."
Story to shape words (quick): "A drone hovers, then climbs, then hovers," graph of height. Hover: height stays the same: constant. Climb: increasing. Hover: constant. The same reading, run the other way, on a quantity that is not Student A's: the lesson says so. Constant, then increasing, then constant.
Fastest change (quick): water in a tub, O to A to B to C to D, each part the same time. Equal-width brackets along the time axis; the rise of each part drawn as a dim beside it; the rise of A to B is the tallest over the same width, so it is the steepest: most water per same minute. From A to B. One beat: a falling piece can be the fastest too; steep is what counts, not up.

**Fast.** Name the pieces in order, left to right; match piece for piece. For speed, compare steepness over equal time.

**Sense.** The chosen story's acts, said in order, draw the same shape in the air: down, flat, up.

**Check.** Glide the dot along the graph again while reading the chosen story; each act lands on its piece.

**Rule out.** Choices whose first act does not match the first piece dim together; then the next piece.

**Line.** "Up is away, down is toward home, flat is standing still. Read it left to right, like a story."

**Trap.** The graph read as a map of the walk: a falling piece means "walking downhill" or "going somewhere down," a rising piece "uphill." The lane above the graph, with Student A's actual position tied to the curve's height, makes the height the distance and nothing else. For "most quickly," the longest or highest piece is chosen instead of the steepest; the equal brackets and the drawn rises kill it.

**Kit.** `curve` polyline through lettered points with `numbers: false`; `glide` along a curve with a `@y` readout and `trend` words appearing per segment; a second figure strip (the lane) with a dot moved in sync; equal-span brackets on the time axis; `dim` on sentence choices.

**Writer.** Strongest: a mixed type that lives on the one example that exposes the path misconception.

### `distance-graph-meaning` — From A to B, how does the distance change?

**Standard.** 8.F.B.5, "where the function is increasing or decreasing" (and constant).

**Foundations first.** The story-graph lesson's one idea, in one beat with its picture: height is the distance from home; left to right is time; rising means farther, falling means closer, flat means the same distance. A short lesson.

**Read.** "x: minutes; y: meters from home. From A to B, how does y change?" Gold on *from A to B*: look only at that piece, not the whole graph. At a glance: the piece between A and B is level, so the distance did not change.

**Example.** Graph with several pieces, A and B at the two ends of a flat piece. Choices: "The traveler must be standing still." "Distance from home decreases." "Distance from home stays constant."
1. The dot glides from A to B; the readout of meters from home holds the same number the whole way. The distance from home stays constant.
2. "Decreases" dims: the piece does not fall.
3. "Must be standing still" dims, with the reason said plainly: the graph records one thing, how far from home. Standing still keeps that the same, but so does walking around the block at the same distance from home. The graph cannot tell those apart, so "must be" claims more than the graph knows.
4. Distance from home stays constant.

**Fast.** Look at the piece named; rising, falling or level; say what the distance did.

**Sense.** A and B are at the same height, so the same distance from home.

**Check.** Read A's height and B's height off the y axis; they match.

**Rule out.** A direction the piece does not show dims; a sentence about what the traveler is doing, rather than what the distance did, dims.

**Line.** "The graph tells what the distance did, not what the traveler did."

**Trap.** Over-reading: a flat piece becomes "stopped," a rising piece "walking uphill." The readout holding steady while the voice names two different ways to keep a distance constant separates the graph from the walk.

**Kit.** As `story-graph-match`; nothing new.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, once `story-graph-match` exists as the reference and with the "must be" reason written here.

## Algebra 1: function-domain-range

### `graph-domain-range` — Read the domain or the range from a graph

**Standard.** HSF.IF.B.5, "Relate the domain of a function to its graph"; HSF.IF.A.1 for the words *domain* and *range* ("from one set (called the domain) to another set (called the range)").

**Foundations first.** Define both with the picture, not with set words. *The domain is every input the function uses: every x where the graph has a point. The range is every output: every y the graph reaches.* Build the shadows on a plain curve: dashed lines fall from its leftmost and rightmost points to the x axis, and a blue band grows between them along the x axis: the domain is the graph's shadow on the x axis. Dashed lines run from its lowest and highest points to the y axis, and a rust band grows: the range is the shadow on the y axis. Then the ends: "the graph shows the whole function" means it stops where the dots are; a filled dot is an end that is included, written ≤; a hollow dot is an end that is not, written <. Shown once on the band: a filled band end for the filled dot, a hollow band end for the hollow one, the same drawing as rounding's catchment.

**Read.** "The graph shows the whole function f. What is the domain (or range) of f?" The gold word is *domain* or *range*: domain is x, across, blue; range is y, up, rust. The answer is an inequality in the right letter. At a glance: for the domain, the leftmost and rightmost x of the graph, filled or hollow; for the range, the lowest and highest point anywhere, including a peak or a dip in the middle.

**Example A, domain.** Graph: filled dot at (−1, 1), down through a dip near (0.5, −1), up to a filled dot at (2, 3). Choices: −3 ≤ x ≤ −1, −1 ≤ x ≤ 2, −1 < x < 2, −1 < x ≤ 2.
1. Gold on *domain*: x values, the x axis.
2. The left end is at x = −1, filled; the right end at x = 2, filled. The shadows fall; the blue band grows from −1 to 2 with both ends filled.
3. Filled means ≤ on both sides: −1 ≤ x ≤ 2.
4. Rule out: −3 ≤ x ≤ −1 dims (the graph has no point left of −1); the two choices with < dim together ("those are for hollow dots"), and for one beat the left dot is shown hollow so the learner sees what −1 < x ≤ 2 would look like.
**Example B, range (a different case: the highest point is not at an end).** Graph: filled (−2, −5), rising to a peak at (0, 4), falling to filled (2, 3). Choices: −5 ≤ y ≤ 3, −5 ≤ y ≤ 4, −5 < y < 4, −2 ≤ y ≤ 2.
1. Gold on *range*: y values, the y axis.
2. The lowest point is the left end, y = −5. The highest is not an end: the peak at y = 4 is above the right end's 3. The shadows fall from the lowest and highest points; the rust band grows from −5 to 4, both ends filled.
3. −5 ≤ y ≤ 4.
4. Rule out: −2 ≤ y ≤ 2 dims ("those are the x values, the domain"); −5 ≤ y ≤ 3 dims ("the end heights only; the peak is higher"); −5 < y < 4 dims ("the dots are filled").

**Fast.** Domain: leftmost x to rightmost x. Range: lowest y to highest y anywhere on the curve. Filled is ≤, hollow is <. A hollow end's y still counts for the range if another point of the graph reaches that height.

**Sense.** Every point of the graph sits above the blue band and beside the rust band.

**Check.** Pick a point on the curve; its x is inside the domain's band and its y inside the range's.

**Rule out.** The other axis's numbers dim first ("wrong letter"); then the wrong end types; for a range, an answer stopping at an end height while a turn goes past it.

**Line.** "Domain is the shadow on the x axis; range is the shadow on the y axis."

**Trap.** Domain and range swapped, and the range read from the two ends only, missing a peak or dip. The two bands in the two colors, grown from the whole curve's shadow, make the first impossible; the shadow from the peak makes the second impossible.

**Kit.** `interval` parts on each axis with filled and hollow ends; `project` dropping dashed lines from a curve's extremes and growing the band; a curve with endpoint dots drawn filled or hollow as authored.

**Writer.** Strongest: the shadows are born here and every later domain lesson (families 15 to 18) points back.

### `context-domain-range` — Which inputs or outputs make sense?

**Standard.** HSF.IF.B.5, "Relate the domain of a function ... where applicable, to the quantitative relationship it describes. For example, ... the positive integers would be an appropriate domain." HSF.IF.A.1 for *range*.

**Foundations first.** The shadows from the graph lesson, in one line. New: in a situation the graph is drawn only where the situation makes sense, and two questions decide its shape. *What does the input count or measure?* A count of whole things (cars, passengers) gives separate dots, because there is no half a car. A smooth amount (time, length, liters) gives a line, because every moment happens. *Where does it stop?* At a stated most ("at most 13 cars"), or at the moment something runs out (the length or the water reaches 0; set the rule equal to 0 and solve). Then domain and range are the shadows of that graph, as before: dots cast separate marks, a line casts a band.

**Read.** The gold words: the quantity the input measures, the stopping condition ("at most," "until it burns out"), and *domain* or *range*. At a glance: a count means "whole numbers"; time means an inequality; a "most" or a run-out means an upper end; and any answer with the other quantity's numbers is gone.

**Example A, a count with a most (domain).** "A ferry takes at most 13 cars, and each car pays 13 dollars. T(c) = 13c. Which describes the domain of T?" Choices: All whole numbers; Whole numbers from 0 to 169; All real numbers from 0 to 13; Whole numbers from 0 to 13.
1. c counts cars: whole numbers; the graph is dots, no line between (no point at 2.5 cars). 2. At most 13: the dots run from c = 0 to c = 13, fourteen of them, and stop. 3. Shadows to the c axis: marks at 0, 1, 2, ..., 13. Domain: whole numbers from 0 to 13. 4. Rule out: "all whole numbers" dims (the ferry fills); "real numbers 0 to 13" dims (no half cars); "0 to 169" dims ("those are the dots' heights, the tolls: the range").
**Example B, a smooth amount that runs out (domain).** "A 672-liter tank drains at 21 liters per minute. W(t) = 672 − 21t ... until the tank is empty. Which describes the domain of W?" Choices: t ≥ 0; Whole numbers from 0 to 32; 0 ≤ t ≤ 32; 0 ≤ t ≤ 672.
1. t is time: smooth; the graph is a line, starting at (0, 672). 2. It stops when the tank is empty: 672 − 21t = 0, t = 32, on the board; the line ends at (32, 0). 3. The blue band on the t axis from 0 to 32, both ends filled. 0 ≤ t ≤ 32. 4. Rule out: t ≥ 0 dims (the draining ends); whole numbers dims (time runs smoothly); 0 to 672 dims ("the liters, not the minutes").

### `function-relation-test` — Which equation or list is (or is not) a function?

**Standard.** HSF.IF.A.1, "assigns to each element of the domain exactly one element of the range," and "The graph of f is the graph of the equation y = f(x)"; 8.F.A.1 for the list case.

**Foundations first.** The definition and the sweep from grade 8, in two beats, as review. New: an *equation* is a rule too, and the question is whether one x can give two y values. The sure test, said with a picture: pick an x, find every y that makes the equation true, and count. If y sits alone on one side and the other side is a recipe in x, the recipe gives one y for each x: a function, and the sweep passes its graph. If y is squared (or otherwise not alone), one x can give two y values, because 25 has two square roots: two points stacked, and the sweep hits twice.

**Read.** "Which equation does *not* define y as a function of x?" or "defines"; "Which relation is (not) a function of x?" The direction word goes gold. At a glance: y alone on the left means a function; a y² means two outputs; in a list, only a repeated first number matters.

**Example A, equations (main).** Choices: y = |x − 4|; y = −3x + 4; x² + y² = 25; y = x³ − 4. Which does not define y as a function of x?
1. Three choices have y alone with a recipe in x; their graphs (a V, a line, a cubic) are drawn teal, and the gold line sweeps each: one crossing everywhere. Functions. (One beat on the V: two inputs share an output, 4 − 1 and 4 + 1 both give 1; that is fine.)
2. x² + y² = 25: y is not alone. Try x = 0: y² = 25, so y = 5 or y = −5. One input, two outputs; the board shows both. The graph is a circle of radius 5; two points stacked at x = 0; the sweep hits twice. Not a function.
3. The three function equations dim together ("y alone, one recipe, one output"). x² + y² = 25.
**Example B, a list (quick, the grade-8 scan).** {(1, −2), (6, 2), (6, 1), (4, −3)} among three function lists: first numbers 1, 6, 6, 4; input 6 gives 2 and gives 1; stacked at x = 6. Not a function. Three beats.

**Fast.** Equations: y alone on one side, a recipe in x on the other, is a function; a y² (or y not alone with a square root waiting) gives two outputs: pick an x and show both. Lists: scan the first numbers.

**Sense.** The chosen equation's graph is one you cannot read like a function: a circle (or a sideways parabola, x = y² − 6, which the "defines" wording offers as a decoy and the lesson names in one beat).

**Check.** Substitute the one x and get two different y's that both make the equation true (0 + 25 = 25; 0 + 25 = 25 again).

**Rule out.** For "does not define": every equation with y alone dims together. For "defines": every equation with y² dims together. For lists: lists with no repeated first number dim.

**Line.** "One input, one output. Squaring y gives two."

**Trap.** y = x² − 6 is called "not a function" because the parabola gives the same output for two inputs (3 and −3 both give 3): the vertical idea turned sideways. The sweep on the parabola passes, and the lesson says again that shared outputs are fine. The reverse: x² + y² = 25 is called a function "because it is an equation"; the two stacked points at x = 0 end it.

**Kit.** `plot` of y = |x − 4|, lines and cubics (exist); a `circle` on axes (exists); a sideways parabola needs `plot` of x as a function of y (new, small); `sweep` with `hits` across non-function curves; `dim`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model once the sideways plot and the sweep exist; the test, the decoys and the trap are fully written here.
