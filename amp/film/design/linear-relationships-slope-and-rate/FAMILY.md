# Linear relationships: slope and rate, grade 8 to geometry: family design

Seven skills, 46 problem types, grade 8 through geometry. Planning sources: the catalog and the `facts.ts` samples, the exact text of every linked standard. This design covers the idea, the picture, the habits, the kit, the seam and the pilot. Per-type concepts use these foundations and pictures.

Nothing here is a rule list. It is one idea, one picture that grows from a table of differences into a shaded plane, and the handful of sure moves that turn every problem in the family into careful reading.

---

## 1. The idea underneath the whole family

**A steady rate draws a straight line.** When one quantity changes by the same amount every time the other goes up by one, the picture is a straight line; the slope is that per-one change; the intercept is where things stood at zero. Every one of the 46 types reads or writes one of four descriptions of one steady change (a story, a table, a graph, an equation), and the whole skill is knowing that all four say the same two numbers: **how much per one** (m) and **how much at zero** (b). A learner who can pull those two numbers out of any of the four forms, and push them back into any other, owns the family.

The foundations, in the order they are built. Each later lesson names the ones above it in a line and shows their picture; it never re-teaches them.

1. **"The same change for each one more" is a rate.** (Grades 6 to 7; owned by family 10, named here.) In a table whose inputs go up by one, subtract neighboring outputs: the same answer every time means a steady rate. When the inputs do not go up by one, the rate is the change in output divided by the change in input: the unit rate, "per one," the same idea as 7 hours for 4 lawns. This must be defined before the word slope is ever said, because slope is this number seen on a graph.

2. **A steady rate makes a straight line, and slope is the rate read off the graph.** (8.EE.B.5, 8.EE.B.6, 8.F.B.4.) Define slope: rise over run, the change in y for a change in x, on a right triangle whose legs follow the grid. Why any two points give the same slope: every slope triangle on one line is a scaled copy of every other (similar triangles, from 8.G, family 26), so the ratio of legs never changes. Sign is direction: a line that climbs left to right has positive slope, one that falls has negative, a flat line zero, an upright line no slope at all. This is the one proof the family carries at grade 8, and the app never asks for it, so the lesson must.

3. **The start is the value at zero.** (8.F.B.4.) Define the y-intercept, also called the initial value: the output when the input is 0; on the graph, the point where the line meets the y-axis; in a story, what was there before anything happened (the fee, the height when planted, the money before the first deposit). A proportional relationship (family 10) is a line that starts at 0: y = mx, and its unit rate is its slope (8.EE.B.5).

4. **y = mx + b says: start at b, then add m for every one of x.** (8.EE.B.6, 8.F.A.3.) Derived by walking: from the start, one step right and m up, again and again; after x steps the height is b plus m taken x times. Table, graph, equation and story are four pictures of that walk. A function is linear exactly when it can be written as "a number times x, plus a number"; x², 1/x and √x cannot; y = 9 can (m is 0, the walk is flat).

5. **A point on the line makes the equation true, so one point and the slope fix the whole line.** (8.F.B.4, HSF.LE.A.2, HSA.CED.A.2.) From slope and a point: walk back to x = 0 along the slope, which is the one line of algebra b = y − m·x. From two points: slope first, then walk from either one. Point-slope form y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) is "from this point, rise over run is m"; standard form Ax + By = C is the same line before anyone solved for y, so solve for y and read. The universal check for every equation-of-a-line type: put the given point in; both sides agree.

6. **Two lines meet where two plans give the same output.** (8.EE.C.8, HSA.CED.A.3, HSA.REI.C.6.) Two plans are two lines on one graph. The gap between them at the start closes by the difference in their rates every step, so they tie after (starting gap) ÷ (rate difference) steps; before the crossing one plan is lower, after it the other. Two facts about two unknown counts are two lines: a count equation (x + y = 42) and a value equation (6x + 3y = 186); their crossing is the answer.

7. **An inequality shades one side of its line.** (HSA.REI.D.12, HSA.CED.A.3.) The boundary is the line of the matching equation, drawn solid when the sign includes it (≤, ≥) and dashed when it does not (<, >). One test point decides the side: if it makes the inequality true, shade its side; if not, the other. A system of inequalities is the overlap. In a story, a point must also make sense (whole tickets, hours that are not negative) before it is an answer.

8. **Parallel lines share a slope; a perpendicular slope is the slope triangle turned a quarter turn.** (HSG.GPE.B.5.) Turn the triangle 90° about its corner: the run becomes the rise and the rise becomes a run pointing the other way, so a slope of a/b becomes −b/a. Same slope and same intercept is the same line, not a parallel one. Again the app uses the criteria and never asks for the proof; the lesson gives it in one turn.

9. **Steady rates that act at the same time add (or subtract) per unit of time; times do not.** (HSA.CED.A.1, HSN.Q.A.1.) Two machines finish 1/3 and 1/6 of a job an hour, so together 1/2 an hour; a pipe fills 1/15 and a drain empties 1/21 of a pool an hour; a mixture's share of salt is the salt in both over the liquid in both; average speed is total distance over total time, never the average of two speeds. This foundation is a guest: it stands on family 10's unit rate and family 9's fraction bar, not on the line, and section 6 says where its two types belong.

**What this family takes, and from whom.** Family 10: the unit rate, "for every," proportional relationships, the double number line, tables of equivalent ratios. Family 13: the coordinate plane, a rule that turns each input into one output, a table and a graph as the same rule seen twice, function notation. Family 11: solving for b, setting two expressions equal, one-variable inequalities. Family 3: subtracting signed numbers in the slope formula, negative slopes. Family 9: slopes as fractions, reducing, reciprocals. Family 8: the bar with a fixed part and equal parts, for fee-plus-rate stories. Family 12: distributing m(x − x₁). Family 26: similar triangles, and the quarter turn.

**What it gives.** Family 15 (where two graphs meet), 16 (linear growth against exponential), 33 (the line through a cloud), 27 (slope proofs on the plane), 31 (vectors as runs and rises).

## 2. The visual language: the slope triangle, the table that lights the graph, and the bar that grows into a line

One picture system for all seven skills. A geometry student turning a slope triangle to prove the perpendicular rule must recognize the same run and rise they counted on a grid in grade 8, and an eighth grader reading a savings plan must see the bar they drew in grade 3 standing up and becoming a graph. Everything is drawn to scale on a grid the learner could copy onto scratch paper.

### The plane and the line

- **Axes** with every grid step ticked and the numbers haloed; the step named aloud when it is not one ("each line is five dollars"). Equal units on both axes whenever the problem says so (slope-triangle-statements says exactly that); when a story has months on one axis and dollars on the other, each axis is labeled with its quantity and unit and the lesson says the scale before anything is read. The view is chosen so the given points, the y-axis crossing and the slope triangle fit with room for labels; nothing is read off a crossing that is off screen.
- **The line** under study is teal; a second function or plan is violet. A line is drawn across the whole view; a plan is drawn from its story's start (month 0, or the third hour a fee covers) and the lesson says so once: "The line goes on both ways; the story starts here." A boundary that is not included is dashed.
- **Points** are dots labeled with their coordinates, the label placed away from the line and the axis numbers. The one point in use right now (the test point, the crossing, the point being plugged in) is gold.

### The two numbers wear two colors, everywhere

- **Slope is rust over blue.** On the graph, the rise is a rust leg and the run a blue leg. On the board, m is written as a fraction with a rust numerator and a blue denominator, or in rust alone when it is a whole number; in a story equation the number multiplied by the variable is rust (with its sign). In a table the output differences are rust arcs and the input differences blue arcs.
- **The start is gold.** The dot where the line meets the y-axis, the x = 0 row of the table, the lone number in the equation, the fee part of the bar: all gold. Gold is also the asked word in the problem card, as in every family.
- **Muted** is the grid, dimmed choices, a dashed boundary, and anything ruled out. No other colors; no tinted boxes behind answers.

Because the same two colors mean the same two things in every form, a learner who has seen three lessons reads the fourth form by color before the voice finishes.

### The slope triangle

Built the same way every time, from the **left** point: a blue run straight right along the grid to the x of the right point, then a rust rise straight up or down to the right point, with a right-angle mark at the corner. Each leg is labeled with its signed length (run "+4", rise "−2"); going right the run is always positive, so the rise alone carries the sign and the learner reads "down, so negative" from the picture. The fraction stands beside the triangle in the same two colors: m = −2/4 = −1/2. Both legs are to scale on the grid; a triangle is never a schematic.

The **unit triangle**, run exactly 1, shows the slope as a plain rise: it is the table's "per one" standing on the graph. The **step** is this triangle copied along the line.

### The table

A figure part beside the graph: a header naming the quantities (x and y, or months and dollars), inputs down the left, outputs down the right. Blue arcs in the left margin show the input steps ("+1", or "+2" when the table skips), rust arcs in the right margin show the output changes. The x = 0 row has a gold band. When a row lights, its point lights on the graph (the row-to-point link), so the learner watches the table become the line. Two plans are two output columns, teal and violet, with the tie row gold.

### The bar, and how it stands up

For a story with a fixed amount and a per-unit amount (a 28-dollar plan plus 5 a month), the first picture is the family-8 bar: a gold part labeled with the start, then equal teal parts each labeled "+5" in rust. Bars for months 0, 1, 2, 3 are drawn as vertical columns side by side on the axes, each one gold at the base and one more teal block tall than the last; their tops lie on a line, and the line is drawn through them. The gold base is b; each added block is m. This is the moment the family gives an old picture a new place, and the lesson says it once: "Every month adds the same block, so the tops line up. That line is the plan."

For foundation 9 the bar is the job: a bar of 1 cut into equal parts, filled each hour by each worker in its color, so "rates add" is seen as two fills landing side by side in the same bar; for mixing, two bars whose lengths are the volumes and whose shaded fractions are the strengths are laid end to end and the shaded share of the whole is read.

### The shaded side

A half-plane is the line's light fill on one side, out to the edge of the view, with the boundary solid or dashed by the sign. The test point is a gold dot; its check is written on the board the moment it is plugged in. A system shows both fills, and their overlap is the only place both fills meet; the chosen point in a feasibility problem lights there.

### The motions, and what each one proves

- **Step** (foundations 1, 2, 4): from the gold start, a unit triangle: blue run of 1, rust rise of m, a point. Again. Again. The points fall on a straight line, which is then drawn. Proves that a constant difference in the table is a straight line on the graph, and that y = mx + b is "start at b, add m each step."
- **Any triangle** (foundation 2, 8.EE.B.6): a small slope triangle and a large one on the same line; the small one dilates onto the large one and fits; both fractions reduce to the same value. Proves the slope is the same between any two points.
- **Tilt** (sign): a line pivots about its gold intercept from climbing to flat to falling while the slope readout runs from positive through zero to negative. Proves sign is direction and size is steepness.
- **Shift** (foundation 3; parallel): a line slides straight up or down; the intercept readout changes, the slope readout does not. Proves parallel lines share a slope and only the start differs (and that g(x) = f(x) + 4 is the same line moved up 4).
- **Walk back** (foundation 5): from the given point, unit (or whole) slope triangles step left until x = 0; the height there is b. For slope −3/5 through (15, 4): three triangles back, each run −5 and rise +3, land at (0, 13). Proves why b = y − m·x and kills "b is the y of the point."
- **Light** (foundation 4): a table row and its point light together; the whole table becomes the whole line. Proves the two forms are one rule.
- **Grow** (foundations 3, 4): the bars standing up and their tops joined by a line, as above.
- **Cross** (foundation 6): two lines drawn from their starts; a gold vertical line drops at the crossing to the axis; the table's tie row lights. The gap between the lines is bracketed at the start and seen to close by the same amount each step. Proves "same at the crossing" and "gap ÷ rate difference."
- **Test** (foundation 7): a gold dot drops onto the plane, the inequality is evaluated beside it, and the fill floods its side (true) or the other side (false). Proves that one point decides the side.
- **Quarter turn** (foundation 8): the slope triangle lifts and turns 90° about its corner; the blue run now stands as a rise and the rust rise lies as a run the other way; the new fraction is written from the turned legs. Proves the negative reciprocal.
- **Fill together** (foundation 9): each hour both workers' colors land in the job bar; the lesson counts hours until the bar is full.

### Column arithmetic and the balance

Solving for b, setting two plans equal, and clearing fractions in point-slope form use the board's margin notes exactly as family 11 does (`−5`, `÷ 15`); the balance itself is not drawn at these grades. Where a story's totals need multi-digit arithmetic (48 × 269), the `stack` is used, never a row of small equations.

## 3. How lessons in this family should go

### The habits every film builds

The same five moves as place value, in this family's words.

1. **Read.** The problem exactly as the app shows it, with its choices. The asked word goes gold: slope, y-intercept, "what does 17 mean," "after how many months," "which point," "parallel." One beat says what the answer must look like before any work: a fraction that is negative because the line falls; an equation whose slope is −3/5 (so half the choices are already gone); a whole number of months; a point inside the shaded part.
2. **At a glance.** What is known with no work: the sign of the slope from whether y goes up as x goes right; b straight from the x = 0 row or the axis crossing; which plan starts higher and which climbs faster; whether the boundary is dashed.
3. **The sure fast way.** The shortest path that always works, on the picture. Shortcuts that sometimes fail are never taught (section below).
4. **Sense.** Before boxing: does the size and sign fit the picture and the story? A falling line has a negative slope; walking back downhill from (12, −6) gives a b below −6; the cheaper plan at the answer month really is cheaper; a share of salt is between 21% and 33%.
5. **Check.** After boxing: put the point in and read both sides; compute the slope from the other pair of points; evaluate both plans at the answer and at the input before it; plug the point into the inequality; compare the ratio of the other two legs.

For multiple choice, the decoys are the misconceptions. They dim together, in one beat, with the one reason said aloud, and then the chosen answer is proved.

### What the learner sketches

A learner who has watched this family should draw, on scratch paper, one of five things:

- **Slope from points:** the two points written one above the other, two arrows down between them labeled with the differences, rise over run; or a quick L with the two legs labeled.
- **Equation of a line:** y = m x + b with the two slots, the point written underneath and plugged in; or the point, and arrows walking back to the axis.
- **Two plans:** a three-column table (step, A, B) run until the tie; or two lines with the crossing marked.
- **Counts and totals:** two labeled lines, "count:" and "money:", each with its equation.
- **Inequality:** the line, a dot at the origin with a check or a cross, shading on the winning side.

### Reading each shape of problem, the sure way, and the check

**Slope from two points, a graph or a table** (`line-slope`, `graphed-line-slope`, `unequal-step-rate`, `inline-linear-features`, the slope half of `staar-line-from-points`). At a glance: put the smaller x first; if y goes up, positive; down, negative. Sure way: subtract the second point from the first in both columns, in the same order, rise over run; on a grid, pick two points where the line crosses grid corners, run right, read the rise with its sign; in a table, take any two rows and divide the output change by the input change, because the inputs may skip ((1, 5), (3, 9) is 4 over 2, which is 2 per one). Check: the other pair of points, or the other order, gives the same fraction; step from one point by the slope and land on the other. Sense: sign matches the picture.

**The two numbers from any form, and what they mean** (`recover-linear-intercept`, `predict-linear-output`, `linear-initial-value-meaning`, `interpret-linear-parameters`, `wage-and-cost-blanks`, `mcas-slope-sign-description`, `mcas-table-intercept-select`). Read: "the number multiplied by the variable is per; the number alone is at zero," with units said aloud ("two centimeters each hour"). b from a table is the y in the x = 0 row (the mcas tables always have one; the lesson also shows what to do if they did not: step back one row). Sure way for a new output: step from a point you know: output at 7 = output at 3 + m × (7 − 3); b is the same move to x = 0. Sign from words: input and output moving the same direction is positive, opposite directions negative, output not moving at all is zero; "it depends on the starting value" is nonsense, because the start is b, not m. Net pay after buying a uniform starts below zero: the intercept is negative, and the cost is its opposite. Check: plug the point into the finished rule.

**Writing the line** (`staar-line-from-points`, `sat-fn-line-equation-choice`, `line-from-point-steps`, `line-equation-forms`, `point-slope-two-points`, `table-standard-form`, `graph-equation-choice`, `graph-of-linear-rule`, `graph-linear-equation`, `linear-model-equation`, `fee-rule-choice`). Read: which form is asked (slope-intercept, point-slope, standard) and what is given. At a glance: if the given point has x = 0, its y is b and the problem is over; if the slope is given, every choice with another slope dims at once; if a graph is given, b is read off the axis before any triangle is drawn. Sure way: m first, then b by walking back (b = y − m·x), then write; for point-slope, copy m and write (x − x₁)(y − y₁) with the signs of the point flipped, so (−2, 3) becomes (x + 2) and (y − 3); for standard form, solve for y, or from y = mx + b clear the fraction and move the x term over. For graphing from an equation, choose x values that make y whole (the intercepts when they are, otherwise step by the denominator); a proportional relationship always has (0, 0). "A flat fee that covers the first 3 hours plus a charge per extra hour" is point-slope in disguise: C = 30 + 5(h − 3), and the lesson says so, because the decoy 5h + 30 charges for hours already covered. Check: the given point makes both sides equal (in point-slope form both sides are zero); for a choice of graphs, the two named points give the stated slope. Rule out, in this order: wrong sign, wrong slope, fails the point.

**Linear or not, and comparing two functions** (`linear-function-choice`, `compare-function-rates`, `mcas-table-intercept-select`). Linear: can it be written as a number times x plus a number? y = 9 can; 12/x and x² cannot. Comparing: pull m and b out of each form first, write them in a two-row table (function, rate, start), then read the statement off it; a graph's slope is read from a triangle, never from how steep it looks, because the axes may not have equal scales. Rule out: statements that swap "rate" and "initial value" are the decoys; find the one word that differs between choices.

**Two plans** (`savings-plan-growth`, `first-cheaper-whole-input`, `linear-model-value`, `fee-plan-chain`, `system-equation-choice`'s plan form). Read which question it is: a balance after n steps (evaluate both, subtract), when equal (set equal), when one is strictly cheaper (inequality, then the first whole input past the crossing), a total over a period (fee × months plus charge × events for each, then subtract). Sure way for "when equal": the starting gap divided by the rate difference: 400 − 175 = 225, 80 − 65 = 15, 15 months; it is the equation 175 + 80x = 400 + 65x solved in one line, and the picture is the gap closing. For "first cheaper," solve the inequality, then name the first whole number past the crossing, never the crossing itself: 10 + 3x < 7x gives x > 2.5, so 3. Check: evaluate both plans at the answer and at the input before it (A 19 and B 21 at 3; A 16 and B 14 at 2); for a balance, the difference is the starting gap plus the rate gap times the steps (300 + 6 × (40 − 60) = 180).

**Counts and totals** (`sat-fn-constraint-choice`, `total-and-cost-equation`, `system-equation-choice`, `system-model-value`, `count-relation-chain`). Read: each kind contributes price × count; the count equation uses the count total and the value equation the money total, and the big number goes with the prices. Units rule out most decoys at once: dollars per candle times candles is dollars, so 14x + 28y = 730 and not x/14 + y/28; swapped prices (28x + 14y) are the realistic decoy and dim only after the prices are matched to their letters. "Full with 3 small or 7 large" means a small crate is a third of the truck and a large a seventh: x/3 + y/7 = 1, so 7x + 3y = 21, checked by both pure loads. Sure way for a count-and-total story: pretend every item is the cheap kind, see how far short the total is, and swap one at a time: 23 questions all worth 2 is 46, the total is 61, 15 short, each swap to a 5 adds 3, so 5 fives and 18 twos; the equation 5n + 2(23 − n) = 61 is that move written down, and the lesson shows both are the same. Check: both facts hold (5 + 18 = 23, 25 + 36 = 61).

**Shaded regions and feasible points** (`half-plane-inequality`, `inequality-solution-point`, `constraint-feasible`). Read the boundary first: dashed means strict, so two of four choices dim; "at most" is ≤, "at least" is ≥. Sure way: test the origin (or any point not on the line): 2·0 + 2·0 < 8 is true, so the origin's side. For a point, plug it in; a point on a solid boundary counts, a point on a dashed one does not ((3, 0) satisfies 4x + 3y ≤ 12 exactly; (1, 2) fails 4x + 2y > 8 exactly). For feasibility, test the simpler limit first (x + y ≤ 8 kills (3, 7) and (2, 7) in one beat), then the other. Check: the chosen point sits inside the shading on the picture.

**Parallel and perpendicular** (`geo-line-pair-relationship`, `perpendicular-slopes`, `geo-line-point-slope-choice`, `parallel-line-intercept`, `line-relationship-equations`). Sure way: put every line in y = mx + b; same slope parallel, slopes multiplying to −1 perpendicular, same slope and same b the same line. Then walk back from the given point to find the new b. A point given on an axis is a gift: (−7, 0) with slope 2 is seven steps right of the axis, so b = 14; "the y-intercept of 3x − 3y = 12" is x = 0, y = −4, and that is b. Check: multiply the slopes; plug the point in.

**Rates acting together** (`rate-combination-model`, `sat-entry-rate-numeric`). Read what each thing does per hour, not how long it takes: 1/3 and 1/6 of a job, so 1/2 together, 2 hours; a drain is subtracted. Average speed is total distance over total time, and the round trip takes longer at 48 than at 50, so the answer is below 49 (the decoy). Mixing is salt over liquid for the whole: 0.84 + 2.64 over 12. Check: the combined rate times the answer time is one whole job; the mixture's strength lies between the two strengths.

### Shortcuts: which always work, which are never taught

Always work, so they are the method: subtract in the same order and read sign from the picture; b = y − m·x; "the number with the letter is per, the number alone is at zero"; step from a point you know; gap ÷ rate difference; pretend all cheap, then swap; test the origin; flip the signs of the point in point-slope form; the given point makes both sides equal.

Sometimes fail, so they are never said: "greater than means shade above" (fails for 3 − y > x, and for upright lines; the test point never fails, and the lesson says that when y is alone with a plus sign, "above" is what the test gives); "the slope is the number in front of x" when the line is not solved for y (the geo decoy 2 for 2x − 6y = −18); "the intercept is the first row of the table" (only when the first row is x = 0; say "the row where x is zero"); "steeper on the picture means bigger slope" (axes may differ); "count the squares" without reading the axis step.

### The misconceptions, as children actually make them

Each is drawn from the app's own decoys or from what students write, and each is named in the lesson that targets it, never added to fill a slot.

- Run over rise: x change on top. The triangle is always built run first, then rise, and the fraction is written in the legs' colors.
- Mixed order: y₂ − y₁ over x₁ − x₂, which flips the sign. The at-a-glance sign check catches it every time.
- −4 − (−2) = −6. Signed subtraction is family 3's; the lesson writes the step out once.
- b is the y of the given point (the (15, 4) → "+4" decoy). The walk back shows b is the y at x = 0, thirteen here.
- "At one hour" for "at zero" (the candle "17 cm one hour after" decoy), and m and b swapped ("lost 17 each hour"). "Per" versus "at zero," with the h = 0 check.
- "Decreases" read as "negative slope" regardless of what the input does. Same direction, positive.
- The output change taken as the rate when the inputs skip ((1, 5), (3, 9) → 4). Divide by the input step.
- The crossing (2.5) given for "first whole input where A is cheaper" (3), or ≤ used for "strictly."
- Charging the rate on all hours when a fee covers the first three (5h + 30).
- The average of two speeds (49) for a round trip; times added for shared work (9 or 4.5 hours).
- y = 9 "is not linear"; y = 6/x "is linear because there is no square."
- The coefficient of x in Ax + By = C taken as the slope; the perpendicular slope negated but not flipped, or flipped but not negated.
- Swapped totals in a system (x + y = 186), swapped prices (28x + 14y, 3x + 7y = 21).
- Dashed and solid ignored; "greater means above" applied blindly.
- Two slope triangles on one line called congruent because they look alike; the slope's reciprocal (−2/3 for −3/2) read from the wrong legs.

### Lines to remember

Said once each, at the moment they apply, and put on the board or in the recap.

- "Same change for each one more: that is a steady rate, and it draws a straight line."
- "Run right, then read the rise. Up is plus, down is minus."
- "Any two points on the line give the same slope."
- "The number with the letter is per. The number alone is the start."
- "To find b, walk back to x equals zero."
- "A point on the line makes the equation true."
- "Same at the crossing. The gap closes by the difference in rates."
- "Count equation, money equation."
- "Pretend they are all the cheap kind, then swap."
- "Test a point. True: shade its side. Dashed means the line is not included."
- "Parallel: same slope. Perpendicular: turn the triangle."
- "Rates add. Times do not."

## 4. Tools to build

What exists: `axes`, `plot`, `dot`, `seg`, `label`, `angle` (with `right`), `glide`, `view`, `move`, `turn`, `slide`, `highlight`, readouts (`@x`, `@y`), the `table` part with `given` and cell highlights (from independence-test), the `bar` part with `partition` and `shade` (from percent-amounts-blanks), `vector`, `stack`, and the board with `problem`, `write`, `watch`, `recap`. The reference `line-slope` drew its slope triangle by hand from segments and labels, which is the proof the picture works and the reason it needs a component. `dim` is specified by the place-value design and not yet in the kit; twenty-six of this family's types are multiple choice.

### 4.1 `line`: a line by points, by slope and intercept, or by Ax + By = C

```json
{ "id": "L", "line": { "m": -0.5, "b": 2 }, "ink": "accent", "dash": false, "from": 0 }
{ "id": "L2", "line": ["A", "B"] }
{ "id": "L3", "line": { "A": -2, "B": -3, "C": 10 } }
```

- Drawn across the view, or from an x (`from`) when the story starts there. `dash: true` for an excluded boundary. Vertical lines allowed (`{ "x": -1 }`).
- Live readouts `@m` and `@b` on a label `of` the line, so an equation beside the graph rewrites itself during a tilt or shift.
- Actions: `{"do":"tilt","target":"L","m":0.75}` pivots about the intercept with the readout running; `{"do":"shift","target":"L","b":6}` slides it parallel; `{"do":"through","target":"L","points":["P","Q"]}` redefines it. Parts: `L.yint` (the gold intercept dot, shown on the word), `L.xint`.
- A point `{ "meet": ["L", "L2"] }` is the crossing, usable like `mid` and `foot`; with `foot` the gold drop to the axis is two lines of JSON.

### 4.2 `slopetri`: the slope triangle

```json
{ "id": "t", "slopetri": ["A", "B"], "labels": true, "frac": true }
```

- Built from the left point: blue run with its signed length under it, rust rise with its signed length beside it, right-angle mark; `frac: true` writes m = rise/run beside the triangle in the two colors, reduced on a second beat (`{"do":"reduce","target":"t"}`). `unit: true` makes the run-1 triangle at a point (`"at": "A"`). Legs are addressable (`t.run`, `t.rise`, `t.frac`) for `show`, `highlight`, and `draw` in order.
- `{"do":"step","target":"t","n":3,"dir":"left"}` copies the triangle along its line n times, dropping a dot at each landing; the walk back and the step are this one action. `{"do":"scale","target":"t","onto":"t2"}` dilates it onto a larger triangle on the same line for the similar-triangles beat. `turn` (existing) does the quarter turn; the labels ride the legs (the KAIZEN wish for labels that ride turned parts is needed here).

### 4.3 `table` additions

- `"deltas": { "col": 1, "ink": "warm" }` draws arcs in the margin between consecutive rows labeled with the differences; one per column, so inputs get blue arcs and outputs rust.
- `"zero": true` gives the x = 0 row a gold band; `{"do":"extend","target":"t","row":["0","?"]}` appends a row for a start the table lacks.
- `{"do":"link","table":"t.row.2","point":"P2"}` lights a row and its plotted point together; `points: "L"` on the table plots every row on a line part when the table is shown.
- Two output columns in two inks, with `{"do":"tie","target":"t"}` lighting the row where they match.

### 4.4 `region`: the shaded side

```json
{ "id": "r", "region": "L", "fill": "accent-fill" }
```

- Shades one side of a line part to the view's edge; the side is set by `"side": "above" | "below" | "left" | "right"` or by `{"do":"test","target":"r","point":"O"}`, which drops the gold dot, writes the check on the board (the kit evaluates the inequality from the line's equation and the sign given as `"sign": "<"`), and floods the true side. The boundary takes the line's `dash`. Two regions over one plane darken where they overlap; `r.overlap` is addressable for highlight.

### 4.5 `bar` additions, and the bars that stand up

- `"parts": [{ "label": "300", "w": 300, "ink": "gold" }, { "repeat": 6, "w": 40, "label": "+40", "ink": "accent" }]`: unequal labeled parts, to scale, for fee-plus-rate stories and for count-and-total stories (a bar of 22 tickets split into n and 22 − n with prices under each).
- `{"do":"stand","target":"b","on":"ax","at":[0,1,2,3]}`: the bar is redrawn as vertical columns at those inputs on the axes, each one block taller than the last, then `line` through their tops. The old picture becomes the new one on screen.
- Two bars aligned at the left for two plans at one input, with the difference bracketed.

### 4.6 `dim` for choices

As specified in the place-value design: `{"do":"dim","choices":[1,3]}` fades a set together in one beat. Needed by every multiple-choice type here.

### 4.7 Blanks in a sentence

`inline-linear-features`, `wage-and-cost-blanks` and `line-from-point-steps` show a sentence or a list of steps with boxes. The problem card needs `$\square$` blanks that fill on the word (`{"do":"fill","blank":1,"tex":"2"}`), so the film looks like the problem.

### Build order

1. `line` with `meet`, and `slopetri` with `step`: unlocks the slope skill (7), every equation-of-a-line type (11), and every graph-reading type.
2. `table` deltas, zero row, link and tie: the table appears in 9 types here and in families 13, 16 and 32.
3. `dim`: 26 types wait on it.
4. `region` with `test`: inequality-regions (2), constraint-feasible, the inequality case of linear-model-value, and family 11's half-planes.
5. `bar` parts and `stand`: the modeling stories (8 types) and family 8.
6. Blanks in the problem card, and labels that ride turned parts.

Each piece is built to the depth this family asks; family 13 (point plotting with callouts, piecewise) and family 33 (scatter and residuals) extend them.

## 5. Where the strongest writer is needed, and where a capable cheaper model can hold the bar

**Strongest writer (the concept is the lesson; reading or a proving motion carries it):**

- `rate-combination-model` (five kinds under one name, each a different act in the bar) and `sat-entry-rate-numeric` (three kinds).
- `fee-plan-chain` (three kinds), `savings-plan-growth` (three), `linear-model-value` (equation or inequality, and the whole-number reading), `staar-line-from-points` (three kinds), `line-equation-forms` (three forms from several givens), `table-standard-form` (a shift and a standard form), `graph-linear-equation` (proportional from words, or standard form to two whole points): mixed types whose one example per kind must be chosen to expose the trap.
- `slope-triangle-statements`: the similar-triangles proof as a motion, then five statements judged one by one.
- `compare-function-rates`: any two of graph, table, equation; the example must be the one where the steeper-looking graph has the smaller slope.
- `system-equation-choice` (words, a pair of tables, or a graph), `half-plane-inequality` (a region or a system; the first lesson to teach the test point).
- The birth lesson of the family, whichever type carries the step from table to line (`unequal-step-rate` in the pilot), because the foundation is the whole lesson.

**A capable cheaper model, from a written concept, once the kit above exists:** `line-slope`, `graphed-line-slope`, `recover-linear-intercept`, `predict-linear-output`, `sat-fn-line-equation-choice`, `line-from-point-steps`, `point-slope-two-points`, `inline-linear-features`, `wage-and-cost-blanks`, `linear-initial-value-meaning`, `interpret-linear-parameters`, `mcas-slope-sign-description`, `mcas-table-intercept-select`, `linear-function-choice`, `graph-equation-choice`, `graph-of-linear-rule`, `linear-model-equation`, `fee-rule-choice`, `first-cheaper-whole-input`, `sat-fn-constraint-choice`, `total-and-cost-equation`, `system-model-value`, `count-relation-chain`, `constraint-feasible`, `inequality-solution-point`, `geo-line-pair-relationship`, `perpendicular-slopes`, `geo-line-point-slope-choice`, `parallel-line-intercept`.

## 6. What looks misfiled, badly conceived, or out of grade, and what the standards ask that the problems never practice

### Misfiled or badly conceived

- `loan-total-cost` and `loan-compare-options` are payments × months (plus a fee) and a subtraction; their judged standards (7.EE.B.3, 4.OA.A.3, 5.NBT.B.5) say the same. They belong in family 21, as the curriculum plan already notes. If they stay, total = fee + payment × months is one reading of a line, drawn as the bar.
- `rate-combination-model` and `sat-entry-rate-numeric` are rate reasoning (HSA.CED.A.1, HSN.Q.A.1), not a relationship between two variables; no slope, no line, no intercept. They belong with family 10's grade-7 rates or an Algebra 1 rates skill. Their link to HSA.REI.A.2 is thin: 1/3 + 1/6 = 1/t is a rational equation, but "examples showing how extraneous solutions may arise" is never touched (family 17 owns it).
- `line-relationship-equations` sits in `linear-equation-modeling` (Algebra 1) but serves HSG.GPE.B.5 alone  and overlaps `perpendicular-slopes` and `geo-line-point-slope-choice`. Move it to `slope-proofs`.
- Four surfaces for "slope and a point to an equation" are spread across three skills: `staar-line-from-points` (slope), `sat-fn-line-equation-choice` and `line-from-point-steps` (linear-models), `line-equation-forms` (modeling). Fine as surfaces; file them together so the lessons can share the walk back.
- `mcas-slope-sign-description`'s positive variant has the battery's charge falling as the days used fall, which contradicts the story; the input-decreasing phrasing should be generated only with contexts where it makes sense, or the sign should come from a context that is naturally positive. A learner who reasons about the story is punished.
- `slope-triangle-statements`: in the facts output several choices are cut off mid-sentence ("The ratio is $", "The slope of line $\ell$ "). Confirm the template renders complete statements; a lesson cannot name a decoy it cannot read.
- `unequal-step-rate`, `predict-linear-output`, `recover-linear-intercept` lean on fractions like −29/4 and −17/2 in a grade-8 slope idea; the fraction arithmetic hides the idea. Whole-number variants should carry the type, with fractions as a stretch.
- `first-cheaper-whole-input` and `savings-plan-growth` (grade 8) are judged to serve HSA.REI.B.3 and HSA.CED.A.1; 8.EE.C.7 and 8.EE.C.8c cover them, so they are in grade, but the catalog's links overstate them.
- `inequality-solution-point` has no linked standard; it is HSA.REI.D.12.
- `half-plane-inequality`'s sample 2x + 2y < 8 is x + y < 4 with a common factor left in; harmless, but a learner who simplifies first should not be confused by choices that never do.

### Standards clauses the problems never practice (exact text)

- **8.EE.B.5**: "Compare two different proportional relationships represented in different ways. For example, compare a distance-time graph to a distance-time equation to determine which of two moving objects has greater speed." No type compares two proportional relationships; `compare-function-rates` compares lines with intercepts. "Interpreting the unit rate as the slope of the graph" appears only in `graph-linear-equation`'s proportional variant.
- **8.EE.B.6**: "derive the equation y = mx for a line through the origin and the equation y = mx + b for a line intercepting the vertical axis at b." No type asks for the derivation; the lesson carries it (the step motion). "Use similar triangles to explain why the slope m is the same" is touched only by `slope-triangle-statements`.
- **8.F.A.3**: "give examples of functions that are not linear. For example, the function A = s² ... contains the points (1,1), (2,4) and (3,9), which are not on a straight line." `linear-function-choice` judges equations only; no type asks whether a table or set of points is linear.
- **8.F.A.2**: "verbal descriptions" among the represented ways: `compare-function-rates` pairs graph, table and equation; a function given in words appears nowhere.
- **8.EE.C.8a and 8b**: "solutions ... correspond to points of intersection of their graphs"; "estimate solutions by graphing"; "Solve simple cases by inspection. For example, 3x + 2y = 5 and 3x + 2y = 6 have no solution." Within this family no system is solved from a graph and none has no solution or infinitely many (family 11's `linear-systems` may own these; the lesson for `savings-plan-growth` should at least show the crossing as the solution).
- **HSA.CED.A.2**: "graph equations on coordinate axes with labels and scales." Only `graph-linear-equation` has the learner place points; no type asks for labels or a scale.
- **HSA.CED.A.3**: "interpret solutions as viable or nonviable options in a modeling context." `constraint-feasible` picks a feasible point; no type asks whether a solution such as 2.5 tickets or −3 hours is viable.
- **HSA.REI.D.12**: "Graph the solutions to a linear inequality in two variables as a half-plane." Every type reads a region already drawn; none has the learner produce one (shade, or choose solid against dashed on an unshaded graph).
- **HSG.GPE.B.5**: "Prove the slope criteria for parallel and perpendicular lines." All four geometry types use the criteria; none asks for the proof or a step of it. The lesson gives the quarter turn.
- **HSN.Q.A.1**: "choose and interpret the scale and the origin in graphs and data displays." Not practiced.
- **7.EE.B.4b**: "Graph the solution set of the inequality and interpret it in the context." The inequality case of `linear-model-value` does not graph; family 11 owns it.
- **HSF.LE.A.2**'s exponential and geometric-sequence halves belong to family 16; here only the linear half is practiced, correctly.

## 7. The pilot: five problem types to build first

The pilot covers the whole family in one sitting: every foundation that has a motion, every new tool, grade 8 and Algebra 1, and every kind of question (open answer, multiple choice, select-three, a story).

| # | Problem type | Grade | Why it is in the pilot |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `unequal-step-rate` | 8 | The family is born here: a table with its difference arcs, the row that lights its point, the unit triangle, the step that draws the line. Its trap (the output change taken as the rate when inputs skip) is the realistic one. Exercises `table` deltas and link, `slopetri` with `unit` and `step`. |
| 2 | `staar-line-from-points` | 8 | Three kinds of one act (two points; slope and a point; the slope of Ax + By = C), the walk back to x = 0, and the decoy "b is the y of the point" dimmed with its reason. Exercises `line`, `slopetri.step` leftward, `dim`. The move it teaches recurs in eleven types. |
| 3 | `savings-plan-growth` | 8 | Two plans as bars that stand up into two lines, the gap closing by the rate difference, the gold crossing, the two-column table with its tie row; three kinds of question. Exercises `bar` parts and `stand`, two inks, `meet`, `table.tie`. The modeling heart of 8.F.B.4 and 8.EE.C.8. |
| 4 | `slope-triangle-statements` | 8 | The one proof at grade 8: the small triangle dilates onto the large one and the two fractions agree; then five statements judged, with the reciprocal and "congruent" decoys named. Exercises `slopetri.scale`, equal-unit axes, a select-three card. The strong-writer seam at its hardest. |
| 5 | `half-plane-inequality` | A1 | Dashed against solid rules out two choices in one beat; the gold test point floods a side; a system as the overlap. Exercises `region` with `test` and `dash`, and kills "greater means above." |

Visual references: `line-slope` for the slope triangle and the quarter-turn construction described under `line-relationship-equations`. If the five land, the next wave is `interpret-linear-parameters` (per and at zero, with units), `system-model-value` (pretend all cheap, then swap), `fee-plan-chain` (point-slope in disguise) and `geo-line-pair-relationship` (standard form solved for y, the same line against a parallel one), which complete the habits and the tool set before batch production.
