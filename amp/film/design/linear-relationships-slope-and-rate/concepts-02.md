# Linear models (`linear-models`, grade 8): the ten concepts

Written from the family design. Three things to say before the concepts, because they shape them.

- **`loan-total-cost` and `loan-compare-options` are misfiled.** No rate per one is interpreted, no line is read; the judged standards (7.EE.B.3, 4.OA.A.3, 5.NBT.B.5) agree. They belong in family 21. The concepts below are written so they ship either way, on family 8's bar, and they borrow nothing from the line except the habit "the number alone is a start; the repeated number is per."
- **`savings-plan-growth` holds five kinds, not three** (balance after n, months to a goal, deposit needed, two plans after n, two plans tied). The design counted three. The concept below covers all five with two full examples and three quick ones; if the film runs past six minutes, split the type: single plan (three slots of one line) and two plans (gap and crossing).
- **Fractions in `recover-linear-intercept` and `predict-linear-output`** (−5/4, −29/4, 11/2) hide a grade-8 idea behind grade-5 arithmetic. The concepts carry the idea on whole numbers and show one fraction case quickly, because the app will ask it. Whole-number variants should be the common ones.

Every film in this skill opens the same way unless its concept says otherwise: the words *rate* (the same change for each one more), *start* (the value at zero), and the line they draw, in one picture lasting under twenty seconds.

#### `loan-total-cost` — Total repaid and finance charge.

**Standard.** Misfiled; truly 7.EE.B.3 ("solve multi-step real-life ... problems ... assess the reasonableness of answers using mental computation and estimation") with 5.NBT.B.5 for the product. No clause of 8.F.B.4 is practiced.

**Foundations first.** Define *borrow* (money you get now), *monthly payment* (the same amount paid back each month), *term* (how many payments; a year is 12), *total repaid* (every payment added up), *finance charge* (what you pay beyond what you borrowed: the cost of borrowing). Picture, built live: a bar of equal parts, one per payment, each the width of its amount, bracketed on top as the total; under it, aligned at the left, a shorter bar for the amount borrowed. The overhang is the finance charge. Said once: "You pay back the loan, and then some. The 'then some' is the charge."

**Read.** Two questions hide in one type: *how much in all* (the long bar) and *the finance charge* (the overhang). The asked phrase goes gold. At a glance: the total is more than the amount borrowed and near payment × count rounded (18 × 600 is 10,800); the charge is a small part of that, under a thousand here. "2 years" is 24 payments, "4 years" 48; the bar groups every 12 parts under a year bracket so the count is seen, not recalled.

**The example.** Student B borrows 10,000 dollars, repays 18 monthly payments of 605 dollars. What is the finance charge? (1) The bar: 18 parts of 605, to scale, the bracket "18 × 605." (2) The product on the `stack`: 18 × 605 = 10,890; the bracket reads 10,890. (3) The 10,000 bar slides in under it, left edges aligned; the overhang is bracketed: 10,890 − 10,000 = 890. (4) 890 dollars.

*Quick case, two loans of one amount* (Student I, 13,500 for 12 months at 1,174 or 36 months at 423): two long bars over one 13,500 bar; 14,088 and 15,228; the overhangs are 588 and 1,728; their difference is 1,140, and it is also the difference of the totals, because both bars sit on the same 13,500. Said once: "Same amount borrowed, so compare totals or compare charges; the gap is the same."

**Fast.** Count the payments (years × 12). Payment × count on the stack. Charge: total minus borrowed.

**Sense.** 18 × 600 = 10,800, so the total is a bit over 10,800 and the charge a bit under 1,000. A smaller payment stretched over more months usually costs more in total (Student I's 423 × 36 beats 1,174 × 12), and the bars show it.

**Check.** 10,000 + 890 = 10,890; 10,890 ÷ 18 = 605.

**Rule out.** For a charge: anything at or above the total (10,890, 20,890) dims, since the charge is only the overhang; anything far from the estimate (285) dims. For a total: anything below the amount borrowed (1,260) dims at once; "borrowed plus one payment" (27,785) is not a total. For two loans: the two single charges (588, 1,728) and the payment difference (751) are the decoys; the question asks for a difference of charges.

**Line.** "You pay back the loan, and then some. The 'then some' is the charge."

**Trap.** Answering the total repaid when the charge is asked (10,890): the aligned bars make the charge the overhang, never the whole bar. Counting years as payments (Student E: 2 × 271 = 542): the year brackets over twelve parts each.

**Kit.** `bar` `parts` with `repeat` and a year bracket every 12; two bars aligned at the left with the overhang bracketed; `stack` must gain `op: "×"` (standard algorithm with partial products), which the family design assumes and AUTHORING does not list.

**Writer.** Cheaper model: one method, one picture, the arithmetic machine-checked.

#### `loan-compare-options` — The cheaper of two offers, counting the fee.

**Standard.** Misfiled, as above; 7.EE.B.3 and 5.NBT.B.5 in substance.

**Foundations first.** The same bar, plus the *fee*: a gold part at the left end, paid once, before any payment. The lesson says why it is gold: it is the number alone, the start, the same role the fee plays in every plan of this family. Two offers are two bars aligned at the left; the shorter bar costs less; the overhang says by how much.

**Read.** Three questions under one name: which costs less overall and by how much, with a fee (gold "counting the fee"); the same without a fee, two interest rates; and a statement pairing *monthly payment* with *total cost*. At a glance for the first two: estimate both totals with rounded payments before any multiplying; for the statement: the lower payment is read straight off the problem, only the totals need work.

**The example.** Student D needs 10,500. Offer 1: 24 payments of 456. Offer 2: a 550 fee, then 72 payments of 169. (1) Estimate: 24 × 450 is about 10,800; 72 × 170 is about 12,200, and that is before the fee. Offer 2 costs more: both "Offer 2 costs less" choices dim. (2) Bars: Offer 1 as 24 parts; Offer 2 as a gold 550 then 72 parts; the second bar is plainly longer. (3) Stacks: 24 × 456 = 10,944; 72 × 169 = 12,168; the fee added: 12,718. (4) Overhang: 12,718 − 10,944 = 1,774. Offer 1 costs 1,774 less.

**Second example (a different case).** Student I compares two loans of 19,000: 60 payments of 376, or 72 payments of 289. Which statement is true? The learner who just learned "longer costs more" will guess wrong here, so this case must be worked. (1) Payment: 289 is less; the Offer 2 part is visibly narrower. (2) Totals: 60 × 376 = 22,560; 72 × 289 = 20,808; the Offer 2 bar is shorter even with more parts. (3) Offer 2 has both the lower payment and the lower total. Said once: "A smaller payment for longer can cost more or less. You cannot know until you multiply."

**Fast.** Total = fee + payment × count, for each; the smaller total wins; subtract.

**Sense.** The estimate before the stacks decides the direction; the exact work only sets the amount. 1,774 is near the estimated 12,200 + 550 − 10,800.

**Check.** Smaller total + difference = larger total (10,944 + 1,774 = 12,718). For the statement: 289 < 376 and 20,808 < 22,560, both read aloud.

**Rule out.** Direction first, from the estimate. Then: the two remaining amounts differ by exactly the fee (1,774 − 1,224 = 550), so one of them forgot it. A difference that equals the difference of the two payments (Student F's 61) is a monthly comparison, not a total.

**Line.** "Count the fee. Then it is payment times months."

**Trap.** Forgetting the fee (1,224): the gold part is drawn first and counted first. Judging by the payment (Student I): the second example exists to break it.

**Kit.** As `loan-total-cost`: `bar` parts with a gold fee part, two bars aligned, overhang bracket, `stack ×`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model from this concept; the second example is named and its reason given.

#### `savings-plan-growth` — Start, deposit, balance; two plans and their crossing.

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Construct a function to model a linear relationship ... Determine the rate of change and initial value ... from a description of a relationship"; 8.EE.C.8c: "Solve real-world ... problems leading to two linear equations in two variables"; 8.EE.C.7 for the tie equation.

**Foundations first.** Built in front of the learner on Student E's plan: *start* (what is in the account at month 0), *deposit* (the same amount added each month), *balance after n months*. The bar: gold 100, then teal blocks each "+30." Bars for months 0, 1, 2, 3 stand up as columns on axes (months across, dollars up, "each line is 50 dollars" said); their tops line up; the line is drawn through them. Said once: "Every month adds the same block, so the tops line up. That line is the plan." The equation beside it: balance = 100 + 30n, the 100 gold, the 30 rust. "The number with the letter is per month. The number alone is the start." Every question in this type asks for one of the three numbers in that sentence, or compares two such sentences.

**Read.** The asked quantity goes gold: *how much after*, *how many months*, *each deposit*, *which holds more and by how much*, *when the same*. At a glance: a balance is more than the start; months to a goal is the gap divided by the deposit, not the goal divided by it; the deposit needed is a rise over a run; for two plans, the one that starts higher leads until the other's faster deposits close the gap.

**Example A (the line read).** Student E starts with 100, deposits 30 at the end of each month; after 17 deposits? (1) The line from the foundation; the gold start at (0, 100). (2) Step 17 times: 17 blocks of 30 is 510. (3) 100 + 510 = 610; the point (17, 610) lights on the line. (4) 610 dollars.

*Quick, months to a goal:* Student F, 450 toward 670 at 20 a month. The gap, 220, bracketed between the start and a dashed goal line; 220 ÷ 20 = 11 steps; the line meets the goal at month 11. One beat: "If the division is not exact, round up: the balance is not there until the next deposit lands." *Quick, deposit needed:* Student H, 375 to exactly 735 in 18 months: rise 360 over run 18: 20 per month. The slope of the plan is the deposit.

**Example B (two plans tied).** Plan A: 175 start, 80 a month. Plan B: 400 start, 65 a month. After how many deposits equal? (1) Both bars stand up into two lines, teal and violet, gold starts at 175 and 400; the gap at month 0 is bracketed: 225. (2) Step both one month: A rises 80, B 65, the gap shrinks by 15; again, 195; again, 180. "The gap closes by the difference in deposits, every month." (3) 225 ÷ 15 = 15 months; the gold crossing drops to the axis at 15. (4) Written as the equation 175 + 80n = 400 + 65n, solved in one margin line: 15n = 225, n = 15. Both plans hold 1,375. (5) The two-column table beside the graph, its tie row gold.

*Quick, two plans after 6 months* (A 300 + 40n, B 60n): the gap starts at 300 and closes by 20 a month; after 6 months, 300 − 120 = 180; A by 180. Evaluated too: 540 against 360.

**Fast.** Balance: start + deposit × months. Months: (goal − start) ÷ deposit, round up. Deposit: (goal − start) ÷ months. Two plans: starting gap ÷ difference in deposits for the tie; starting gap − difference × months for the lead.

**Sense.** 610 is near 100 + 30 × 20 = 700, a little under. Fifteen months is plausible for a 225 gap closing 15 at a time. The lead after 6 months is less than the starting gap, since B is catching up.

**Check.** 610 − 100 = 510 = 30 × 17. Both plans at 15: 175 + 1,200 and 400 + 975 are 1,375. Goal: 450 + 20 × 11 = 670.

**Rule out.** A balance below the start dims; deposit × months alone (510) left out the start; (start + deposit) × months (2,210) is far too big. Goal ÷ deposit (34) ignores what is already saved. For two plans, the starting gap unchanged (300) says no one deposited; "Plan B by" when A still leads dims with the graph.

**Line.** "The number with the letter is per month. The number alone is the start." And for two plans: "Same at the crossing. The gap closes by the difference in deposits."

**Trap.** Forgetting the start (510) and multiplying start and deposit together by the months (2,210): the gold base is the same height in every column and only the blocks multiply. Dividing the goal instead of the gap (34): the bracket is drawn from the start, not from zero.

**Kit.** `bar` `parts` and `stand`; `line` from 0 with two inks; `meet` with the gold drop; a gap bracket between two lines at a given x that shrinks as the step repeats; `table` with two output columns and `tie`; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest: five kinds, a pilot type, and the stand-up and gap motions are the lesson.

#### `sat-fn-line-equation-choice` — The equation from a slope and a point.

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Determine the rate of change and initial value of the function from a description of a relationship or from two (x, y) values"; HSF.IF.A.2 only for the f(x) wording.

**Foundations first.** y = mx + b has two slots; the slope is given, so one slot is filled before any work. Define *y-intercept* as the start, the y where x is 0, the gold dot on the axis. The one move to learn: from the given point, walk back along the slope to x = 0 (`slopetri.step`), and b is the height there. Said once, as the move is drawn: "To find b, walk back to x equals zero."

**Read.** "Which equation" with m given: every choice whose slope is not m is gone before anything else (the reciprocal 5x, the sign flip). If the point has x = 0, its y is b and the problem is over in one beat. Otherwise the point's y is not b, and b is found by walking.

**The example.** A pool's level falls 4 centimeters each hour; after 6 hours it is 32 centimeters. Which equation gives the level y after x hours? (1) Falling means the slope is −4; y = 4x + 56 dims. (2) At a glance: falling, so the level at hour 0 was higher than 32; the two choices with b = 8 and b = 32 cannot be right. One remains, but prove it. (3) The point (6, 32) on axes labeled hours and centimeters; six unit triangles step left, each run −1, rise +4, landing at (0, 56), the gold dot. (4) b = 32 − (−4)(6) = 32 + 24 = 56. (5) y = −4x + 56. (6) Check: −4 × 6 + 56 = 32.

*Quick case, a point left of the axis:* slope −1 through (−4, 11). Walking to x = 0 goes right four steps, each rise −1: (0, 7). b = 11 − (−1)(−4) = 7; the decoy 15 added instead of subtracting. *One beat:* slope 1/3, y-intercept 1, or slope 2/5 through (0, −9): the point is on the axis; write b and only the slope can go wrong.

**Fast.** Write m. If x = 0, write b. Else b = y − m·x. Check with the point.

**Sense.** A falling line started higher than any later point; a rising one started lower. Walking left along a negative slope goes up.

**Check.** The given point makes both sides equal.

**Rule out.** Slope first, in one beat (wrong sign, reciprocal). Then b on the wrong side of the point's y for the slope's direction. Then plug the point into what remains.

**Line.** "To find b, walk back to x equals zero."

**Trap.** b is the y of the given point (y = −4x + 32): the walk shows the axis is six steps away and the height there is 56. Subtracting with the slope's sign dropped (b = 8): the triangles go up as they step left.

**Kit.** `line`, `slopetri` with `step` left and right, `L.yint`, `dim`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model: one method, the decoys named.

#### `inline-linear-features` — Slope, intercept and direction in a sentence.

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Determine the rate of change and initial value of the function ... from two (x, y) values, including reading these from a table or from a graph."

**Foundations first.** Define *slope* as rise over run on a triangle built from the left point: blue run right, rust rise up or down, the sign carried by the rise. Define *y-intercept* as the height at x = 0. Define *increases* and *decreases* as the direction the line goes reading left to right, which is the sign of the slope. Build one triangle live on a grid before the example.

**Read.** Three blanks in one sentence; the card shows the sentence with boxes, filled on the word. At a glance: put the smaller x first; y goes up or down from there, so the direction blank and the slope's sign are known before any subtraction. The facts output lists only answer letters; the writer must read the template for each blank's options.

**The example.** A line through (−4, 1) and (−2, −2). (1) Both points on a grid, (−4, 1) first. From left to right y drops from 1 to −2: decreasing, slope negative; the direction blank fills. (2) The triangle: run +2 (blue), rise −3 (rust); m = −3/2, written in the two colors; the slope blank fills. (3) One more run of 2 reaches x = 0 with rise −3: the gold dot at (0, −5); b = −5; the intercept blank fills. (4) The sentence read whole: slope −3/2, y-intercept −5, y decreases as x increases.

*Quick, from a graph* (A(−2, 0), B(−1, 2)): b is read off the axis crossing first, (0, 4); the triangle from A to B gives run 1, rise 2, m = 2. *Quick, from a table:* any two rows; divide the output change by the input change, since the table may skip; the row where x is 0 holds b.

**Fast.** Smaller x first. Run right, read the rise. Walk to x = 0 for b, or read the axis or the x = 0 row.

**Sense.** A falling line's intercept, reached by walking right from (−2, −2), is below −2. The two points of (−3, 3) and (3, 1) straddle the axis, so b sits between 3 and 1.

**Check.** Plug the other point into y = −3/2·x − 5: −3/2 × (−4) − 5 = 6 − 5 = 1.

**Rule out.** The direction blank is decided by the picture alone; a slope option with the wrong sign dims with it; a reciprocal (−2/3) has the legs upside down.

**Line.** "Run right, then read the rise. Up is plus, down is minus."

**Trap.** Run over rise, and mixed order (y₂ − y₁ over x₁ − x₂), which flips the sign: the at-a-glance direction catches a wrong sign every time, and the triangle is always built run first.

**Kit.** Blanks in the problem card that fill on the word; `slopetri` with `frac`; `table` deltas with uneven input arcs; `dim`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model once the blank card exists.

#### `linear-initial-value-meaning` — What the number alone means.

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Interpret the rate of change and initial value of a linear function in terms of the situation it models, and in terms of its graph or a table of values."

**Foundations first.** Define *initial value*: the output when the input is 0, and say what input 0 is in the story (the moment the candle is lit, the moment filling begins). Picture: the line from input 0 to the story's last input, the gold dot on the axis, and a table beside it with the input-0 row gold. Said once: "The number with the letter is per. The number alone is the start, wherever it is written."

**Read.** "What does 17 mean": the 17 has no letter, so it is the start. The four choices are four places on the picture: the start (correct), the per-unit change (the −2's job), the input-1 row, and the change over the whole period. At a glance: any statement that says "each hour" is about the slope and is gone.

**The example.** H = 17 − 2h, the first 4 hours of a candle. (1) Table h = 0 to 4: 17, 15, 13, 11, 9; the line through them, (0, 17) gold. (2) Set h to 0: the 2h term is nothing; H is 17. "When it was lit, it was 17 centimeters." (3) The decoys placed on the picture: "lost 17 each hour" is a slope, and a 17-centimeter candle losing 17 an hour is gone before the first hour ends, while the model runs four; "17 tall one hour after" is the h = 1 row, which reads 15; "lost 17 over 4 hours" is 17 − 9 = 8. All three dim. (4) The candle was 17 centimeters tall when it was lit.

*One beat:* C = 3d + 9: the 9 is written last and is still the start, "before any distance was traveled."

**Fast.** Set the input to 0; the letter term vanishes; say what is left, with units, at the moment input is 0.

**Sense.** The start must be plausible for the story: a candle 17 centimeters tall, a ride that costs 9 dollars before it moves.

**Check.** The h = 1 row is 15, not 17, so "one hour after" is wrong; the chosen statement matches the gold row.

**Rule out.** "Each unit" statements name the slope; "after one" statements name the second row; "over the first n" statements name a difference. Three gone in one beat, with the picture pointing at each.

**Line.** "The number with the letter is per. The number alone is the start."

**Trap.** Swapping per and start ("lost 17 each hour"), and "at one hour" for "at zero": the h = 0 check and the gold row make both visible.

**Kit.** `line` with `from: 0` to the story's end; `table` with `zero: true`; `dim`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

#### `recover-linear-intercept` — The output at input 0 from a rate and a point.

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Determine the rate of change and initial value of the function ... from two (x, y) values" (here one value and the rate).

**Foundations first.** Rate: the same change for each one more of the input. Start: the output at input 0. Given the rate and one point, the start is a walk away: from the point, step back one input at a time, changing the output by the rate each step, until the input is 0. Drawn once before the example.

**Read.** "Output when the input is 0" is b. At a glance: a positive rate and a point right of the axis means b is below the point's output; a negative rate means above.

**The example.** Rate 3 through (3, 14). (1) The point on axes; three unit triangles step left, each run −1, rise −3: 11, 8, 5; the gold dot at (0, 5). (2) The same in one line: b = 14 − 3 × 3 = 5. (3) The rule y = 3x + 5. (4) Output 5.

*Quick case, a negative fraction rate* (−5/4 through (6, −7/2)): walking left along a falling line goes up: six steps of +5/4 is 30/4 = 15/2; −7/2 + 15/2 = 4. The picture: the line climbs to the left of the point. Three of the app's variants carry fractions, so the arithmetic is shown once.

**Fast.** b = y − m·x.

**Sense.** Rising line, point to the right: b below 14. Falling line: b above the point. Rate 1/2 through (6, 3) walks down to 0: the line passes through the origin.

**Check.** 3 × 3 + 5 = 14.

**Line.** "To find b, walk back to x equals zero."

**Trap.** b taken as the point's output (14), or the rate subtracted once instead of once per step (14 − 3 = 11): the three triangles are counted aloud.

**Kit.** `slopetri` `unit` and `step` left; `L.yint`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

#### `predict-linear-output` — A new output from two points.

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Determine the rate of change ... from two (x, y) values"; then the rule used to predict.

**Foundations first.** Rate as output change over input change, with the warning built into the definition: if the inputs skip, the output change is for the whole skip, so divide. A two-row table with a blue input arc and a rust output arc shows it before the example. Then "step from a point you know": new output = known output + rate × (steps to the new input).

**Read.** "Output at input 9" from two points. At a glance: is y rising or falling between the points; a point with x = 0 is the start for free.

**The example.** Through (0, −1) and (3, −4), output at 9. (1) Table and graph: the inputs skip by 3 (blue arc "+3"), the output drops 3 (rust arc "−3"); rate = −3 ÷ 3 = −1 per one. Said: "The output change is for three steps, not one." (2) From (3, −4) to input 9 is 6 steps; each step changes the output by −1; the triangles step right to (9, −10). (3) −4 + (−1) × 6 = −10. (4) Check from the start: b is −1 since (0, −1) is on the axis; y = −x − 1 at 9 is −10.

*Quick, a fraction rate with a skip* ((2, 11/2) and (4, 8), output at 8): change 8 − 11/2 = 5/2 over 2 is 5/4 per one; four steps to input 8 add 5: 13. The decoy 18 came from using 5/2 as the per-one rate.

**Fast.** Rate = Δy ÷ Δx. Steps = new input − known input. New output = known output + rate × steps.

**Sense.** The line falls, so the output at 9 is below −4 by six steps of 1.

**Check.** From the other known point: −1 + (−1) × 9 = −10.

**Line.** "Divide by the input change. Then step from a point you know."

**Trap.** The output change taken as the rate when inputs skip (−3 per one, giving −22): the blue arc is labeled +3, and the division is written.

**Kit.** `table` deltas on both columns; `slopetri` `step` right.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

#### `first-cheaper-whole-input` — The first whole input where a plan is strictly cheaper.

**Standard.** 8.EE.C.7b (the tie as a linear equation in one variable) and 8.EE.C.8c; the catalog's HSA.REI.B.3 and HSA.CED.A.1 overstate it.

**Foundations first.** Two plans are two lines from x = 0: one starts higher (the fee, gold) and climbs slowly; the other starts at 0 and climbs fast. The gap at the start closes by the difference in rates each step; at the crossing they cost the same. Define *strictly cheaper*: less, not equal. The tie may fall between whole inputs or on one, and the question asks for a whole input.

**Read.** "First whole x where A is strictly cheaper": the plan with the smaller rate wins only after the tie. At a glance: the tie is the gap divided by the rate difference; the answer is the first whole number past it.

**The example.** Plan A costs 10 + 3x; Plan B costs 7x. (1) Lines from 0: A from (0, 10) climbing 3; B from the origin climbing 7; the gap at 0 bracketed: 10. (2) Table x = 0 to 4 beside the graph: A 10, 13, 16, 19, 22; B 0, 7, 14, 21, 28; the gap closes by 4 each row. (3) 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5: the crossing drops to the axis between 2 and 3; the equation 10 + 3x = 7x gives the same 2.5 in two margin lines. (4) At 2, A is 16 and B is 14: B cheaper. At 3, A is 19 and B is 21: A cheaper, first time. (5) 3; the row lights.

*Quick case, the tie on a whole number* (7 + x against 2x): the gap 7 closes by 1 each step, so they tie at exactly 7, both 14. Equal is not strictly cheaper; the answer is 8, where A is 15 and B is 16.

**Fast.** Gap ÷ rate difference is the tie; the first whole number strictly past it.

**Sense.** The fee plan cannot be cheaper at 0; the answer is past the crossing on the picture.

**Check.** Both plans at the answer and at the input before: 19 against 21, and 16 against 14.

**Line.** "Same at the crossing. Strictly cheaper starts at the next whole number."

**Trap.** Giving the crossing (2.5) or rounding it down (2): the table row at 2 shows B still cheaper; and at a whole tie, giving the tie itself (7): the row reads equal.

**Kit.** `line` from 0, `meet` with the gold drop, `table` two outputs with `tie`, the shrinking gap bracket.

**Writer.** Cheaper model; both cases named.

#### `graph-equation-choice` — The equation of a drawn line.

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Determine the rate of change and initial value ... from a graph"; 8.F.A.3: "y = mx + b ... whose graph is a straight line."

**Foundations first.** Two numbers to read off any line: b where it crosses the y-axis (gold dot), and m from a slope triangle between two grid-corner points, run right then rise. Say the axis step before reading anything ("each line is one"). Both read in that order, b first, because the crossing needs no work.

**Read.** "Which equation": every choice is y = mx + b, so the choices are four (m, b) pairs. At a glance: the line climbs, so m is positive; it does not pass through the origin, so b is not 0.

**The example.** The line through A(−2, 0) and B(1, 6) (the writer uses the template's points; choose the view so the crossing is on screen). (1) Direction: climbing; y = −2x + 4 dims. (2) The gold crossing at (0, 4); b = 4; y = 2x dims (b = 0 would pass through the origin). (3) Triangle from A: run +3, rise +6; m = 6/3 = 2. (4) y = 2x + 4; the swapped y = 4x + 2 dims because it would cross at 2, and the line crosses at 4. (5) Check: A gives 2 × (−2) + 4 = 0.

**Fast.** Read b at the axis. Run right between grid corners, read the rise, reduce.
