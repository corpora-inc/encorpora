# Linear relationships, part 1: the slope skill and compare-functions, grade 8

Ten problem types from two skills, written from the family design. Planning sources: the `facts.ts` variants and the exact standard text. Each stands on the family's foundations 1 to 5 and uses the pictures of section 2: the slope triangle built from the left point (blue run, rust rise), the table with difference arcs and the row that lights its point, the gold start.

**The shared opening of the slope skill.** `unequal-step-rate` is the birth lesson and builds everything from the table. Every other film in the skill opens with the same twenty-five seconds, drawn live, never assumed: a line on a grid; two points on it; from the left point a blue run straight right along the grid, then a rust rise straight up or down to the right point, a right-angle mark; "slope is the rise over the run: how much y changes for each one of x"; the fraction in the legs' colors; the line's direction gives the sign (climbing left to right is plus, falling is minus). Then the example.

**Collected flags, before the concepts.** `mcas-slope-sign-description` generates stories that contradict themselves (a battery's charge falling as the days used fall); `slope-triangle-statements` prints truncated choices; `unequal-step-rate` leans on fractions like −29/4 that bury a grade-8 idea; `graph-linear-equation` includes point-slope form, which is Algebra 1 surface inside a grade-8 skill; `linear-function-choice` judges equations only, while 8.F.A.3's own example is a set of points; `graphed-line-slope` and `compare-function-rates` ship figures the facts do not print, so the designer defines the picture and the template must be able to produce one like it. Each is said again in its concept.

### `unequal-step-rate` — The rate from a table, and the line it draws (birth lesson)

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Determine the rate of change ... from two (x, y) values, including reading these from a table." 8.EE.B.5's "interpreting the unit rate as the slope of the graph" is what the lesson adds. Flag: four of six variants carry fractions like −29/4 and −17/2; the whole-number variant must carry the type, fractions as a stretch.

**Foundations first.** (1) A rate is "the same change for each one more" (family 10, named in a line: 7 hours for 4 lawns is a rate per lawn). (2) A table of inputs and outputs, with blue arcs in the left margin showing input steps and rust arcs in the right margin showing output changes; the arcs are the whole picture. (3) When the inputs skip, an output change covers several steps, so per one is output change ÷ input change. (4) The row-to-point link: each row is a point; a steady rate puts the points on a straight line; the rate seen on the graph is called the **slope**, and the unit triangle (run exactly 1, rise m) is the "per one" standing on the graph.

**Read.** "Output change per input unit" (gold): one number, the same between any two rows. At a glance: outputs grow while inputs grow, so positive; outputs grow faster than inputs (4 while inputs go 2), so more than 1.

**The example.** (1, 5), (3, 9), (6, 15). (1) Table: inputs 1, 3, 6; outputs 5, 9, 15. Blue arcs +2, +3; rust arcs +4, +6. "The output changes are not equal. But neither are the input steps." (2) Per one: 4 over 2 steps is 2 each; 6 over 3 steps is 2 each. Same number: a steady rate of 2. (3) Each row lights, and its point lights on the graph beside it. Between (1, 5) and (3, 9) a slope triangle: run +2, rise +4; it splits into two unit triangles, run 1, rise 2 each. (4) Step: from (1, 5) a unit triangle, again, again: (2, 7), (3, 9), (4, 11), (5, 13), (6, 15); the landings hit the table's rows; the line is drawn through them. "That is the rate on the graph: the slope." (5) Answer 2.

Quick second case (a different look: falling, fractional, and a one-step pair available): (0, 4), (1, 15/4), (4, 3). Rows 0 and 1 are one step apart, so the rate is read straight off: −1/4. Rows 1 and 4 confirm: −3/4 over 3 steps is −1/4. Shown because the learner meets it and because it says: look for a one-step pair first.

**Fast.** Any two rows: output change ÷ input change. **Sense.** The picture: the line climbs about two squares for every one across. **Check.** The other pair, (3, 9) to (6, 15): 6 ÷ 3 = 2; step from one point by the rate and land on the next. **Line.** "Same change for each one more: that is a steady rate, and it draws a straight line." **Trap.** The first output change, 4, given as the rate because two rows look like one step. The input arc reads +2 and the triangle splits in two on screen; it cannot survive. **Kit.** `table` with `deltas` and `link`; `slopetri` with `unit` and `step`. All in the family's build list. **Writer.** Strongest: the foundation is the whole lesson and the step motion is the proof.

### `line-slope` — Slope from two points

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Determine the rate of change ... from two (x, y) values." 8.EE.B.6 underneath: slope is "the same between any two distinct points."

**Foundations first.** The shared opening: slope on a triangle, the two colors, sign is direction. One more thing, said once and shown: the subtraction on the board is the triangle in numbers. Run = right x minus left x; rise = right y minus left y, in the same order; the rise carries the sign.

**Read.** "Slope of the line through (8, −2) and (12, −4)." At a glance: put the smaller x first, 8 then 12; y goes from −2 to −4, down; the answer is negative. The drop (2) is smaller than the run (4), so the fraction is between 0 and −1.

**The example.** (1) Grid with the origin in view; both points plotted and labeled. (2) From (8, −2), the left point, a blue run right to x = 12: four steps counted on the grid, labeled +4. (3) A rust rise straight down to (12, −4): two steps, labeled −2; right-angle mark. (4) Board, in colors: run = 12 − 8 = 4; rise = −4 − (−2) = −4 + 2 = −2 (signed subtraction written out once, and the picture agrees: down 2). (5) m = −2/4. (6) Reduce, on the picture: the triangle shrinks to run 2, rise −1, from (8, −2) to (10, −3), on the same line; same shape, same slope: m = −1/2. "Reducing the fraction is the same triangle, smaller."

**Fast.** Smaller x first; rise over run from the subtraction; sign from the picture. **Sense.** The line falls, gently. **Check.** Step from (8, −2) by run 2, rise −1, twice: (10, −3), (12, −4), the other point. Or subtract in the other order, both numbers: 2 over −4 is still −1/2. **Line.** "Run right, then read the rise. Up is plus, down is minus." **Trap.** Two realistic ones. Mixed order, −4 − (−2) over 8 − 12, which gives +1/2: the at-a-glance "it falls, so minus" catches it. And −4 − (−2) = −6: the subtraction is written beside a rise that is visibly two squares. Run over rise (4/−2 = −2) is kept impossible by the fraction always written in the legs' colors, rise on top. **Kit.** `slopetri` with `frac`, `reduce` as the shrinking triangle, `step`. Nothing beyond. **Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one method, one component.

### `graphed-line-slope` — Slope from a line on a grid

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Determine the rate of change ... including reading these from ... a graph." Flag: the facts print no figure; the picture below is the designer's, and the template must draw points on grid corners as the app's figure does.

**Foundations first.** The shared opening. Plus two reading habits: a coordinate is read over, then up (left 4, down 2 is (−4, −2)); and the grid step is read before anything is counted ("each grid step is one unit" is in the problem; the lesson says to look for that line every time).

**Read.** "Slope of the line through A and B." At a glance: the line climbs left to right, so positive; it climbs less than one square for each square across, so a fraction under 1.

**The example.** A(−4, −2), B(4, 0) on a unit grid. (1) Read A: left 4, down 2; read B: right 4, up 0. Labels placed clear of the line. (2) From A, the left point, blue run right to x = 4: eight steps counted, +8. (3) Rust rise up to B: two steps, +2. Right-angle mark. (4) m = 2/8 in colors. (5) Reduce on the picture: the triangle from A to (0, −1), where the line crosses a grid corner, has run 4, rise 1; same slope: m = 1/4. (6) Quick tilt beat: if B sat below A, the rise would point down and the slope would be negative; nothing else changes.

**Fast.** Pick two points where the line crosses grid corners; run right; read the rise with its sign; reduce. **Sense.** Gentle climb, small positive fraction. **Check.** From A, run 4, rise 1, twice: (0, −1), (4, 0), which is B. **Line.** "Run right, then read the rise." **Trap.** Run over rise (8/2 = 4) is the realistic grid mistake; the colored fraction ends it. Counting grid lines instead of steps gives 9 or 3: the lesson counts steps aloud as arcs, never lines, as the number line does. **Kit.** `slopetri` must attach to the app's own figure (`example: true`) and its point names, or the lesson rebuilds the figure at the same scale. **Writer.** Capable cheaper model.

### `mcas-slope-sign-description` — The sign of a slope from a story

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Interpret the rate of change ... in terms of the situation it models." Flag first: the positive variants are generated by making both quantities "decrease," which produces stories that contradict themselves (a battery's charge falling as the days of use fall; time decreasing). The template should pair the input-decreasing wording only with contexts where it makes sense, or let the output direction alone vary. The lesson still teaches the reversed read, because the app asks it.

**Foundations first.** A line on labeled axes (the quantities named, "distance climbed" across, "temperature" up). Slope sign is direction: a gold dot slides right along the line; on a climbing line it goes up (positive), on a falling line down (negative), on a flat line neither (zero), while a slope readout runs through the tilt. Then the one idea the type turns on: slide the dot *left* on a climbing line and it goes down; the line has not changed. "Reading the line backwards is still the same line." So the sign is about whether the two quantities move the same way or opposite ways.

**Read.** Two sentences: which way the input moves, which way the output moves. At a glance: same direction, plus; opposite, minus; output unmoving, zero. "It depends on the starting value" is never the answer.

**The example.** "As the distance d climbed decreases, the temperature T increases. Sign of the slope?" Choices: Positive, Negative, It depends on the starting value, Zero. (1) The two words go gold in turn: decreases, increases. Opposite directions. (2) Picture: a falling line, T against d; the gold dot slides left (distance decreasing) and rises (temperature increasing). (3) Read it forward to be sure: slide right, it falls: as you climb, it gets colder. Negative. (4) "Depends on the starting value" dims with its reason: shift the line up and down (the start changes), the direction does not. Zero dims: the temperature moves.

Quick second case: "As time decreases, the water level decreases." Both the same way; the dot slides left and goes down on a *climbing* line. Positive. Said once: the story means the level rises as time passes.

**Fast.** Same direction, positive; opposite, negative; output unchanged, zero. **Sense.** Picture the dot sliding and the line it is on. **Check.** Rewrite the sentence forward ("as d increases, T decreases") and ask the sign again; it must match. **Rule out.** "Depends on the starting value" always dims: the start is b, direction is m. **Line.** "Same direction, plus. Opposite directions, minus." **Trap.** "Decreases" read as negative regardless of the input: the dot sliding both ways on one unchanged line makes it impossible. **Kit.** `line` with `tilt` and `shift`, a dot that glides along the line, labeled axes, `dim`. **Writer.** Capable cheaper model.

### `staar-line-from-points` — Writing the line, three ways

**Standard.** 8.F.B.4: "Determine the rate of change and initial value of the function ... from two (x, y) values." 8.F.A.3 for the Ax + By = C kind: rewriting into y = mx + b is what shows it "defining a linear function." Three kinds: slope and a point; two points; the slope of Ax + By = C.

**Foundations first.** y = mx + b read aloud: "start at b, then add m for every one of x" (named from the birth lesson, the step shown once from a gold start). The **y-intercept** defined: the value when x is 0, where the line meets the y-axis, gold. Then the idea the whole type turns on: a point on the line makes the equation true, so from the slope and one point you can walk back to x = 0 and read b.

**Read.** Which kind: what is given, what is asked (gold: "equation," "slope and y-intercept," "rate of change"). At a glance, kind A: the slope is given, so every choice with another slope is gone before any work; b is not the y of the point unless that point has x = 0. Kind B: does y fall as x goes right? Kind C: are the x and y terms on the same side with the same sign? Then y must shrink as x grows: negative.

**The example (kind A, full).** "Slope −3/5 through (15, 4). Which is an equation?" Choices: y = −3/5 x − 5, y = −3/5 x + 13, y = −3/5 x + 4, y = 3/5 x + 13. (1) y = 3/5 x + 13 dims: wrong sign. (2) Picture: the point (15, 4) on the grid, the line through it falling. Walk back: a slope triangle stepping left, run −5 rise +3, each landing a dot: (10, 7), (5, 10), (0, 13). The axis crossing is gold: 13. "Going back against a falling line goes up, so b is above 4." (3) Board: 4 = −3/5 · 15 + b; 4 = −9 + b; b = 4 − (−9) = 13. (4) y = −3/5 x + 13. (5) y = −3/5 x + 4 dims: 4 is the y of the point, not the value at zero (and a line through (0, 4) and (15, 4) would be flat). y = −3/5 x − 5 dims: 4 − 9, the sign slipped. (6) Check: −3/5 · 15 + 13 = −9 + 13 = 4.

**Kind B, brief.** (1, 1) and (4, −8): run 3, rise −9, m = −3; walk back one step from (1, 1): up 3, (0, 4); b = 4. Rule out: m = 3 (y falls, so negative), m = −1/3 (run over rise), b = 1 (the y of the point). **Kind C, brief.** 4x + 8y = 10: get y alone: 8y = −4x + 10; y = −1/2 x + 5/4. The rate is what multiplies x once y is alone: −1/2. Rule out: 1/2 (x and y on one side with the same sign means opposite directions). Check: x = 0 gives 5/4, x = 2 gives 1/4; down 1 over 2.

**Fast.** m first, then b = y − m·x (the walk back in one line), then write; for Ax + By = C, get y alone first. **Sense.** Walking back against a falling line goes up: b above the point's y. **Check.** The given point makes both sides equal. **Rule out.** Wrong sign, then wrong slope, then fails the point. **Line.** "To find b, walk back to x equals zero." **Trap.** b taken as the point's y (the +4 and b = 1 decoys), and b = y + m·x (the −5). The dots landing at (0, 13) make the first impossible; the written 4 − (−9) the second. **Kit.** `line`, `slopetri.step` leftward with landing dots, `dim`. **Writer.** Strongest: three kinds under one name, and the walk back must be staged as a proving motion, not described.

### `graph-linear-equation` — Place two points to graph a line

**Standard.** 8.EE.B.5: "Graph proportional relationships, interpreting the unit rate as the slope of the graph" for the proportional variants; HSF.IF.C.7a: "Graph linear ... functions and show intercepts" for the rest. Flag: the point-slope variants are Algebra 1 surface (HSF.LE.A.2) inside a grade-8 skill; keep them as a stretch and let standard form and the proportional case carry the type.

**Foundations first.** A line is every point that makes its equation true; two points fix a line; so graphing is finding two sure points and placing them. Where the sure point hides in each form: in y = mx + b it is (0, b); in y = kx it is (0, 0), always; in y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) it is (x₁, y₁) with the signs flipped; in Ax + By = C, pick an x that makes y whole, and y = 0 or x = 0 is the first try. The second point comes from stepping by the slope.

**Read.** Which form, and where its sure point is. At a glance: the sign of the slope says which way the line leans; any placed pair must lean that way.

**The example (standard form, full).** Graph −2x − 3y = 10. (1) Try y = 0: −2x = 10, x = −5. A sure point: (−5, 0), placed. (2) Get y alone for the slope: −3y = 2x + 10; y = −2/3 x − 10/3. The slope is −2/3: falls. (The start, −10/3, is not on a grid corner, which is why x = 0 was not the first try.) (3) From (−5, 0), run 3 right, rise 2 down: (−2, −2), placed. (4) The line through them. (5) Check: −2(−2) − 3(−2) = 4 + 6 = 10. True.

**Quick cases, each a different read.** Point-slope: y − 2 = −1/2 (x + 3): the point is (−3, 2), signs flipped; run 2, rise −1: (−1, 1); check 1 − 2 = −1 and −1/2 (−1 + 3) = −1. Proportional from words: y proportional to x, y = 6 when x = 2: (0, 0) always, and (2, 6); per one is 3, so (1, 3) also works; "any two points on the line are right." Flat: y = 4: every point has y = 4; (−3, 4) and (1, 4).

**Fast.** Find one sure point from the form, then step by the slope. **Sense.** The placed pair leans the way the sign says. **Check.** Put the second point into the original equation. **Line.** "Two points make the line. Find one sure point, then step by the slope." **Trap.** Reading (3, −2) from y − 2 = m(x + 3): the lesson plugs the point into the form so both sides are zero. Choosing x = 0 in standard form and stranding a fraction: the first try is y = 0 when C ÷ A is whole. **Kit.** `line`, `slopetri.step`; the app's own placing figure. **Writer.** Strongest: four forms, and the example for each must be the one where the sure point is found cleanly.

### `slope-triangle-statements` — Two triangles on one line

**Standard.** 8.EE.B.6: "Use similar triangles to explain why the slope m is the same between any two distinct points on a non-vertical line." HSG.SRT.B.5 for the similarity criteria. The catalog's 8.F.B.4 link is weak; 8.EE.B.6 is the real one. Flag: the facts show choices cut off mid-sentence ("The ratio is $", "The slope of line $\ell$ "); confirm the template renders complete statements.

**Foundations first.** The shared opening, then two words on `term` cards: **similar** (same shape: equal angles, every length in one triangle is the same multiple of its match in the other, the scale factor) and **congruent** (similar with factor 1: same size). Then the proof, as a motion: on one line, every slope triangle has a right angle and shares the angle the line makes with the horizontal (the horizontal legs are parallel, cut by the line), so all are similar; the small triangle dilates onto the large one and fits. Same shape means the rise-to-run ratio is the same: that is why any two points give the same slope.

**Read.** Six statements, choose three. The triangles' corners are given; the right angles named; the hypotenuses on ℓ. At a glance: the line falls, so its slope is negative; the triangles are plainly different sizes, so "congruent" is gone.

**The example.** ABC: A(−6, 1), B(−4, 1), C(−4, −2); DEF: D(−2, −5), E(4, −5), F(4, −14); right angles at B and E. (1) Equal-unit grid, both triangles drawn to scale, legs labeled: AB 2, BC 3; DE 6, EF 9. (2) Slope from the small triangle, A to C: run +2, rise −3: −3/2. From the large, D to F: run +6, rise −9: −9/6 = −3/2. Same. (3) Why: the small triangle scales by 3 and lands on the large one. Similar, factor 3; every length, the hypotenuse included, is 3 times. (4) Judge each statement in turn. Congruent: no, the factor is 3, not 1. Slope −2/3: no, that is run over rise; the slope is −3/2. DE is 3 times AB: 6 and 2, yes. Angles at B and E equal: both right angles, yes. BC/AB = 3/2: yes. DF is 2 times AC: no; the legs are 3 times, so the hypotenuse is 3 times (√13 against 3√13). (5) The three: c, d, e.

**Fast.** Run and rise of each triangle; the ratio of the runs is the scale factor and every matched pair shares it; slope is rise over run with its sign. **Sense.** The large triangle is visibly three of the small. **Check.** 6/2 = 9/3 = 3; −9/6 reduces to −3/2. **Rule out.** "Congruent" at a glance; a slope with the right sign but legs swapped is the reciprocal; a hypotenuse factor different from the legs' factor. **Line.** "Same line, same shape. Any two points on the line give the same slope." **Trap.** The reciprocal −2/3 (run over rise), the hypotenuse given its own factor, "congruent" because they look alike. The dilation landing exactly and the colored fraction make each impossible. **Kit.** `slopetri.scale`, equal-unit axes with a tall view, a select-three card with `dim`, two `term` cards. **Writer.** Strongest: the proof is a motion and five judgments follow it.

### `mcas-table-intercept-select` — Which tables start higher (or lower)

**Standard.** 8.F.A.2: "Compare properties of two functions each represented in a different way (algebraically ... numerically in tables)." 8.F.B.4: "Determine the ... initial value ... from a table."

**Foundations first.** The **y-intercept** defined: the y when x is 0; in a table, the row where x is 0. If the table has that row, read it (shown on one small table from the x: 0, 1, 3 variants: the intercept is the first y). If it does not, find the rate from the arcs and walk back, one rate per step, to x = 0; on the board, b = first y − rate × first x. Then the comparing picture: the y-axis alone as a vertical number line with the stated intercept marked; each table's start drops onto it as a gold dot, above or below.

**Read.** "Greater than" or "less than" (gold) a stated intercept; choose three. At a glance, when the tables start at x = 3: a rising table's start is below its first row, a falling table's start above it. So a falling table whose first y is already above the stated value cannot be "less" and dims now.

**The example.** y = 2x − 5, intercept −5; tables at x = 3, 4, 5; choose the three whose intercept is less than −5. (1) Mark −5 on the axis strip. (2) Table a: 5, 10, 15; arcs +5; rising, so its start is below 5; walk back three steps: 5 − 15 = −10; dot at −10, below −5: yes. (3) The rest, two beats each. b: −2, −1, 0; rate 1; −2 − 3 = −5: on the mark, neither. c: −5, −3, −1; rate 2; −5 − 6 = −11: yes. d: 13, 19, 25; rate 6; 13 − 18 = −5: neither. e: −4, −5, −6; falling; its start is above −4, so above −5: no (dimmed at a glance). f: 4, 8, 12; rate 4; 4 − 12 = −8: yes. (4) a, c, f.

**Fast.** Rate from any two rows; b = first y − rate × first x; compare. **Sense.** Rising starts lower than the first row, falling starts higher. **Check.** Exactly three chosen; for one, step forward from the start: −10 + 3 × 5 = 5, the first row. **Rule out.** Tables landing exactly on the stated value are neither; tables whose direction already puts them on the wrong side dim at a glance. **Line.** "The intercept is the row where x is zero. If the table doesn't have it, walk back." **Trap.** The first row taken as the intercept; table c, whose first y equals −5 but whose start is −11, shows it going wrong live. **Kit.** `table` with `deltas` and `extend` (adding the x = 0 row), a compact set of six small tables or one at a time beside a vertical `numberline` strip with dropping dots. **Writer.** Capable cheaper model.

### `compare-function-rates` — Two functions, two numbers each

**Standard.** 8.F.A.2, exactly: "Compare properties of two functions each represented in a different way (algebraically, graphically, numerically in tables, or by verbal descriptions) ... determine which function has the greater rate of change." Flag: the graph is not in the facts; the example below defines it, and the template should be able to produce the case where the higher line has the smaller slope.

**Foundations first.** The two numbers in every form, in their colors: in an equation the number with x is the **rate of change** and the number alone the **initial value**; on a graph the rate is a slope triangle and the start is the y-axis crossing; in a table the rate is the arcs and the start the x = 0 row; in words, "per" and "when x is 0." The scratch-paper picture: a two-row table, function | rate | start. Compare as signed numbers.

**Read.** Four statements that differ by two letters. At a glance: find the two numbers for each function, write them in the summary, and the statement reads itself.

**The example.** Function A by graph: a line through (0, 5), (2, 6), (4, 7). Function B: y = 2x − 1. (1) B first: rate 2 (rust), start −1 (gold). (2) A from the graph: the y-axis crossing is 5, gold; a triangle run 2, rise 1: rate 1/2. (3) Summary: A 1/2 and 5; B 2 and −1. (4) Rate: 2 against 1/2, B. Start: 5 against −1, A. "Function B has the greater rate of change; function A has the greater initial value." The other three dim together: each has one comparison backwards. (5) Sense, on the picture: B drawn in violet on A's axes starts lower but climbs faster and meets A at (4, 7), then passes it. "Higher is not faster."

**Quick second case (signs).** A by table, x: 0, 1, 2; y: 2, 5, 8: rate 3, start 2. B by words: 11 when x is 0, decreases by 5 each step: rate −5, start 11. Rate: 3 against −5, A. Start: 11, B. "B drops by 5 each step; a drop is a negative rate, less than 3."

**Fast.** Pull m and b out of each form into the summary, then read the statement. **Sense.** The steeper line (equal scales) has the greater rate; the higher crossing the greater start; a falling function's rate is negative. **Check.** Read the chosen statement against the summary, one letter at a time. **Rule out.** All three others at once, from the summary. **Line.** "The number with the letter is per. The number alone is the start. Higher is not faster." **Trap.** The higher line taken as the faster one; the size of a drop taken as the rate without its sign. Both are the example. **Kit.** `line`, `slopetri`, `table` with `deltas`, a second function overlaid in violet, a small summary table, `dim`. **Writer.** Strongest: the example graph must be chosen so the decoy is tempting, and any pair of the four forms can appear.

### `linear-function-choice` — Which rules are linear

**Standard.** 8.F.A.3: "Interpret the equation y = mx + b as defining a linear function, whose graph is a straight line; give examples of functions that are not linear." Flag: the standard's own example is a set of points, (1, 1), (2, 4), (3, 9), "not on a straight line"; the app judges equations only. The lesson uses the points test anyway, because it is the standard's test and the one that always works.

**Foundations first.** Linear means steady: the same change for each one more, so a straight line. In writing it is "a number times x, plus a number," in any order and with either number zero or negative: 3 − 5x is −5x + 3; y = 9 is 0x + 9. The test that always works: y at x = 1, 2, 3; equal changes mean linear. Picture: a three-row table with rust arcs beside a small graph of its three points; for a linear rule the points sit on one line; for x² + 2 (3, 6, 11; changes 3, 5) and 12/x (12, 6, 4; changes −6, −2) they do not.

**Read.** "Defines a linear function" or "not linear" (gold; the two questions are opposites, so read which). At a glance: x squared, x under a fraction bar, or x under a root means not linear; x alone times a number means linear; a bare number means linear with zero rate.

**The example.** "Which equation defines a linear function?" Choices: y = 12/x, y = x² + 2, y = 4x², y = 3 − 5x. (1) The three with x² or x in a denominator dim together: x is not alone. (2) y = 3 − 5x: write it as −5x + 3; a number times x, plus a number. (3) Test: 1, 2, 3 give −2, −7, −12; arcs −5, −5; the three points fall on a line. (4) For contrast, 12/x: 12, 6, 4; arcs −6, −2; the points bend. (5) y = 3 − 5x.

**Quick second case (the reversed question and the constant).** "Which is not linear?" y = 4x + 3, y = 9, y = 6/x, y = 7 − 4x. y = 9 is not the answer: 9, 9, 9, arcs 0, 0, a flat line, steady. y = 6/x: 6, 3, 2, arcs −3, −1, not steady. Answer y = 6/x.

**Fast.** Look at how x appears. **Sense.** The graph is a straight line, or it bends. **Check.** Three inputs 1, 2, 3; equal changes. **Rule out.** Everything with x², x in a denominator or under a root dims together when "linear" is asked; everything of the form mx + b, a bare number included, dims together when "not linear" is asked. **Line.** "A number times x, plus a number. Steady change, straight line." **Trap.** y = 9 called not linear because it has no x; y = 6/x called linear because it has no square. The 1, 2, 3 arcs decide both on screen. **Kit.** `table` with `deltas`, small graphs with three points and a line, `dim`. Nothing new. **Writer.** Capable cheaper model.
