# Exponents, roots and scientific notation: concepts, part 1

Ten types: the seven of `integer-powers` plus the three misfiled with them, and the first of `exponent-laws`. Each stands on the family design's foundations and pictures; the numbering (Foundation 1, 2...) is the design's. Order of work for the pilot: `integer-power` first, because the strip is born there; `negative-integer-power` next; the rest once the strip and ladder have passed independent visual review.

Three types are misfiled, and are said so at the top of their entries; they are designed anyway because the app will film them wherever they sit.

---

### `integer-power` — Find the value of a power

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.1, the clause "evaluate numerical expressions involving whole-number exponents." The goal's "exponent 0 gives 1" is also 6.EE.A.1 (zero is a whole number) and is foundation 6's first beat.

**Foundations first.** Foundations 1 and 2, built live. (1) A plain product, written out: three fives, 5 · 5 · 5, each in its own cell of the strip; a gold bracket counts them: 3. "Three fives multiplied." (2) The cells slide together and fold into 5³; the count lands as the small raised 3. Name the parts on the folded form: the big number is the **base**, the one that repeats; the small raised number is the **exponent**, how many; the whole thing is a **power**. "Evaluate" means find the one number it equals. (3) Unfold it again so the child sees both ways at once. (4) The wrong picture, beside it: 5 · 3 is two cells, a five and a three. "Five to the third is not five times three." Pause. The wrong picture stays on screen beside the right one until the first example starts.

**Read.** "Find the value of 3⁴": one number. "Value" goes gold. At a glance: four threes multiplied is bigger than three threes (27), so the answer is past 27; and it is certainly not 12.

**The example.** 3⁴. (1) Unfold: four cells of 3, bracket "4 factors", 3⁴ beside. (2) Run: each cell lights in turn and the label under the strip grows: three; times three, nine; times three, twenty-seven; times three, eighty-one. Each product is said before the next cell lights; the label never skips. (3) 81 boxed. (4) Pairs, one beat: the four cells group in two pairs, 9 · 9 = 81, the same. (5) The ladder beside the strip, read downward for the check: 81, ÷3, 27, ÷3, 9, ÷3, 3; "divide by three once more": 1 appears at rung 0 and turns gold. "Three to the zero is one: the rung before the first factor. Anything to the zero is one." The exponent is the number of steps from 1.

**Quick case (a different word, the same method).** "Find the value of 8²." "Squared" is named by the square: an 8-by-8 grid of unit squares, to scale, fills as the voice says "eight rows of eight"; the count label reads 64. Two cells, 8 · 8, fold to 8². (Base 10 is never the example here, by the family design; 10² is covered by the method.)

**Fast.** Run the product one factor at a time, saying each: 3, 9, 27, 81. Pair up when the count is even. Both always work.

**Sense.** Bigger than the base, bigger than 3 · 3 · 3 = 27; nowhere near 12.

**Check.** Divide back by the base as many times as the exponent and land on 1 (the ladder descent), or count the factors written and multiply the last pair again.

**Line.** "The exponent counts the factors. Four threes multiplied, not three times four."

**Trap.** 3⁴ = 12, by 3 × 4 or 3 + 3 + 3 + 3. The strip shows four cells of 3 joined by dots, and the wrong picture (a 3 and a 4) stands beside it with two cells. The example is chosen so the trap gives a different number from every rung (12 is on no rung of the ladder of 3). Also 3⁰ = 0: the descent lands on 1 and the rung goes gold.

**Kit.** `factors` with `unfold`, `fold`, `run` and the wrong-picture strip (a `groups` strip of two different cells); `ladder` with `step` down and `mark`; `grid` for the 8-by-8 square (exists).

**Writer.** Strongest. The family is born here; the pacing of the run, the wrong picture held long enough, and the grade-6 voice decide whether every later lesson's sketch is trusted.

### `power-missing-exponent` — Find the exponent

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.1, "evaluate numerical expressions involving whole-number exponents", run backwards: the value is given and the exponent is found. The variants' "how many factors of 10 multiply to make 1,000" is the same question in words.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 2 restated: a power is climbed one factor at a time, and the exponent is the number of steps. The ladder of 6 is drawn with its first three rungs empty; the voice says that each rung is ×6 above the last and the exponent column counts the rungs. One beat: the exponent is a small count of steps; the value on a rung is a different kind of thing from the step number beside it.

**Read.** "If 6ⁿ = 216, what is n?": n is the exponent, a count of sixes. "Exponent" (or "how many factors") goes gold. At a glance: 6 · 6 = 36 is far too small, so n is more than 2; and n is a small number, a few steps, never anything the size of 216.

**The example.** 6ⁿ = 216. (1) Ladder of 6: rung 1, 6. (2) ×6: rung 2, 36. Not yet. (3) ×6: rung 3, 216; the rung turns gold. (4) Count the blue arrows from 1: one, two, three. n = 3. (5) The strip beside: 6 · 6 · 6, bracket "3 factors", folds to 6³.

**Quick case (a different wording and a sure shortcut).** "How many factors of 10 multiply to make 1,000,000?" The ladder of 10: 10, 100, 1,000 ... each step adds one zero, because ×10 is one shift (family 2's foundation 7). Six zeros, six steps: 6. "For ten, count the zeros." This shortcut is exact for powers of ten and is taught as such; for any other base, climb.

**Fast.** Climb the ladder to the value and count the steps (or divide the value by the base until 1 and count the divisions; same steps, read downward). For powers of ten, count the zeros.

**Sense.** The exponent is a small number; 6² = 36 was too small and 6⁴ = 1,296 would be too big, so 3 is the only step that lands on 216.

**Check.** Multiply that many copies: 6 · 6 · 6 = 216.

**Line.** "The exponent is the number of steps."

**Trap.** Dividing once and answering the quotient: 216 ÷ 6 = 36, or "81 ÷ 9, so 9". The ladder keeps the step count and the rung value in separate columns; the answer is read from the exponent column, which only ever holds small numbers.

**Kit.** `ladder` with `step` up and `mark`; `factors` fold.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one method, one component, the example fixed here, and the math check catches a wrong rung.

### `power-repeated-product` — Match a product and a power

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.1, "write ... numerical expressions involving whole-number exponents", in multiple-choice form (the only type that practices the "write" clause; the design flags that no type has the learner write a power).

**Foundations first.** Foundation 1 in both directions, on the strip: a row of equal cells folds into a power (the repeated factor becomes the base, the gold count becomes the exponent); a power unfolds into its cells. Said once: the base is the thing in the cells; the exponent is the count above them. They are different kinds of number and never trade places.

**Read.** "Which power equals 9 · 9 · 9 · 9 · 9 · 9?" The choices are read aloud: 9 · 6, 9⁵, 9⁶, 6⁹. "Power" goes gold: the answer is a base with an exponent. At a glance: the repeated number is 9, so the base is 9; that alone leaves two choices.

**The example.** (1) The six cells of 9 appear under a bracket; the count ticks one to six as each lights. (2) Fold: the cells slide into 9⁶, the 6 landing as the exponent. (3) 9⁶. Rule out, named in one beat as the choices dim together: 9 · 6 is two cells, a nine and a six, a product not a power; 6⁹ has sixes in its cells, nine of them, the wrong base; 9⁵ has one cell too few: count them. (4) The chosen power unfolds beside the problem's product and the cells match one to one.

**Second case (the other direction).** "Which product equals 5³?" choices 3 · 3 · 3 · 3 · 3, 5 · 3, 5 + 5 + 5, 5 · 5 · 5. Unfold 5³: three cells of 5 joined by dots. Dim together: 3 · 3 · 3 · 3 · 3 (base and exponent swapped: five threes); 5 · 3 (five times three, two cells); 5 + 5 + 5 (adding, not multiplying: the strip's cells are joined by dots). 5 · 5 · 5. This direction earns its own screen because the adding decoy lives only here and is what children write.

**Fast.** The repeated factor is the base; count the cells for the exponent. Reverse: write the base as many times as the exponent, with dots between.

**Sense.** The base of the answer is the number you can see repeated; the exponent is a count, so it is the smaller-looking number in a long product.

**Check.** Unfold the chosen power and match it cell for cell against the given product.

**Rule out.** Wrong base (the repeated number is not in the cells); a two-number product (a product of base and count is not a power); a plus sign (adding is not multiplying); then count.

**Line.** "The base is what repeats. The exponent is how many."

**Trap.** 9 · 6 and 6⁹: the count and the base swapped or multiplied. The fold animates the count rising into the exponent's place, so it is seen as a count, not a factor. 5 + 5 + 5 = 15 = 5 · 3: the cells carry dots, and the lesson says once that adding three fives and multiplying three fives are different things.

**Kit.** `factors` fold and unfold; `dim` for choices.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: two fixed cases, decoys named here, one component.

### `power-compare` — Which is greater?

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.1, "evaluate numerical expressions involving whole-number exponents": two values found, then compared.

**Foundations first.** One beat each: a power's value is found by running the product (foundation 2); a product of two numbers is just two cells. "Greater" means the bigger value; the only way to know is to find both values. Equal is a real possibility, and the choices include it.

**Read.** "Which is greater, 3⁴ or 3 · 4?" choices: They are equal, 3 · 4, 3⁴. "Greater" goes gold. At a glance: 3 · 4 is 12, while four threes multiplied passes 12 after the second factor, so the power wins before the run finishes.

**The example.** 3⁴ or 3 · 4 (foundation 1's wrong picture asked as a question). (1) Two strips: four cells of 3 in teal; a 3 and a 4 in rust. (2) Run the rust strip: 12. (3) Run the teal strip: 3, 9, 27, 81. (4) 81 against 12: 3⁴. "They are equal" dims: the values differ.

**Second case (the trick that is true).** "Which is greater, 2⁴ or 4²?" Run both: 2, 4, 8, 16; 4, 16. Equal. "They are equal" is the answer, and it is true here because sixteen is both four twos and two fours. One beat so the child does not learn that swapping never matters: 2³ is 8 and 3² is 9, not equal. The values decide, every time.

**Fast.** Run each product; compare the two values. No comparison is made before both values are known.

**Sense.** A power with four factors of 3 is at least 27 before its last factor; 12 cannot win.

**Check.** Both values are on the board under their strips; reread them.

**Rule out.** "They are equal" goes only when the two values are the same number, never from the look of the symbols.

**Line.** "Find both values. Then compare."

**Trap.** Deciding 2⁴ and 4² are equal because 2 · 4 = 4 · 2: right answer, wrong reason, and the same habit says 3⁴ = 3 · 4. Every comparison in the lesson is settled by two run labels, and the 2³ against 3² beat shows the swap is not a rule.

**Kit.** `factors` run on two strips; `dim`.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: two fixed cases, one method.

### `power-order-trap` — What the exponent touches

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.1, "evaluate numerical expressions involving whole-number exponents", with the expression holding a product or a sum; order of operations is 6.EE.A.2c's "perform arithmetic operations ... in the conventional order", named as a foundation from family 12.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 3, drawn as three unfolds side by side, the strip showing exactly what the exponent grabs: 2 · 5² unfolds to a loose 2, then two 5s under the bracket; (2 · 5)² unfolds to two cells each holding (2 · 5); 3 + 3² unfolds to a loose 3, a plus, then two 3s under the bracket. Said plainly: the exponent touches only the number or group right before it. Parentheses make a group; without them the exponent grabs one number. A power is multiplication already grouped, so it is done before the multiplying or adding around it (family 12's order, not a new rule).

**Read.** "Evaluate 3 + 3²": one number. At a glance: the exponent is touching only the second 3, so this is 3 plus nine, a bit more than 9.

**The example.** 3 + 3². (1) Unfold: loose 3, plus, then 3 · 3 under a gold bracket. (2) The bracketed part runs: 9; the strip folds to 3 + 9. (3) 12. The trap card, held: 36 comes from squaring 3 + 3 first, which the exponent never touched; 9 comes from reading 3² as 3 · 2.

**Second example (the group case).** "Evaluate (3 + 7)²." (1) Unfold: two cells, each holding (3 + 7); the exponent grabbed the whole group. (2) Inside first: each cell becomes 10. (3) 10 · 10 = 100. The trap card, held: 3² + 7² = 9 + 49 = 58 is wrong because the exponent counts copies of the whole group; there are two tens, not a nine and a forty-nine. This case earns a full example because the distributed square is the error children carry into Algebra 1.

**Quick case (power then multiply).** "Evaluate 5 · 2³." A loose 5, then three 2s: 2, 4, 8; 5 · 8 = 40. Not 10³. (4 · 2² and (2 · 5)² are the same two cases with other numbers; 3 + 4² the first.)

**Fast.** Look at what sits right before the exponent: a group in parentheses, or one number. Unfold that, and only that. Inside parentheses first; then the power; then multiply; then add.

**Sense.** 3 + 3² is a little more than 9; (3 + 7)² is a round hundred; 5 · 2³ is five eights.

**Check.** Reunfold the strip and count the cells: the exponent's count of copies of exactly what it touched.

**Line.** "The exponent touches only what is right before it. Powers first."

**Trap.** 3 + 3² = 36 (grouping what was not grouped); (3 + 7)² = 58 (distributing the exponent over a sum); 5 · 2³ = 1,000 (multiplying first). Each is named on a card the moment its unfold makes it impossible; the three cards are the three ways the strip can be misdrawn.

**Kit.** `factors` unfold with a loose cell outside the bracket and with a group as a cell's text.

**Writer.** Strongest. The whole lesson is reading what the exponent grabs, and a weak writer states "powers first" instead of staging the three unfolds.

### `power-doubling-story` — A quantity doubles or triples each step

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.1, "write and evaluate numerical expressions involving whole-number exponents": the story is written as 2ⁿ and evaluated. (The exponent-as-steps idea is what family 16 inherits.)

**Foundations first.** Foundation 2 in a story: a quantity that doubles each step is the ladder of 2; the step number is the exponent; step 0 is the start, before any doubling, and the quantity there is 1. Built on the app's own bar graph: the four bars (folds 0 to 3) read 1, 2, 4, 8, and a blue ×2 is said between each pair, so the picture is seen as a ladder climbed. "After n folds, 2ⁿ layers." Said once: the bars are to scale, and they are about to run out of room; the count will not.

**Read.** "Folding a sheet doubles its layers. The bars show 0 to 3 folds. How many layers after 8 folds?" "8 folds" goes gold: rung 8 of the ladder, eight doublings from one sheet. At a glance: far more than 16; 2 · 8 is two cells, not eight.

**The example.** (1) Read the bars: 1, 2, 4, 8; the ladder of 2 draws rungs 0 to 3 beside them with to-scale bars that match. (2) Step: fold 4, 16; fold 5, 32: the bar runs off the card edge with a marker. "The picture stops; the count does not." (3) Fold 6, 64; fold 7, 128; fold 8, 256; the rung goes gold. (4) 2⁸ = 256. (5) From the last bar instead: fold 3 to fold 8 is five more doublings: 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256. Same rung.

**Quick case (tripling, and "new people").** "Each day everyone who learned it the day before tells 3 new people; bars show days 0 to 3; how many new people on day 4?" Bars 1, 3, 9, 27, ×3 each; one more step: 81. The question asks the new people that day, which is the bar, not a running total.

**Fast.** Start at 1 and multiply by the base once per step; or climb from the last bar shown, counting the remaining steps. Pair up to shorten: 2⁸ = 16 · 16.

**Sense.** Eight doublings from one is in the hundreds; far past 16.

**Check.** Count the blue arrows from rung 0 to the gold rung: 8. Or halve back from 256 eight times to 1.

**Line.** "The exponent is the number of steps."

**Trap.** 8 folds → 16 layers (2 × 8); or landing on 2⁷ = 128 by counting from the first bar as step 1. The ladder labels every rung with its fold number starting at 0, and the two ways of climbing (from 1, from the last bar) land on the same rung.

**Kit.** `scene` for the app's bar graph (exists); `ladder` with `bars: true` and clipping at the card edge, and column titles that can read "folds | power | layers" instead of "exponent | power | value".

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: the app's own figure plus one ladder, the two cases fixed here.

### `negative-integer-power` — Evaluate a negative power

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.1, "Know and apply the properties of integer exponents to generate equivalent numerical expressions"; the clause practiced is the last link of its example, 3⁻³ = 1/3³ = 1/27: a negative power rewritten as a positive power of the reciprocal and evaluated.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 6, as a descent that proves. The ladder of 3 with rungs 2 and 1 shown: 9, 3, each step down ÷3. "Divide by three again": rung 0 appears, 1, and turns gold: "three to the zero is one, the rung before the first factor." Again: rung −1, 1/3. Again: rung −2, 1/9. The rungs below zero hold fractions, never negative numbers: dividing past 1 makes small, not negative. Said once: a negative exponent counts divisions, and the sure way to write one is to flip the base (its **reciprocal**: 3 becomes 1/3) and then use the positive exponent, because 3⁻² is 1/3 · 1/3. "Flip, not minus." The sign of the answer never comes from the exponent.

**Read.** "Evaluate 5⁻⁴. Give an exact fraction or whole number." "Exact" goes gold: a fraction, not a decimal. At a glance: the base is positive, so the answer is positive; the exponent is negative, so it is a fraction under 1.

**The example.** 5⁻⁴. (1) Flip the base: 1/5. The exponent becomes +4. (2) Unfold: four cells of 1/5 under a bracket; the tops multiply to 1, the bottoms run 5, 25, 125, 625. (3) 1/625. (4) Check: 1/625 · 625 = 1: a power and its negative are reciprocals, four steps down and four steps up on the ladder.

**Second example (the sign).** (−2)⁻³. The minus is inside the parentheses, so it belongs to the base and is raised too. (1) Flip: 1/(−2), exponent +3. (2) Unfold: three cells of −2 on the bottom; an odd count of negatives makes a negative (family 3): (−2)(−2)(−2) = −8. (3) 1/(−8) = −1/8. (4) Check: −1/8 · −8 = 1. The trap card, held: −8 makes the exponent's minus into the answer's minus; 1/8 loses the base's sign. Two separate facts: the exponent's sign says flip; the base's sign and the count say the answer's sign.

**Quick cases (a fraction base; exponent −1).** (5/4)⁻²: flipping a fraction swaps top and bottom (family 9), 4/5; squared: 16/25. (−1/5)⁻¹: exponent −1 is one flip and nothing else: −5.

**Fast.** Flip the base; raise to the positive exponent. Find the sign from the base alone: negative base with an odd exponent gives a negative answer; otherwise positive.

**Sense.** A positive base with a negative exponent gives a fraction between 0 and 1 (5⁻⁴ is a little more than a thousandth). A base that is a fraction smaller than one flips to something bigger than one.

**Check.** Multiply the answer by the positive power: 1/625 · 625 = 1; −1/8 · −8 = 1.

**Line.** "A negative exponent means flip, not minus."

**Trap.** 5⁻⁴ = −625 or −1/625; (−2)⁻³ = 1/8 or −8; 2⁻³ = −6. The descent shows rungs below zero holding fractions, never negatives; sign and flip are found in two separate beats on the board, and the trap card names both losses.

**Kit.** `ladder` with `step` down past zero (fraction values) and `mark`; `factors` unfold with fraction and negative cells on a fraction bar.

**Writer.** Strongest. The descent is a proof, and sign and flip must be kept apart on screen; this is the misconception the strand's tests target most.

### `staar-signed-exponent-order` — Order of operations with negatives

**Misfiled and off grade.** The exponent is incidental; the work is integer arithmetic with negatives: 7.NS.A.3, "solve ... mathematical problems involving the four operations with rational numbers." Move to grade 7 (family 3 or family 12 at grade 7), as the curriculum plan already flags. Designed here because it borrows foundation 3.

**Standard.** 7.NS.A.3 (above), with 6.EE.A.2c's conventional order; 6.EE.A.1 only for the single power.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 3's three-unfold shot (what the exponent touches) with one addition that is the whole point here: (−6)² unfolds to two cells of −6, two negatives make a positive (family 3), 36; −6² unfolds to a minus standing outside the bracket and two cells of 6: −36. Parentheses decide whether the exponent grabs the sign. Then the order said once, plainly: inside parentheses; powers; multiply and divide left to right; add and subtract left to right.

**Read.** "Evaluate (1 − 7)² − 5 × 8": one number, possibly negative. At a glance: the first part is a square, so positive; the second is 40; the answer is 36 take away 40, a little below zero.

**The example.** (1 − 7)² − 5 × 8. (1) Inside first: 1 − 7 = −6; keep the minus. (2) Unfold the square: (−6)(−6) = 36. (3) 5 × 8 = 40. (4) 36 − 40 = −4. Check: −4 + 40 = 36 = (−6)².

**Second example (no parentheses).** −6² + 6 × 4. (1) The exponent grabs only the 6: −(6 · 6) = −36. (2) 6 × 4 = 24. (3) −36 + 24 = −12. The trap card, held beside the first example: with parentheses the square was +36; without them it is −36. This case is the reason the type exists.

**Quick case (left to right).** 23 − 4 × 3² + 11: 3² = 9; 4 × 9 = 36; 23 − 36 = −13; −13 + 11 = −2. Adding and subtracting go left to right; the 11 is added, not swept into the subtraction (23 − 47 = −24 is the slip).

**Fast.** Unfold what the exponent touches first; parentheses, powers, multiply and divide, add and subtract, left to right within a step. Keep every negative as a number, never as a sign to sort out later.

**Sense.** Track signs before values: a square in parentheses is positive, a minus outside a square stays negative; 36 − 40 is just under zero.

**Check.** Undo the last step: −4 + 40 = 36; −12 − 24 = −36; −2 − 11 = −13.

**Line.** "Parentheses decide whether the exponent grabs the sign."

**Trap.** (1 − 7)² = −36; −6² = +36; 23 − 36 + 11 = −24. The two squares unfold side by side with the minus inside or outside the bracket, and the left-to-right beat writes each step on its own line.

**Kit.** `factors` unfold with a signed cell and a minus outside the bracket; the board.

**Writer.** Strongest: mixed order of operations with negatives, where the lesson is reading grouping and sign, not a recipe.

### `prime-factorization-exponents` — Write a prime factorization with exponents

**Misfiled.** Prime factorization is family 7 (`gcf-lcm`; 6.NS.B.4 territory in Common Core, named outright in state standards). It touches this family only by foundation 4: an exponent counts a repeated prime. Move it; the lesson borrows the strip for one beat.

**Badly seeded.** Both sampled variants (30, 66) have no repeated prime, so the exponent appears only in a decoy and the type's own title is never practiced. The generator should produce numbers with a repeated prime (60, 72, 90); the lesson's example uses one.

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.1, "write ... numerical expressions involving whole-number exponents", in letter only; the content is factoring into primes.

**Foundations first.** Prime: a whole number bigger than 1 with exactly two factors, itself and 1 (2, 3, 5, 7, 11 ...). Composite: has other factors. The factor tree (family 7's): split a number into two factors bigger than 1, keep splitting whatever is not prime, stop when every branch ends in a prime, drawn live. Then foundation 4 on the strip: the primes collected in order, 2 · 2 · 3 · 5; the two 2s fold into 2², "the exponent counts how many times that prime appears"; a prime that appears once has no exponent.

**Read.** "Which is the prime factorization of 60, with exponents for repeated factors?" "Prime" goes gold: every factor in the answer must be prime, and their product must be 60. At a glance: any choice with a composite in it (a 6, a 10) is not finished; any choice whose product is not 60 is wrong.

**The example.** 60. (1) Tree: 60 = 6 × 10; 6 = 2 · 3; 10 = 2 · 5; the four leaves circle as primes. (2) The leaves line up on the strip in order: 2 · 2 · 3 · 5. (3) The two 2s fold: 2² · 3 · 5. (4) Check: 4 · 3 · 5 = 60.

**Quick case (no repeats, as the app currently generates).** 30 = 2 · 3 · 5, every prime once, no exponent anywhere. The decoy 2 · 3 · 5² multiplies to 150; "with exponents" does not mean an exponent must be written.

**Fast.** Tree to primes, line them up smallest first, fold repeats into exponents, multiply back.

**Sense.** Small primes for a small number; the product of the choice is about the size of the number before you finish multiplying.

**Check.** Multiply the chosen factorization back to the number.

**Rule out.** A composite factor (5 × 6, 2 × 33): not finished. A product that is not the number (2 × 3 = 6, 2 · 3 · 5² = 150): multiply and dim. Then one remains.

**Line.** "Every branch ends in a prime. The exponent counts how many times a prime appears."

**Trap.** Writing an exponent because the question says "with exponents" (5² for a single 5), or stopping at 5 × 6 because the tree's first split looks finished. The strip's fold happens only when two equal cells meet, and the tree circles only primes.

**Kit.** A factor tree (nodes and branches: `seg`, `label`, `circle` can draw it today; a `tree` helper that splits a node live belongs to family 7's kit); `factors` fold; `dim`.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one method, decoys named, example fixed.

### `prime-factorization-value` — Three questions about a prime factorization

**Misfiled.** Family 7 with the type above; two of its three kinds have no exponent at all. Move it. It is a **mixed** type and the lesson teaches reading: three different questions share one picture (the primes of a number), and no recipe joins them.

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.1, "evaluate numerical expressions involving whole-number exponents", for the evaluate kind only; the other two are prime factorization (family 7).

**Foundations first.** Prime, defined as above in one beat. Foundation 4: a prime factorization written with exponents is a count of repeated primes, 2⁴ · 5 · 7² meaning four 2s, one 5, two 7s; the strip unfolds it to seven cells. Then the one idea true for all three questions: read what is asked about the primes: their product, the biggest one, or how many there are.

**Read.** Three wordings, one each on its own screen: "What number has the prime factorization 2⁴ × 5 × 7²?" (a product: one number); "What is the greatest prime factor of 99?" (a prime, so it must be prime, and it divides 99); "Write 54 as a product of primes, repeating each time it is a factor. How many primes are in the product?" (a count, repeats included).

**The examples, one per kind.**
Product: 2⁴ · 5 · 7². (1) Unfold: four 2s, a 5, two 7s. (2) Each power runs: 2, 4, 8, 16; 7, 49. (3) 16 · 5 = 80; 80 · 49 = 3,920. Sense: about 80 · 50 = 4,000. Check: 3,920 ÷ 49 = 80, ÷ 5 = 16 = 2⁴.
Greatest prime: 99. Tree: 99 = 9 × 11; 9 = 3 · 3; 11 is prime. Primes: 3, 3, 11; the greatest is 11. Check: 11 is prime and 99 ÷ 11 = 9. (The trap card: 99 itself is not prime, 9 is not prime, 33 is not prime; the answer must be a leaf of the tree.)
Count: 54. Tree: 54 = 2 × 27; 27 = 3 · 9; 9 = 3 · 3. Strip: 2 · 3 · 3 · 3, four cells; folded, 2 · 3³, and the exponents say the same count: 1 + 3 = 4. Check: 2 · 3 · 3 · 3 = 54.

**Fast.** Product: run each power, then multiply the results. Greatest prime: tree to the leaves, take the biggest. Count: tree to the leaves and count every cell, or add the exponents (a lone prime counts 1).

**Sense.** The product kind: an estimate before multiplying (80 · 50). The others: the answer to "greatest prime" is prime; the count is small and includes repeats.

**Check.** Divide the product back by its primes to 1; test that the greatest prime divides the number; multiply the counted primes back.

**Line.** None shared, by design; the recap names each kind by what it asks: "the product", "the biggest leaf", "how many leaves".

**Trap.** 2⁴ = 8 in the product kind (the strip's four 2s run to 16); 9 or 33 as a "greatest prime factor" (circled leaves only); counting 2 distinct primes in 54 instead of 4 (the question says "repeating each time", gold; the exponent is a count).
