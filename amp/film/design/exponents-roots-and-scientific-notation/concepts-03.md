# Exponents, roots and scientific notation: part 3 (the last ten types)

Concepts for the remaining types of `scientific-notation` and all of `rational-exponents`, written from the family design. Facts snapshot 2026-11-09. As in place value, each concept is one idea, one picture, one example that exposes the trap, the sure fast way, and the check.

Two shared openings, so the concepts below need not repeat them:

- **The scientific-notation opening** (about twenty seconds, grade 8): the chart of ten with its double headers, a number's digits in it, the first nonzero digit's column gold; "digits times size": a × 10ⁿ, a from 1 up to 10, n the place of the first digit. Owned by `standard-to-scientific`; named here in one line and shown, never re-taught.
- **The radical opening** (about twenty seconds, Algebra 2): √a is the side of a square whose area is a (√64 = 8); so √a · √a = a, by definition; a square of area 4 · 86 is a 2-by-2 array of squares of area 86, so its side is 2√86: a square factor comes out as its root; every radical answer is placed on the number line between two whole numbers (81 < 86 < 100, so √86 is a bit over 9). The sketch for every radical lesson is the **prime strip with pairs bracketed**: write the radicand's primes, bracket each pair, one of each pair comes out.

### `scientific-operations` — Calculate in scientific notation (8.EE.A.4)

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.4: "Perform operations with numbers expressed in scientific notation." Correctly filed.

A mixed type with two methods, not one: multiply and divide use the laws on the tens; add and subtract need the tens to match first. The lesson never says the four are "the same steps".

**Foundations first.** The opening. Then two facts, each shown once. (1) Tens multiply by adding exponents and divide by subtracting, because the exponent counts tens: on the ladder of ten, ×10² is two rungs up, ÷10² two rungs down, and dividing by 10⁻² (a hundredth) is two rungs *up* (family 2: divide by a hundredth is times a hundred). (2) You can only add things measured in the same unit: 3 hundredths and 7 thousandths are not 10 of anything, exactly as the grade-4 stack lines up the point. "Match the tens before you add. Multiply or divide anything."

**Read.** The operation sign goes gold; it decides which method. At a glance: for ÷ by a number less than 1 the answer is bigger than the top; for − of a much smaller number the answer is a little less than the first.

**The example (division, full).** "(9 × 10³) ÷ (4.5 × 10⁻²). Write in scientific notation." Choices 2 × 10³, 2 × 10⁻⁵, 2 × 10¹, 2 × 10⁵. (1) Digits with digits: 9 ÷ 4.5 = 2. (2) Tens with tens: on the ladder of ten, start at rung 3; dividing by 10⁻² is two blue steps up: rung 5. The board writes 3 − (−2) = 5 beside the ladder: subtracting a negative climbs. (3) 2 × 10⁵. The decoy 10¹ is 3 − 2, the mistake of dropping the minus; the ladder makes it a step the wrong way.

**Quick case (multiplication with a trade).** (4 × 10³) × (5 × 10²): 20 × 10⁵. Not finished: 20 is not from 1 up to 10. In the chart the 2 of 20 × 10⁵ sits in the millions: 2 × 10⁶. (3 × 10⁰ × 1 × 10⁸ is the same move with nothing to trade: 0 + 8.)

**Second example (subtraction, full; a different method).** "(9.9 × 10⁻²) − (7 × 10⁻³)." Choices 2.9 × 10⁻², 2.9 × 10⁻³, 9.2 × 10⁻³, 9.2 × 10⁻². (1) The tens differ: hundredths against thousandths. (2) Both numbers into the headed stack as decimals: 0.099 over 0.007, points aligned; the 7 sits under the second 9, not the first. (3) Rewrite on the board: 7 × 10⁻³ = 0.7 × 10⁻²: same number, same column, now the same ten. (4) 9.9 − 0.7 = 9.2; the stack reads 0.092. (5) 9.2 × 10⁻².

**Quick case (addition).** 2.95 × 10⁹ + 5.5 × 10⁸: 5.5 × 10⁸ = 0.55 × 10⁹; 2.95 + 0.55 = 3.5; 3.5 × 10⁹.

**Fast.** × and ÷: digits with digits, exponents add or subtract, then one trade if a left 1-to-10. + and −: match the tens, then digit parts add or subtract.

**Sense.** 9,000 ÷ 0.045 must be far bigger than 9,000: the exponent must beat 3. 0.099 − 0.007 is just under 0.099.

**Check.** Do it in decimals once: 9,000 ÷ 0.045 = 200,000; 0.099 − 0.007 = 0.092; then read the chart back.

**Rule out.** Division by a number under 1 makes it bigger: 10³, 10⁻⁵, 10¹ dim together. Subtracting 0.007 cannot drop 9.9 to 2.9: both 2.9s dim; 9.2 × 10⁻³ is ten times too small.

**Line.** "Match the tens before you add. Multiply or divide anything."

**Trap.** 9.9 − 7 = 2.9 with unmatched tens; 3 − 2 = 1 instead of 3 − (−2) = 5. The stack shows the 7 one column right; the ladder shows the step up.

**Kit.** `stack` with `headers: "powers"` (the place and its 10ⁿ); `ladder` base 10 with `step` up and down; `dim`. Nothing else.

**Writer.** Strongest: two methods under one name, decoys that are three different misconceptions, and the renormalizing beat.

### `scientific-notation-work` — Both notations in one story (8.EE.A.3, 8.EE.A.4)

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.4: "problems where both decimal and scientific notation are used"; 8.EE.A.3: "express how many times as much one is than the other." The richest type in the family and the only one practicing these clauses.

**Foundations first.** The opening. Then the reading lesson: this card is four questions, each row asks something different, and the slot shape says what kind of answer it wants: a pair of slots is a × 10ⁿ, a single slot is a plain number. The lesson draws the four-row slot card exactly as the app lays it out and reads each row aloud before any work.

**Read.** Row by row: "write 500,000 as a × 10ⁿ"; "write 0.005 the same way"; "how many times as much a brick as a marble": a plain number, a ratio; "the total mass of 3,200 marbles": a count times a mass, then back into scientific notation. "Use the average mass of a marble for each one" goes gold: the marble, not the brick. At a glance: 500,000 is big, so n is positive; 0.005 is small, so n is negative; the brick is thousands of times a marble; the total is a few thousand grams.

**The example** (the cargo-and-sand variant with every slot open: 500,000 g; 0.005 g; brick 4 × 10³, marble 2 × 10⁰; 3,200 marbles). (1) 500,000 into the chart: the 5 sits in the hundred-thousands, 10⁵; 5 × 10⁵. (2) 0.005 into the chart: the 5 sits in the thousandths, 10⁻³; 5 × 10⁻³. The same digit, two sizes; the place carries the sign. (3) Brick over marble: digits 4 ÷ 2 = 2; tens 10³ ÷ 10⁰: 3 − 0 = 3 (10⁰ is one, the rung before the first factor); 2 × 10³ = 2,000. The row wants a plain number: 2,000. (4) Total: 3,200 × 2 × 10⁰ = 3,200 × 2 = 6,400 grams; into the chart, the 6 sits in the thousands: 6.4 × 10³. The four answers drop into their slots.

**Fast.** Convert: the first digit's place is the exponent. Ratio: digits divided, exponents subtracted, then written out as a number. Total: multiply, then read the chart.

**Sense.** Half a million is 5 hundred-thousands. Two-thousand marbles of 2 g weigh 4,000 g, a brick. 3,200 marbles at 2 g: about 6,000 g.

**Check.** Convert back: 5 in the 10⁵ column with four zeros after is 500,000; 5 in the thousandths is 0.005. Multiply back: 2,000 × 2 = 4,000. 6.4 × 10³ = 6,400.

**Line.** "The exponent of ten is the place of the first digit."

**Trap.** 0.005 = 5 × 10⁻² (counting the zeros, or the sign dropped); the ratio written as 2 × 10³ in a one-slot row; the total left as 6400 × 10⁰; multiplying by the brick's mass. The chart gives the sign from the column; the slot card is read aloud row by row before any work.

**Kit.** `digits` with powers headers and `first`; a `problem` card that draws slot rows ([a, n] pairs and single slots) as the app does. Also flag to the app: say in the template, per row, what is asked.

**Writer.** Strongest: a four-part story with two notations and two slot shapes; the lesson is reading.

### `rg-rational-exponent-forms` — Three cases under one name (HSN.RN.A.2)

**Misconceived as one type.** Three unrelated questions share the name: a product of a radical and a rational power; the value of a negative fraction power; a negative fraction power written in radical form. Propose splitting into three types. Until then: one lesson, one shared foundation, three short cases, each on its own screen. Also: the answer 64/8 should read 8 and 4/16 should be 1/4 (section 6); the lesson shows the choices the app shows at the time of writing and reads 64/8 aloud as eight.

**Foundations first.** Define radical and rational exponent together: ⁿ√x = x^(1/n), because n of them multiplied make x; in x^(p/q), the bottom is the root, the top is the power; a minus sign is the flip (the ladder below zero). Said in one beat each over the ladder of x split into sixths; then: once a root is a power, every law applies.

**Case A (full).** "Which is equivalent to 6∛x · 2x^(1/2), x > 0?" (1) ∛x = x^(1/3). (2) Numbers with numbers: 6 · 2 = 12. (3) Same base: exponents add, as steps on the ladder add: two sixths then three sixths, five sixths: 1/3 + 1/2 = 2/6 + 3/6 = 5/6. (4) 12x^(5/6). Rule out: 8x^(5/6) (coefficients added), 12x^(2/5) (tops and bottoms added: fractions do not add that way), 12x^(1/6) (subtracted). Check: x = 64: 6 · 4 · 2 · 8 = 384 and 12 · 64^(5/6) = 12 · 2⁵ = 384.

**Case B (full, short).** "(1/4)^(−3/2)" (shown as 4/16). Flip: 4^(3/2). Root first: √4 = 2. Power: 2³ = 8. Choices read as numbers: −1/8 (sign), 1/8 (no flip), 4 (flip only, no power): dim by name. Check: 8^(2/3) = (∛8)² = 4; so 8^(−2/3) = 1/4.

**Case C (full, short).** "(x/y)^(−5/3) in radical form." Flip: (y/x)^(5/3). Bottom is the root, top the power: ∛((y/x)⁵). Decoys: the minus kept as a sign; the flip forgotten; root and power swapped. Check: for y/x = 8: ∛(8⁵) = 2⁵ = 32 = 8^(5/3).

**Fast.** Radical to power, then the laws; minus means flip, root before power.

**Sense.** Case A: the exponent grows, since two factors of x were multiplied. Case B: a base under 1 to a negative power comes out over 1.

**Line.** "Bottom is the root, top is the power, minus is the flip."

**Trap.** 1/3 + 1/2 = 2/5; −8 for a negative exponent; ⁵√(…)³ for a 5/3 power. The ladder in sixths adds steps; the flip is shown as the rung below zero, never as a sign.

**Kit.** `ladder` `split` into sixths with two blue arrows of 2 and 3 steps; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest: three cases with three decoy sets, kept short without becoming a recipe list.

### `rational-exponent-equation` — Solve x^(p/q) = k (HSA.REI.A.2)

**Misfiled.** HSA.REI.A.2, "Solve simple rational and radical equations in one variable, and give examples showing how extraneous solutions may arise": family 17 `radical-equations`. Designed here, filed there. Only one variant generates (x^(1/3) = 4); the lesson still teaches the cases, because the standard's second clause is the cases.

**Foundations first.** x^(1/3) = 4 says: the cube root of x is four. On the ladder of x split in thirds, rung 1/3 reads 4; rung 1 is three steps of ×4 up: 4, 16, 64. Undoing a power is raising to the reciprocal, because (x^(1/3))³ = x^(1/3 · 3) = x¹ (family 11: undo in reverse).

**Read.** "Solve for all real x": every real answer, and only real ones. At a glance: x is the cube of 4, so a number well above 4.

**The example.** "x^(1/3) = 4." Choices x = 12, x = 64, x = 4, x = −64 or 64. (1) Cube both sides, said aloud as the same thing to both. (2) Left: (x^(1/3))³ = x. Right: 4³ = 4 · 4 · 4 = 64, three cells on the strip. (3) x = 64. (4) Check by substituting: 64^(1/3) = ∛64 = 4, true.

**The cases card** (shared with family 17; three columns, each line checked by substitution). x^(1/3) = 4: an odd root, one answer, 64; (−64)^(1/3) = −4, not 4. x^(2/3) = 4: cube both sides, x² = 64, x = 8 or −8, and both check: (−8)^(2/3) = (∛(−8))² = 4; an even top gives two. x^(1/2) = −3: squaring gives 9, but 9^(1/2) = 3, not −3; an even root is never negative: no solution, and the 9 is what "extraneous" means. The card stays until read.

**Fast.** Raise both sides to q/p. Odd root: one answer. Even top: ±. Even root with a negative right side: none.

**Sense.** 64 is 4 cubed; 12 is far too small to have cube root 4 (12 is between 8 and 27, so its cube root is between 2 and 3).

**Check.** Substitute.

**Rule out.** 12 is 3 · 4: the exponent is a root, not a multiplier. 4 is the root itself. ±64: (−64)^(1/3) = −4 fails the check. All three dim with their reasons.

**Line.** "Undo the power with its reciprocal, then put the answer back in."

**Trap.** x = 12 (multiplying by 3); ±64 for an odd root. The ladder climbs three steps of ×4; the substitution of −64 gives −4.

**Kit.** `cases` card; `ladder` split in thirds. The `cases` card is the one piece this family has not yet built.

**Writer.** Strongest: the lesson is the cases argument, and the substitution check must be staged, not described.

### `simplify-square-root` — Simplest radical form (HSN.RN.A.2)

**Standard.** HSN.RN.A.2, "Rewrite expressions involving radicals … using the properties of exponents": √(ab) = √a · √b is the power-of-a-product law at exponent ½. The film seed √0 is a poor worked example (section 6).

**Foundations first.** The radical opening: the square and its side; √a · √a = a; the 2-by-2 array of equal squares; the prime strip with pairs bracketed. Define "simplest radical form": a whole number outside, and no square factor left under the root.

**Read.** "Simplify" (gold): the answer is a whole number times a root with nothing square left under it. At a glance: 344 is between 18² = 324 and 19² = 361, so the answer is about 18 and a half; 344 is even, so at least one 2 is in play.

**The example.** "Simplify √344." Choices √86, 4√22, 2√86, 2√22. (1) The prime strip: 344 → 2 · 172 → 2 · 2 · 86 → 2 · 2 · 2 · 43; four cells. (2) Bracket the pair of 2s in gold; the loose 2 and the 43 stay teal. (3) The square array: a square of area 344 drawn as a 2-by-2 array of squares of area 86 (2 · 43), to relative scale, areas inside; each small side is √86, so the big side is two of them. (4) The pair comes out as one 2; what is left multiplies under the root: 2 · 43 = 86. (5) 2√86.

**Quick cases.** √144: 2 · 2 · 2 · 2 · 3 · 3, every prime paired, 2 · 2 · 3 = 12, nothing left under: a 12-by-12 array of unit squares. √86: 2 · 43, no pair: it stays as it is.

**Fast.** Pair up the primes; one of each pair comes out; the rest stays under. This never stops early (√72 → 2 · 2 · 2 · 3 · 3 → 6√2 in one pass), which "find the largest square factor" sometimes does.

**Sense.** 2√86 is 2 × a bit over 9, about 18.5; √344 is between 18 and 19.

**Check.** Square it: (2√86)² = 4 · 86 = 344.

**Rule out.** Square each: (2√22)² = 88, (√86)² = 86: half the size, gone at a glance; (4√22)² = 352, not 344. Only 2√86 squares back.

**Line.** "A square factor comes out as its root. Pair up the primes."

**Trap.** 4√86 (the square factor taken out whole): the array has side 2, not 4. The lesson says once: 4 came out as 4; it should come out as 2.

**Kit.** `factors` with a `pair` action (bracket matched cells in gold, slide one of each pair out); `squares` with `gather`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, one picture with named actions, the check is arithmetic.

### `combine-square-roots` — Simplify, then add like roots (HSN.RN.A.2)

**Standard.** HSN.RN.A.2. Decoys are badly conceived (−9√13 + 3√17 against √28 + 5√63: 13 and 17 come from nowhere); the realistic ones are 6√91 (radicands added, coefficients added), 17√14, 5√7 + 3√7 = 8√7 (the 5 not multiplied through). Flagged in section 6; the lesson shows the app's choices but names the real mistakes on the board.

**Foundations first.** The radical opening and the prime strip. Then sticks: √7 is a stick of one length (to scale, about 2.65); 2√7 is two of them end to end under a bracket; a √2-stick is shorter and cannot be counted among them. "Like roots add like like terms."

**Read.** "Simplify" (gold): simplify each root first, then combine what matches. At a glance: both terms are positive, so the answer is positive; √28 is a bit over 5 and 5√63 is about 40, so the answer is about 45.

**The example.** "Simplify √28 + 5√63." (1) √28: primes 2 · 2 · 7, the pair out: 2√7. (2) √63: 3 · 3 · 7, the pair out: 3√7; the 5 in front multiplies the 3 that came out: 15√7. (3) Sticks: two √7-sticks, then fifteen more laid on the same row; one bracket over all: 17. (4) 17√7.

**Second example (a different case: unlike parts stay apart).** "−2√49 + √138." √49 = 7, a plain number: −2 · 7 = −14. 138 = 2 · 3 · 23, no pair: stays. A number and a root are different sticks: −14 + √138, and that is the answer. The decoy −21 + 2√138 changes both; nothing changed.

**Quick case.** 4√16 + 5√121: both roots are whole numbers (4, 11): 16 + 55 = 71. Just arithmetic once the roots are read.

**Fast.** Pair primes in each root; multiply what comes out by the coefficient in front; add coefficients of the same root; leave different roots alone.

**Sense.** 17√7 is about 17 × 2.65, about 45; the terms were about 5.3 and 39.7.

**Check.** Decimals: 5.29 + 39.69 = 44.98 and 17 × 2.646 = 44.98.

**Rule out.** Negative choices for a sum of positives dim at once. Radicands 13 and 17 appear in neither term. √7 alone is far too small.

**Line.** "Like roots add like like terms. Different roots stay apart."

**Trap.** √28 + √63 = √91: the sticks are 5.3 and 7.9 long; √91 is 9.5, not 13.2. And 5√63 = 5√7 or 3√7: the 5 multiplies the 3 that came out.

**Kit.** `sticks` (two rows, left-aligned, to scale); `factors` `pair`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, two clearly different cases, decoys named in this concept.

### `multiply-square-roots` — The product of two radical terms (HSN.RN.A.2)

**Standard.** HSN.RN.A.2. Decoys nonsensical (−300√5 against (6√2)(3√6)); realistic ones are 18√12 (not simplified), 18√8 (radicands added), 9√12 (coefficients added). Flagged in section 6.

**Foundations first.** The radical opening. Then the one fact: √a · √b = √(ab), because (√a · √b)² = √a√a · √b√b = ab, so √a√b is the number whose square is ab. One beat on the board. "Numbers with numbers, roots with roots."

**Read.** "Simplify" (gold): a product, then simplest form. At a glance: 6√2 is about 8.5 and 3√6 about 7.3, so the answer is about 62; both positive, so positive.

**The example.** "Simplify (6√2)(3√6)." (1) Numbers with numbers: 6 · 3 = 18. (2) Roots with roots: √2 · √6 = √12. (3) Faster than computing 12: the prime strip of both radicands side by side, 2 | 2 · 3; a pair of 2s across the two roots comes out as 2; the 3 stays. (4) 18 · 2 = 36 outside, √3 under. (5) 36√3.

**Quick case (signs, and a root that is a whole number).** (−√49)(−6√95): √49 = 7; −7 · −6 = 42 (two negatives, family 3); 95 = 5 · 19, no pair: 42√95. For (3√10)(−√1): √1 = 1, so −3√10.

**Fast.** Coefficients multiply with their signs; write both radicands' primes in one strip, pair across both; what is left multiplies under the root. Never compute the big radicand first (√46 · √88: 2 · 23 | 2 · 2 · 2 · 11 → two pairs → 4√253).

**Sense.** 36√3 is about 36 × 1.73, about 62.

**Check.** Square both sides: (36√3)² = 1296 · 3 = 3888; (6√2)² · (3√6)² = 72 · 54 = 3888.

**Rule out.** Negative choices for two positive factors dim together; radicand 5 appears nowhere; 4√3 is about 7, far smaller than either factor.

**Line.** "Numbers with numbers, roots with roots, then pair up the primes."

**Trap.** 18√12 left as the answer (not finished); √2 · √6 = √8. The strip pairs across the two roots so there is nothing left to simplify; the fact (√a√b)² = ab is shown once.

**Kit.** `factors` with `pair` across two groups. Nothing else.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, the check is arithmetic.

### `rationalize-radical-denominator` — A rational denominator (HSN.RN.A.2)

**Standard.** HSN.RN.A.2. Decoys (2/11 √22 against 10/√13) come from nowhere; realistic are 10/13 (root dropped), 10√13/169 (denominator squared), 10√13/√13 (not finished). Flagged in section 6.

**Foundations first.** Define "rationalize": write the fraction so no root is on the bottom. Two facts, each one beat. Family 9: multiplying top and bottom by the same thing changes nothing (1/2 = 2/4, the bar re-cut). The definition: √91 · √91 = 91. So multiply top and bottom by the root that is on the bottom, and the bottom becomes a whole number.

**Read.** "Rationalize and simplify" (gold): the answer has a whole number below, a root above, and the fraction reduced. At a glance: 7/(3√91) is a small positive number: √91 is about 9.5, so about 7/28.6, about a quarter.

**The example.** "Rationalize the denominator and simplify 7/(3√91)." (1) Is √91 already simplest? 91 = 7 · 13, no pair: yes. (2) Multiply top and bottom by √91 only, not by 3√91: the 3 is already a whole number. (3) Top: 7√91. Bottom: 3 · √91 · √91 = 3 · 91 = 273. (4) Reduce: 273 = 7 · 39, so 7√91/273 = √91/39. (5) √91/39, written as the app writes it, (1/39)√91.

**Quick case.** 10/√13: times √13 over √13: 10√13/13; 10 and 13 share nothing, done.

**Fast.** Multiply top and bottom by the root alone; replace root times itself with the radicand; reduce the whole-number part.

**Sense.** √91/39 is about 9.54/39, about 0.245; the original, 7/(3 × 9.54), is about 0.245.

**Check.** Multiply the answer by the original denominator and recover the numerator: (√91/39) · 3√91 = 3 · 91/39 = 273/39 = 7.

**Rule out.** A root still below dims; 2/11 √22 and 3/11 √22 have a radicand from nowhere; 11/182 √91 is about 0.58, more than twice the size.

**Line.** "A root times itself is the number. Top and bottom, the same thing."

**Trap.** Multiplying only the bottom (10/13), or writing 13² below. The fraction bar re-cut as 1/2 = 2/4 shows both must change; √13 · √13 = 13 is said once, on the definition.

**Kit.** Board only; the bar from family 9 for the one "same thing top and bottom" beat if wanted.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: a short board lesson with an exact check.

### `rational-power` — Evaluate a fraction power (HSN.RN.A.2, carrying A.1)

**Standard.** HSN.RN.A.2, "Rewrite expressions involving … rational exponents using the properties of exponents." The lesson carries HSN.RN.A.1's explanation (why a fraction exponent is a root) because the app never asks for it and the learner must own it anyway. Pilot lesson 5.

**Foundations first.** The split ladder, built live. Ladder of 9: rung 0 is 1, rung 1 is 9, one blue arrow ×9 between. Split that step into two equal steps: together they multiply by 9, so each is ×3; the half rung reads 9^(1/2) = 3. Said as the law demanding it: (9^(1/2))² = 9^(1/2 · 2) = 9, so 9^(1/2) must be the number whose square is 9. Then the ladder of 8 split in three: ×2, ×2, ×2; 8^(1/3) = 2 and 8^(2/3) = 4, two of the three steps. So in m/n: the bottom is how many equal steps the whole rung is cut into (the root); the top is how many of them to climb (the power). A minus sign is the flip, the rung below zero. Relabel: the ladder of 8 in thirds *is* the ladder of 2 in wholes.

**Read.** "Evaluate" (gold): one exact number. At a glance: the base 1/243 is under 1 and the exponent 3/5 is positive, so the answer is between 1/243 and 1; 243 ends in 3 and is 3 · 81, so the ladder of 3 is in play.

**The example.** "Evaluate (1/243)^(3/5)." (1) A fraction to a power is top and bottom each to the power (family 9): 1 stays 1. (2) Bottom first: the fifth root of 243. Climb the ladder of 3: 3, 9, 27, 81, 243, five steps; so 243 = 3⁵ and its fifth root is 3. The ladder relabels: 243 in fifths is 3 in wholes. (3) Top: three steps: 3³ = 27. (4) 1/27.

**Second example (a different case: the flip).** "Evaluate 27^(−1/3)." Flip first: (1/27)^(1/3). Root: the cube root of 27 is 3 (3, 9, 27: three steps). No power to take. 1/3.

**Fast.** Flip if negative; root first (the bottom), then power (the top). Or write the base as a power of a small number and multiply exponents: 243^(3/5) = (3⁵)^(3/5) = 3³.

**Sense.** A base under 1 to a positive power stays under 1; a negative exponent on 27 makes something under 1. Root first keeps numbers small: 243³ is over fourteen million and nobody needs it.

**Check.** Raise it back: (1/27)⁵ = 1/3¹⁵ and (1/243)³ = 1/3¹⁵, as the definition requires. 27^(1/3) = 3, so 27^(−1/3) = 1/3.

**Line.** "Bottom is the root, top is the power, minus is the flip. Root first."

**Trap.** 27^(1/3) = 9 (dividing by three); −3 for the negative exponent; power before root. The split ladder shows three equal ×3 steps, not a division; the flip is a rung below zero, never a sign.

**Kit.** `ladder` with `split` (equal arrows between rungs 0 and 1) and `relabel`; `mark` for the gold rung. From the family's build list.

**Writer.** Strongest: the foundation is the whole lesson and the split motion is the proof.

### `symbolic-rational-exponent` — Roots as powers (HSN.RN.A.2)

**Standard.** HSN.RN.A.2, "Rewrite expressions involving radicals and rational exponents." Rendering: the app shows √[2]{x¹}; it should show √x (section 6). The lesson writes √x.

**Foundations first.** From the split ladder, in one beat each: ⁿ√x = x^(1/n), because n of them multiplied make x; ⁿ√(x^m) = x^(m/n), the bottom is the root and the top is the power; 1 over a power is the rung below zero: 1/x^a = x^(−a), the sign flips, not the fraction. A root with no index is index 2; a bare x is x¹.

**Read.** "Equivalent for x > 0" (gold on equivalent): the same number for every positive x. At a glance: a "1 over" means a negative exponent; a root means a fraction with the index on the bottom.

**The example.** "For x > 0, which is equivalent to 1/⁵√(x³)?" Choices x^(3/5), x⁸, x^(5/3), x^(−3/5). (1) The root: ⁵√(x³) = x^(3/5): on the ladder of x cut in fifths, three steps up. (2) One over it: the same three fifths of a step, but below zero: x^(−3/5). (3) Check with x = 32 = 2⁵: ⁵√(32³) = 2³ = 8, so the expression is 1/8; and 32^(−3/5) = 1/2³ = 1/8.

**Quick case (the exponent is a fraction, and fractions reduce).** "Which is equivalent to x^(1/2)?" Choices √(x²), ⁴√(x²), √(x⁴), ⁴√x. Turn each into a power: x, x^(2/4), x², x^(1/4); 2/4 = 1/2. ⁴√(x²). Check x = 16: ⁴√256 = 4 = √16. One beat for √x = x^(1/2): index 2, power 1.

**Fast.** Bottom is the root, top is the power; "one over" flips the sign; reduce the exponent fraction.

**Sense.** x = 32 makes every fifth root a whole number; the answer with a negative exponent must be a fraction under 1, and 1/8 is.

**Check.** Substitute a base that is a perfect power (32, 64, 16).

**Rule out.** x⁸ is 3 + 5, not a power of a root; x^(5/3) swaps root and power; x^(3/5) is the root without the "one over". They dim with their names.

**Line.** "Bottom is the root, top is the power. One over flips the sign, not the fraction."

**Trap.** 1/x^(3/5) = x^(5/3): flipping the fraction instead of the sign. The ladder puts x^(−3/5) three fifths below zero and x^(5/3) well above one; x = 32 gives 1/8 against 32^(5/3), which is far bigger than 32.

**Kit.** `ladder` split in fifths with rungs below zero; `dim`. Rendering fix: √x, x not x¹.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, the substitution check settles every choice.
