# Exponents, roots and scientific notation: concepts, part 2

Ten problem types: the six of `exponent-laws` and four of `scientific-notation`. Each stands on the family design (the factor strip, the power ladder, the chart of ten, the five habits), says what must be built before the first example, and gives the one example that makes the idea visible.

Three notes that apply to several types:

- **The three law types open the same way.** As every decimal-fractions film in place value opens with the same twenty-second definition, `product-of-powers-exponent`, `quotient-of-powers-exponent` and `power-of-power-exponent` each open with the same thirty-second strip foundation (below, under the first of them), then work their own law. Every variant of all three carries a negative exponent, so foundation 6 (a negative exponent counts factors below a fraction bar) is part of that opening, not an afterthought.
- **"For every nonzero real x."** The app's wording. Said once in passing in each law film: "x can be any number except zero, because a negative exponent puts x under a fraction bar, and nothing is divided by zero." Then the phrase is never mentioned again.
- **"Move the decimal point."** The app's method text for the scientific-notation types teaches the point move as the method. The family teaches the chart of ten (the first digit's place carries its sign with it) and the point move only as the check, because the sign of the exponent is exactly where children fail. The method text should follow the lessons; flagged in each.

## Exponent laws (grade 8)

### The shared opening for the three law films (foundations 1, 5, 6)

Built live before the first example, in this order, one idea per beat with a pause after each:

1. **An exponent counts factors.** x⁵ unfolds on the strip into five teal cells, x · x · x · x · x, a gold bracket above reading "5 factors", x⁵ beside it. "The base is what repeats. The exponent is how many." Names said in passing: base, exponent, power.
2. **A negative exponent counts factors below a bar.** The ladder of x, three rungs: x², x¹, x⁰ = 1, each step down ÷x. "Divide by x again": x⁻¹ = 1/x appears, drawn on the strip as one x cell under a fraction bar with 1 above. "A negative exponent is a count of factors on the bottom. It is not a minus sign." The ladder leaves; the bar stays.
3. **x over x is one.** One cell above a bar, one below; the pair dims to muted on the word "one". "The same thing on top and bottom is one, so it goes."

From here the film works its law. The product film uses join and the bar; the quotient film uses the bar and cancel; the power film uses stack.

### `product-of-powers-exponent` — Multiply powers of one base: the counts add.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.1, "know and apply the properties of integer exponents to generate equivalent ... expressions." The standard's own example is numerical (3² × 3⁻⁵); this type practices the property in the letter x and asks only for the exponent k. The catalog's HSA.SSE.A.2 link is a stretch; the type is 8.EE.A.1.

**Foundations first.** The shared opening. Then one line: "k is an exponent. The question asks for a count, not a value."

**Read.** "x⁴ × x⁶ = x^k. What is k?" The k goes gold: the answer is a count of factors. At a glance: same base, multiplied, so every factor is an x and the answer is how many there are. With a negative exponent in play, some of those factors are on the bottom and the count comes down.

**The example.** x⁴ × x⁶ = x^k. (1) x⁴ unfolds into four teal cells under a bracket "4 factors"; x⁶ into six rust cells, "6 factors". (2) The two strips slide end to end (join); one bracket stretches across all ten and the count rewrites as 4 + 6 = 10, gold. (3) The strip folds: x¹⁰. (4) On the board: x⁴ · x⁶ = x^(4+6) = x¹⁰, k = 10. (5) The wrong picture, one beat on a `watch` card that stays: "x²⁴ would need twenty-four x's. There are ten. Multiply the powers, add the counts."

**Second example, the different case** (the bar): x⁻² × x³ = x^k. (1) x⁻² is two cells below a bar; x³ three above. (2) Cancel two pairs on counted words ("x over x is one, one"), leaving one cell above. (3) x¹, k = 1; the board writes −2 + 3 = 1: "adding the exponents, signs and all, is the same count." Quick beat, three or four beats, for both negative: x⁻² × x⁻⁴: six cells, all below the bar, nothing to cancel: x⁻⁶; −2 + (−4) = −6. "Both below: the count stays negative."

**Fast.** Add the exponents with their signs. Why it always works: every factor is an x; those on top count up, those below count down.

**Sense.** More factors above than below means a positive k (3 above, 2 below: k = 1, small and positive).

**Check.** Let x = 2: 2⁴ · 2⁶ = 16 · 64 = 1,024 = 2¹⁰. For the second: (1/4) · 8 = 2 = 2¹. Or write the factors out and count.

**Line.** "Multiply the powers: count all the factors. Add."

**Trap.** x⁴ · x⁶ = x²⁴ (exponents multiplied), and with a negative exponent the sign lost: x⁵ · x⁻¹ written as x⁶. The join shows ten cells; the bar shows the −1 as one factor below that cancels one above, so x⁵ · x⁻¹ is visibly four.

**Kit.** `factors` join, `top`/`bottom` with `cancel`. The leftover count after a cancel must be written with its sign ("1 above" or "6 below → x⁻⁶"). Nothing beyond the family's tools.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, from this concept: one method, one component, the math check catches slips.

### `quotient-of-powers-exponent` — Divide powers of one base: top minus bottom.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.1, as above; the subtraction clause of the properties. HSA.SSE.A.2 is a stretch.

**Foundations first.** The shared opening, then two more beats: (a) **Dividing puts the divisor's factors below the bar.** x⁶ ÷ x⁴: six cells above, four below; cancel four pairs; two left above: x². "Top minus bottom: 6 − 4 = 2. That is the law." (b) **If the divisor's factors are already below, dividing flips them up.** x⁻¹ is one x below; dividing by 1/x is multiplying by x (family 9: dividing by a fraction). "Dividing flips the divisor's factors across the bar." These two beats are the whole law.

**Read.** "x² ÷ x⁻¹ = x^k. What is k?" k gold; the answer is a count. At a glance: same base, divided; the divisor's exponent is negative, so the answer is bigger than 2, not smaller.

**The example.** x² ÷ x⁻¹. (1) x² unfolds: two cells above the bar. (2) The divisor x⁻¹ appears as one rust cell below its own bar; "divide by it": the cell flips across to the top and turns teal. (3) Three above, nothing below: x³. (4) Board: 2 − (−1) = 2 + 1 = 3, k = 3. "Subtracting a negative adds, because dividing by a bottom factor puts it on top."

**Second example, the different case** (both land below, nothing cancels): x⁻⁶ ÷ x⁴. Six cells below; dividing by x⁴ sends four more below; ten below, all one side: x⁻¹⁰; −6 − 4 = −10. `watch` card: "Not −2. Six below and four more below is ten below. Top minus bottom, even when it goes past zero." Quick beat: x⁰ ÷ x⁴: nothing above (x⁰ = 1, the rung before the first factor), four below: x⁻⁴. The case x⁻⁵ ÷ x⁻³ (flip up, cancel three, two left below: −2) is the method and needs no example.

**Fast.** Top exponent minus bottom exponent, signs included.

**Sense.** Dividing by a fraction (x⁻¹) makes the result bigger, so k > 2. Dividing by x⁴ pushes everything down, so k is more negative than −6.

**Check.** x = 2: 4 ÷ (1/2) = 8 = 2³. Second: (1/64) ÷ 16 = 1/1,024 = 2⁻¹⁰.

**Line.** "Divide: top minus bottom, signs and all. The divisor's factors cross the bar."

**Trap.** x⁻⁶ ÷ x⁴ = x⁻² (big minus small, the sign waved away), x² ÷ x⁻¹ = x¹. Ten cells below the bar is the first; the one cell flipping up is the second.

**Kit.** `factors` top/bottom and cancel; one new motion, **flip**: a cell crossing the bar (the divisor's below-cells move above). Specify it as `{"do":"flip","target":"f.g1","at":"divide"}`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept.

### `power-of-power-exponent` — A power raised to a power: rows of factors, so multiply.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.1; the power-of-a-power clause. HSA.SSE.A.2 is a stretch.

**Foundations first.** The shared opening, then: **parentheses make the inside one thing.** (x⁵)³ means x⁵, three times: three cells each holding x⁵; each opens into five x's; three rows of five (stack), counted down the side 5, 10, 15: x¹⁵. "Rows of factors. Multiply." Then: a negative outer exponent puts the rows below the bar; a zero outer exponent is no rows at all, which is 1 = x⁰.

**Read.** "(x⁵)⁻² = x^k. What is k?" k gold. At a glance: the outer exponent is negative, so the whole thing lands below the bar and k is negative; its size is 5 × 2.

**The example.** (x⁵)⁻². (1) Inner power x⁵ unfolds to five cells, bracket "5 factors". (2) Outer exponent −2: "two rows, below the bar": the strip copies into two rows under a fraction bar, side count 5, 10. (3) Ten factors below: x⁻¹⁰. (4) Board: 5 × (−2) = −10, k = −10. `watch` card: "Not x³. Five plus negative two is adding, which is for multiplying powers. Here one power is raised to another: rows, so multiply."

**Second example, the different case** (negative inside and out): (x⁻²)⁻². x⁻² is two cells below; a negative outer exponent flips the inside across the bar (the quotient film's flip) and then raises it to the positive power: two rows of two above: x⁴; (−2)(−2) = 4. "Negative times negative is positive: two flips land on top." Quick beat: (x⁻¹)⁰: zero rows: 1 = x⁰; "anything to the zero is one." (x⁴)¹: one row, covered by the method.

**Fast.** Multiply the exponents, signs included.

**Sense.** A negative outer exponent sends everything below (k negative); two negatives, back above (k positive).

**Check.** x = 2: (2⁵)⁻² = 1/32² = 1/1,024 = 2⁻¹⁰. Second: (1/4)⁻² = 16 = 2⁴. Or count the cells in the rows.

**Line.** "Power of a power: rows of factors. Multiply."

**Trap.** (x⁵)⁻² = x³ (exponents added) or x⁻⁷. The stack shows ten cells, not three; the rows are the array the child knows from grade 3.

**Kit.** `factors` stack with the side count; stack below a bar; the flip from the quotient film.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept.

### `rg-exponent-monomial-power` — A monomial to a power, alone or times another monomial.

**Flag first.** Tagged HSA.APR.A.1 and HSA.SSE.A.2; it is 8.EE.A.1 and nothing more (the family design, section 6). It shares its sort motion with `negative-monomial-power`; that film owns the sign, this one owns the bare letter and the second monomial.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.1, "apply the properties of integer exponents to generate equivalent ... expressions": the power of a product, then the product of powers.

**Foundations first.** Coefficient defined in passing ("the number in front"). The strip opening, shortened: an exponent counts factors. Then the one new idea, built: **the exponent reaches every factor inside the parentheses.** (−2x²y³)⁴ unfolds into four cells each holding −2x²y³ ("the inside, four times"). "A letter with no exponent written has exponent one; it is one factor." That line is said here, before any example, because the second case turns on it.

**Read.** "Which expression is equivalent to (−2x²y³)⁴?" with its four choices. "Equivalent" gold: the same value for every x and y, written as one monomial. At a glance: the coefficient must be raised too, and (−2) to an even power is positive; every letter's exponent is a multiple of 4.

**The example.** (−2x²y³)⁴; choices 16x⁸y¹² | 16x⁶y⁷ | −2x⁸y¹² | 16x⁹y¹². (1) Unfold: four cells of −2x²y³. (2) Sort: each cell breaks into its factors and like factors gather under their own brackets: four −2s; four x²'s, each two x's, eight x cells (the stack: four rows of two); twelve y's. (3) Four −2s: run the product, −2, 4, −8, 16: "an even count of negatives is positive." (4) Board: (−2)⁴ · x^(2·4) · y^(3·4) = 16x⁸y¹². Rule out before boxing: −2x⁸y¹² left the coefficient alone; x⁶y⁷ added 4 instead of multiplying; x⁹: nine is not four twos. Dim together, one phrase each. Box 16x⁸y¹².

**Second example, the different case** (a second monomial, and the bare letter): (2x³)²(−3xy³); choices −12x⁸y³ | −6x⁷y³ | −12x⁷y³ | −12x⁶y³. (1) The power first: (2x³)² = 4x⁶ (two cells sorted: 2 · 2 = 4, six x's). (2) Times −3xy³: coefficients 4 · (−3) = −12; x⁶ joins with one bare x: seven x's; y³ alone. (3) −12x⁷y³. Rule out: −6 forgot to square the 2; x⁶ forgot the bare x is one factor; x⁸ added the outer 2 to the x again after it was done.

**Fast.** Coefficient to the power (sign by odd or even count), each exponent times the power, a bare letter is exponent 1; then coefficients multiply and exponents of the same letter add.

**Sense.** Sign from the count of negatives; after a power, every exponent is a multiple of the outer exponent.

**Check.** x = 2, y = 1: (−2 · 4 · 1)⁴ = (−8)⁴ = 4,096; 16 · 2⁸ = 16 · 256 = 4,096. Second: (2 · 8)² · (−3 · 2) = 256 · (−6) = −1,536; −12 · 2⁷ = −1,536.

**Rule out.** Coefficient not raised (the original number or sign still showing): gone. In a single power, any exponent not a multiple of the outer power: gone. Sign by the count of negatives.

**Line.** "The exponent reaches every factor inside: the number, the sign, every letter. A bare letter is to the one."

**Trap.** The coefficient left alone (−2x⁸y¹²), exponents added (x⁶y⁷), and the bare x counted as nothing (x⁶ in the second case). The four −2 cells and the one lone x cell joining six others make all three impossible.

**Kit.** `factors` with TeX cells (−2x²y³), sort, stack, join; `dim` for choices. The sort must show the breaking of a cell into its factors, not just the result.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept, with one caution: the sort must be staged as a motion (cells break, like factors gather), never described in words over a static picture.

### `exponent-quotient-slots` — Divide one monomial by another: coefficient and exponent into slots.

**Flags first.** The problem renders 4y¹; a learner should see 4y, never y¹ (template fix, as the family flagged for x¹ elsewhere; if it stays, the lesson says "y to the one is y" in passing). The answer slots are labeled with letters that mean nothing to the learner (`{"coef":"c","exp":"a"}`); the film shows the two blanks exactly as the app lays them out. Keep the negative-exponent case (20w⁶/4w¹²) the common one: it is the trap.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.1; the quotient of powers with a coefficient.

**Foundations first.** Coefficient defined. **Numbers with numbers, letters with letters**: 20w⁶ over 4w¹² is (20/4) · (w⁶/w¹²), because factors can be regrouped (family 7). The quotient film's bar and cancel in one beat: w over w is one.

**Read.** "Write 20w⁶/4w¹² in the form c w^p. Complete the sentence." Two blanks: a coefficient c and an exponent p, both gold in turn. At a glance: 20 ÷ 4 is 5; there are more w's below than above, so p is negative and the answer is 5 over a power of w.

**The example.** 20w⁶/4w¹². (1) Split: 20/4 · w⁶/w¹²; 20 ÷ 4 = 5 on the board. (2) The strip: six w cells above the bar, twelve below. (3) Cancel six pairs on counted words; six remain below. (4) Six below is 1/w⁶ = w⁻⁶; the board writes 6 − 12 = −6. (5) c = 5, p = −6 drop into their slots: 5w⁻⁶. `watch` card: "Not 6. Twelve below take all six above and leave six below. Top minus bottom, even past zero." Quick beat, the other case: 12y⁵/4y: five above, one below; four remain above: 3y⁴ (c = 3, p = 4). 6x²/2x³ (one left below: 3x⁻¹) is the main case again and needs no example.

**Fast.** Divide the coefficients; top exponent minus bottom exponent with its sign.

**Sense.** More w's below than above: the exponent is negative and the answer is a fraction, 5/w⁶.

**Check.** Multiply back: 5w⁻⁶ · 4w¹² = 20w⁶ (coefficients 5 · 4; exponents −6 + 12 = 6).

**Line.** "Numbers with numbers, letters with letters. Top minus bottom, even past zero."

**Trap.** p = 6 (the bigger exponent minus the smaller). The cancel leaves six cells visibly below the bar; the side the leftovers are on gives the sign.

**Kit.** `factors` top/bottom with cancel. The board needs the problem card with two labeled blanks as the app shows it ("c = □, p = □"), filled by `answer` into each; if the board cannot draw a slot sentence, that is a small addition.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept.

### `negative-monomial-power` — A term with a negative coefficient to a power: the exponent reaches the sign.

**Flag first.** Tagged HSA.APR.A.1 and HSA.SSE.A.2; it is 8.EE.A.1 (family design, section 6). Same method as the first case of `rg-exponent-monomial-power`; this film owns the sign.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.1; the power of a product, with a signed coefficient.

**Foundations first.** The exponent counts factors (one beat on the strip). **The exponent reaches everything inside the parentheses, the sign too**: (−4n²)³ is −4n² three times: three cells each holding −4n². Family 3's fact named in one line: an odd count of negative factors makes a negative product; an even count, a positive one.

**Read.** "Which expression is equivalent to (−4n²)³?" with four choices. "Equivalent" gold. At a glance: three negatives, so the answer is negative; the −4 is raised too, so 4 does not appear as it is; the exponent of n is a multiple of 3.

**The example.** (−4n²)³; choices 64n⁶ | −4n⁶ | −64n⁶ | −64n⁵. (1) Unfold: three cells of −4n². (2) Sort: three −4s under one bracket, three n²'s under another, which open into six n cells (three rows of two). (3) Run the −4s: −4, then 16, then −64; the sign flips at each step and ends negative: "three negatives: negative." (4) n² three times: 2 · 3 = 6, n⁶. (5) Board: (−4)³ · n^(2·3) = −64n⁶. Rule out, together, one phrase each: 64n⁶ lost the sign (three negatives, not two); −4n⁶ left the coefficient alone; −64n⁵ added the exponents. Box −64n⁶.

**Second example, the different case** (even power, so positive), quick, four beats: (−4a⁴)⁴; choices 4a¹⁶ | 256a⁸ | −256a¹⁶ | 256a¹⁶. Four −4s run −4, 16, −64, 256: "an even count: positive." a⁴ four times: a¹⁶. 256a¹⁶. Rule out: 4a¹⁶ (coefficient not raised), 256a⁸ (added), −256a¹⁶ (even count is positive).

**Fast.** Coefficient to the power, sign by odd or even; the letter's exponent times the power.

**Sense.** Odd exponent on a negative inside: negative. Even: positive. The letter's exponent is a multiple of the outer one.

**Check.** n = 1: (−4)³ = −64 for the number and sign; count the n cells: six.

**Rule out.** Wrong sign for the count of negatives: gone. The original coefficient still showing: gone. A letter exponent that is not a multiple of the power: gone.

**Line.** "The exponent reaches the sign too. Odd count of negatives: negative."

**Trap.** −4n⁶ (the coefficient left alone) and 64n⁶ (the sign lost). The run −4, 16, −64 shows the sign at every step and the three −4 cells show the coefficient is raised.

**Kit.** `factors` unfold, sort, run with a signed running product; `dim`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept.

## Scientific notation (grade 8)

### `mcas-sci-notation-select` — Which equations write a digit times a power of ten correctly?

**Flag first.** Filed under 6.EE.A.1 as well; the 6.EE link is only that the exponents are whole numbers. It is 8.EE.A.3's form, read into a numeral. The method text teaches the point move; the lesson teaches the place.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.3, "use numbers expressed in the form of a single digit times an integer power of 10": this type practices reading that form as a standard numeral.

**Foundations first.** **The ladder of ten** (foundation 8), three rungs up from 10⁰ = 1: 10¹ = 10, 10² = 100, 10³ = 1,000, each step ×10, one more zero; "the exponent counts the zeros, because each ten adds a place." **The chart of ten**: the place-value chart with each column named twice, THOUSANDS and 10³. "A digit times ten to the n: the digit sits in the ten-to-the-n column." Built with 7 × 10⁴: the 7 drops into the ten-thousands column; empty places fill with zeros; 70,000. Said in passing: scientific notation (digits times size), standard numeral.

**Read.** "Each equation writes a number in scientific notation as a standard numeral. Choose the 3 equations that are true." The 3 and "true" go gold: each equation is judged on its own, and exactly three survive. At a glance: 28 and 35 are 7 × 4 and 7 × 5; a power of ten here is at least a thousand, so those two are nonsense. That leaves four, one of which is wrong.

**The example.** The one variant: 2×10³ = 2,000 | 7×10⁴ = 28 | 3×10⁵ = 300,000 | 3×10⁶ = 3,000,000 | 7×10⁵ = 35 | 2×10⁶ = 200,000. (1) Dim 28 and 35 together: "seven times ten to the fourth is seventy thousand, not twenty-eight. The exponent is not a multiplier." (2) The remaining four, one at a time in the chart: the 2 in the thousands: 2,000, true. The 3 in the hundred thousands: 300,000, true. The 3 in the millions: 3,000,000, true. (3) 2 × 10⁶: the 2 in the millions reads 2,000,000; the equation says 200,000, the hundred thousands: false; dims. (4) The three true ones marked.

**Fast.** A single digit times 10ⁿ is that digit followed by n zeros. Always true when the digit part is a single digit and n is zero or more, which is every variant here; the film says that condition aloud once.

**Sense.** 10⁴ is ten thousand; seven of them is seventy thousand, nowhere near 28.

**Check.** Exactly three chosen; each read aloud in words ("three times one hundred thousand is three hundred thousand") and the zeros counted against the exponent.

**Rule out.** A right side smaller than its power of ten (28, 35) is the exponent used as a multiplier: gone together. Then count zeros on the rest.

**Line.** "A digit times ten to the n: the digit, then n zeros."

**Trap.** 7 × 10⁴ = 28, the exponent multiplied. The ladder of ten makes ×10⁴ four shifts, never ×4. Second: one zero off (2 × 10⁶ = 200,000); the chart column names the place.

**Kit.** `digits` with powers headers; a six-choice select-many card with equations (the wrapped-pill layout already handles long choices); `dim`; a mark on the chosen choices.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept.

### `scientific-to-standard` — From a × 10ⁿ to the plain number.

**Flag first.** The only generated variant has a negative exponent; confirm positive-exponent variants exist (the lesson covers one as a quick case because the learner will meet it). The method text ("move the decimal point") should follow the lesson: the chart is the method, the point move the check.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.4 ("interpret scientific notation") and the reading prerequisite for 8.EE.A.3; 8.EE.A.1 for the meaning of 10⁻⁴.

**Foundations first.** **The ladder of ten down past zero**: 10¹ = 10, 10⁰ = 1, 10⁻¹ = 1/10 = 0.1, 10⁻² = 0.01, 10⁻³ = 0.001, 10⁻⁴ = 0.0001, each step ÷10. "A negative exponent on ten is a place to the right of the ones." **The chart of ten** with both names on each column, from thousands to millionths, the point line between ones and tenths. The rule, built once with 3 × 10²: the 3 sits in the ones; times 10² is two shifts left; it lands in the hundreds and reads 300. "A number times ten to the n: its first digit lands in the ten-to-the-n column; the other digits follow in order; empty places get zeros." Standard form named in passing: the plain number, every place written.

**Read.** "Write 4.282 × 10⁻⁴ in standard form." "Standard form" gold: a plain decimal. At a glance: the exponent is negative, so the number is smaller than 4.282 and less than 1; it starts "zero point", and the digits 4, 2, 8, 2 stay in that order.

**The example.** 4.282 × 10⁻⁴. (1) 4.282 in the chart: 4 in the ones, 2, 8, 2 in tenths, hundredths, thousandths. (2) "Times ten to the negative four: four shifts right." Four blue steps, a gold counter ticking 1, 2, 3, 4; the 4 slides from the ones into the ten-thousandths column, the other digits following. (3) The emptied places fill with muted zeros: ones, tenths, hundredths, thousandths. (4) Read the chart: 0.0004282. (5) The check in one beat: "the 4 is in the ten-thousandths, which is ten to the negative four. Four shifts." Then, said once as the familiar rule: "That is what 'move the point four places left' means: the same picture, seen from the point." Quick second case, four beats: 3.5 × 10⁶: the 3 lands in the millions, the 5 follows, five zeros fill to the ones: 3,500,000.

**Fast.** Put the first digit in the 10ⁿ column, let the rest follow, fill the empty places with zeros, read.

**Sense.** A negative exponent means small: the answer begins 0.000; a positive one means large, with commas.

**Check.** Read the first nonzero digit's column: ten-thousandths, 10⁻⁴. Or count the shifts back: four places right returns 4.282.

**Line.** "The first digit lands in the ten-to-the-n column; the rest follow; zeros fill the gaps."

**Trap.** 0.00004282 (four zeros after the point, the exponent counted as zeros) or 0.004282. In the chart three places are empty between the point and the 4, and the 4 is in the fourth; the zeros are what is empty, not the exponent.

**Kit.** `digits` with powers headers from 10⁶ to 10⁻⁷, `slide` with the blue steps and gold counter, muted fill zeros.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept.

### `standard-to-scientific` — From a plain number to a × 10ⁿ. (Pilot lesson 4.)

**Flag first.** The method text teaches "count the places the point moves" with a sign rule bolted on; the lesson teaches the place of the first digit, which carries its sign, and uses the count only as the check.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.4 and 8.EE.A.3, "numbers expressed in the form of a single digit times an integer power of 10": writing the form. (The variants' digit parts 8.6, 4.3, 2.58 go past a single digit, which 8.EE.A.4's operations need.)

**Foundations first.** **Scientific notation defined**: a × 10ⁿ, where a has exactly one nonzero digit before the point (from 1 up to, not including, 10) and n is an integer. "Digits times size: a carries the digits, n says where they sit." Why those limits, in one line: with one digit before the point, every number has exactly one such name. **The chart of ten**, headers both ways, from billions to millionths. **The rule, built from the last lesson run backwards**: 8.6 × 10⁻⁵ puts the 8 in the 10⁻⁵ column; so if the 8 is in the 10⁻⁵ column, the exponent is −5. "The exponent of ten is the place of the first nonzero digit." Shown with 300: the 3 is in the hundreds, 10²: 3 × 10². A pause.

**Read.** "Write 0.000086 in scientific notation." with its four choices. "Scientific notation" gold: a digit part from 1 up to 10, times a power of ten. At a glance: the number is small, so the exponent is negative; the digits are 8 and 6, so a is 8.6.

**The example.** 0.000086; choices 86 × 10⁻⁶ | 8.6 × 10⁻⁵ | 0.86 × 10⁻⁴ | 8.6 × 10⁴. (1) The number in the chart, digit by digit: tenths 0, hundredths 0, thousandths 0, ten-thousandths 0, hundred-thousandths 8, millionths 6. (2) The first nonzero digit's column goes gold: HUNDRED-THOUSANDTHS, 10⁻⁵. "The 8 lives in the ten to the negative fifth place." (3) a: the digits with the point after the first, 8.6. (4) 8.6 × 10⁻⁵. (5) Rule out before boxing: 86 and 0.86 are not from 1 up to 10: not scientific notation; 10⁴ says the number is in the ten thousands: this number is tiny. Three dim together, one phrase each. (6) Box. (7) Check: 8.6 in the chart slides five places right; the 8 lands in the hundred-thousandths: 0.000086. Said once: "counting those five shifts is the check; the place is the method, because the place knows its sign."

**Second example, the different case** (large, positive exponent, dropped zeros), brief: 2,580,000,000; the 2 is in the billions, 10⁹; a is 2.58, the digits that are not placeholder zeros; 2.58 × 10⁹. Rule out: 10⁻⁹ is tiny; 10¹⁰ is the ten billions and the 2 is not there; 25.8 is not from 1 up to 10.

**Fast.** Read the first nonzero digit's place: that is n, sign and all. Write the digits with the point after the first: that is a.

**Sense.** Big number, positive n; small number, negative n; a number from 1 up to 10, n = 0. The size of n is how far the first digit is from the ones.

**Check.** Shift a back by n places and read the chart; it must be the number given.

**Rule out.** A digit part with no digit before the point, or two: gone. A sign that disagrees with the size: gone. One left; prove it from the chart.

**Line.** "The exponent of ten is the place of the first digit."

**Trap.** 86 × 10⁻⁶ (the point left after the last digit, six zeros counted), 0.86 × 10⁻⁴, and 8.6 × 10⁴ (the sign lost by counting places without the direction). The gold column reads 10⁻⁵ with its sign built in; the definition's "one digit before the point" is said before the example.

**Kit.** `digits` with powers headers to 10⁹ and 10⁻⁶, `first` (the gold column with its header read), `slide` for the check; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: it is a pilot lesson, the first time the chart is read as powers of ten and "the place of the first digit" is said; the foundation is the whole lesson.

### `scientific-times-larger` — How many times as large is one number as another?

**Flag first.** The only generated variant has a non-whole answer (12.5); confirm whole-number variants generate too. The question's word order ("as large as A is B") puts the divisor first, which is the trap.

**Standard.** 8.EE.A.3, "express how many times as much one is than the other" (the standard's own example: the world population is more than 20 times the United States'). The exact clause this type practices.

**Foundations first.** **"How many times as large" is a division** (family 10): 12 is 3 times as large as 4 because 4 fits into 12 three times, 12 ÷ 4 = 3. Two to-scale bars, 4 laid along 12 on counted words. "The answer is a plain number: how many copies." **Digits with digits, tens with tens**: (8 × 10⁸) ÷ (6.4 × 10⁷) is (8 ÷ 6.4) × (10⁸ ÷ 10⁷), because factors can be regrouped; 10⁸ ÷ 10⁷ is one rung up the ladder of ten, ×10 (the quotient law: 8 − 7 = 1).

**Read.** "How many times as large as 6.4 × 10⁷ is 8 × 10⁸?" "How many times" gold: a division. Then the order, slowly: "as large as 6.4 × 10⁷" names the thing being compared with, the divisor; "is 8 × 10⁸" names the one asked about: 8 × 10⁸ ÷ 6.4 × 10⁷. At a glance: 10⁸ is ten of 10⁷, and 8 is a bit more than 6.4, so the answer is a bit more than 10; and 8 is less than twice 6.4, so less than 20.

**The example.** (1) Two to-scale bars, aligned at the left: 6.4 × 10⁷ (64 million) in rust, 8 × 10⁸ (800 million) in teal, labeled. The small bar is visibly far more than ten times shorter than... (correction: visibly fits more than ten times into the long one). (2) Board: (8 × 10⁸) ÷ (6.4 × 10⁷) = (8 ÷ 6.4) × (10⁸ ÷ 10⁷). (3) Digits: 8 ÷ 6.4: 6.4 once leaves 1.6, which is a quarter of 6.4: 1.25. (4) Tens: 10⁸ ÷ 10⁷ = 10¹ = 10; the blue arrow on the ladder. (5) 1.25 × 10 = 12.5. `watch` card: "1.25 × 10¹ is not finished. 'How many times' is a plain number: 12.5." (6) The bars: the rust bar copies along the teal one on counted words, one to twelve, and a half. (7) Box 12.5.

**Fast.** Divide the asked-about number by the compared-with number: digits with digits, tens with tens (exponents subtract), then multiply out to a plain number.

**Sense.** One rung of ten means at least ten; 8 is more than 6.4 and less than twice it, so between 10 and 20.

**Check.** Multiply back: 12.5 × 6.4 = 80, so 12.5 × (6.4 × 10⁷) = 80 × 10⁷ = 8 × 10⁸. Or do it in standard form once: 800,000,000 ÷ 64,000,000; the same seven zeros go from both: 800 ÷ 64 = 12.5 (8.EE.A.4's "both notations" in one beat).

**Line.** "'How many times as large' is a division. Digits with digits, tens with tens. The answer is a plain number."

**Trap.** Dividing the wrong way because the sentence names the divisor first (6.4 ÷ 8 = 0.8, 10⁻¹: 0.08), and leaving the answer as 1.25 × 10¹. The bars make the first impossible (the answer must be more than one when the second bar is longer); the `watch` card and the copies counted along the bar make the second.

**Kit.** `bar` with a copy-along action (the shorter bar laid along the longer n times with a count, the curriculum plan's "copy n times"), two bars to scale on one left edge with scientific-notation labels; the ladder of ten for the one blue step. If copy-along is not built, two aligned bars with ticks every 6.4 × 10⁷ along the long one serve.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept, provided the read beat (which divides which) is kept exactly as written here; that reading is the whole difficulty.
