# Parametric models: the two concepts

**Order and filing, first.** `parametric-models` has no linked standard. Parametric equations are not in Common Core; they are standard state precalculus (the `fl-` style content). The catalog should record the skill as beyond-CCSS, with `parametric-linear-motion` linked to HSN.VM.A.3 as its primary. Film `parametric-linear-motion` first: the convert lesson borrows its picture (the start dot, the velocity arrow, the clock, the trace) and its one universal check (the point at t = 0 must sit on the path). Both stand on `vector-components` (the legs, length as the hypotenuse), which the lessons name in one line and never re-teach.

### `parametric-linear-motion` — Position, path, speed, time, direction of a point moving on a line.

**Standard.** HSN.VM.A.3 (+), primary: "Solve problems involving velocity and other quantities that can be represented by vectors": the velocity is the vector, speed is its length. HSF.IF.A.2 in the position ask: "evaluate functions for inputs in their domains." HSA.REI.B.3 in the time ask: "Solve linear equations ... in one variable." HSA.CED.A.2 is a stretch for the path ask; the relationship is given, not created. **Note:** the goal lists direction, but no generated wording asks it; confirm in `facts.ts`. If absent, the direction of the velocity (its angle, from the signs and the tangent of the rates) is a gap, and the lesson gives it one beat anyway because it is the picture's fourth reading.

**Foundations first** (about thirty seconds, built live). A drone seen from above, meters on both axes, one grid unit one meter. x(t) = 2 + t, y(t) = 1 − 4t. Define the words as they appear: *parametric equations* give x and y separately, each from t; *t is the clock*, in seconds; at t = 0 the point is at (2, 1): the *start*, the two constants. Every second, x goes up by 1 and y goes down by 4: the *velocity* is the move per second, ⟨1, −4⟩, the two t-coefficients, drawn from the start as an arrow with its blue leg 1 and rust leg −4. Tick the clock: the dot slides, a copy of the arrow stamps at t = 1, 2, 3, and the teal trace is the *path*: a straight line, because the same move is repeated. *Speed* is how far it travels each second: the length of that arrow. Line, said once: "Start, plus t copies of the velocity." Everything the problems ask is a reading of this one arrow.

**Read.** The word asked goes gold: *speed* (a number with m/s, never a pair), *where* at a time (a pair, sitting on the path), *at what time* (a number of seconds), *path* (an equation in x and y with no t). At a glance: the velocity is the pair of t-coefficients; the start is the pair of constants; the slope of the path is y-rate over x-rate.

**The example (speed), fully worked.** "A drone's position, in meters, is x(t) = 2 + t, y(t) = 1 − 4t with t in seconds. What is its constant speed, in simplest exact form? Choices: 17 m/s, 4 m/s, √17 m/s, 5 m/s." (1) Gold on *speed*: distance per second, a number. (2) Each second the drone moves ⟨1, −4⟩: the arrow with legs 1 and −4 on the picture, one arrow per stamp. (3) The distance of that move is the arrow's length: the hypotenuse of the legs. You already know: length is the hypotenuse, never the sum. (4) √(1² + (−4)²) = √(1 + 16) = √17; the negative squared is positive; written along the arrow. (5) No whole number squares to 17, so √17 stays as it is: √17 m/s.
Sense: longer than the longer leg (4), shorter than the legs added (5): √17 is about 4.1, between them.
Check: 1² + 4² = 17.
Rule out: 4 m/s is one leg alone and 5 m/s is the legs added, both dim at once: a hypotenuse is neither; 17 m/s forgot the root and is far longer than the arrow drawn. One remains.

**Second example (path), fully worked: a different method, the only algebra in the type.** "x(t) = −1 − 3t, y(t) = 2 + 4t. Eliminate t to write the path as y in terms of x. Choices: y = −4/3 x + 2, y = −4/3 x + 2/3, y = −3/4 x + 5/4, y = −4/3 x + 10/3." (1) Picture: start (−1, 2), velocity ⟨−3, 4⟩, stamps every second, the trace, extended backwards as a dashed line until it crosses the y-axis at (0, 2/3), visibly not at 2. (2) At a glance: each arrow rises 4 while running −3, so the path's slope is 4/(−3) = −4/3: the slope is the ratio of the rates. (3) Method: t is the clock, and the path has forgotten the clock, so get rid of t. From x = −1 − 3t: t = (x + 1)/(−3). (4) Substitute: y = 2 + 4·(x + 1)/(−3) = 2 − (4/3)(x + 1) = −4/3 x − 4/3 + 2 = −4/3 x + 2/3. (5) The dashed crossing on the picture is at 2/3: the board and the picture agree.
Rule out: −3/4 x + 5/4 has the reciprocal slope (rates swapped): dims. The start (−1, 2) must be on the path: −4/3(−1) + 2 = 10/3, not 2; −4/3(−1) + 10/3 = 14/3; both dim together. −4/3(−1) + 2/3 = 2: yes.
Check: t = 1 gives (−4, 6): −4/3(−4) + 2/3 = 16/3 + 2/3 = 6.

**Quick cases, three or four beats each, the picture doing the work.** Position: "x(t) = 3 − t, y(t) = 4 − 3t. Where is the drone at t = 2 s?" Two copies of ⟨−1, −3⟩ from (3, 4): (2, 1), then (1, −2); the board writes x(2) = 1, y(2) = −2 under the walk. (−2, 1) is the pair swapped; dims with (2, 1) and (−2, −6), which are not two arrows from the start. Time: "x(t) = −5 + 5t, y(t) = 2 − 2t. At what time is its x-coordinate 20?" The clock runs until x = 20; x must go from −5 to 20, a distance of 25, at 5 per second: 5t = 25, t = 5 s. Check: −5 + 25 = 20. 4 s is 20 ÷ 5 (forgot the start); 25 s forgot to divide. Direction, if generated: the velocity's angle, from the signs of the rates (the quadrant) and tan of rust over blue.

**Fast.** Velocity: the t-coefficients. Speed: its length. Position: start plus t copies, slot by slot. Time: solve the one coordinate's equation. Path: solve for t, substitute; the slope is the ratio of the rates.
**Sense.** Speed between the larger rate and the two rates added. A position two seconds in is two arrows along the trace. A time is positive when the target is ahead of the start in the direction of the rate.
**Check.** Legs squared add to the speed squared; x(t) and y(t) recomputed at the found t; t = 0 and t = 1 both satisfy the path.
**Line.** "Start, plus t copies of the velocity. Speed is the length of the arrow."
**Trap.** Speed as the sum of the rates (5), or a rate alone (4), or the root forgotten (17): the arrow's length is written along it, visibly between the longer leg and the two legs end to end. Reading the path's intercept as the constant of y(t) (+2): the start dot sits at x = −1, not on the y-axis, and the dashed trace crosses at 2/3. Forgetting the start when solving for a time (t = 4): the clock is seen starting at x = −5.
**Kit.** `path` (4.4) as specified: `run` with the clock readout, `velocity: true` with legs and `length`, `stamps: 1`, `run until x=20`; a dashed backward extension of the trace (a `seg` with `extend` and `dash` on the stamped points suffices); `dim`. Nothing beyond the family's tools.
**Writer.** Strongest. Five asks must each be one glance at the same arrow, with the speed and path examples full and the rest quick; that proportion is craft, not a recipe.

### `parametric-rectangular-convert` — Eliminate t from a line, a parabola or a circle; or write a line parametrically.

**Standard.** None linked. Beyond-CCSS state precalculus; record it so. HSA.CED.A.2, "Create equations in two or more variables to represent relationships between quantities," is practiced only loosely, since the relationship is handed over in the other form. The circle case derives a circle's equation from the Pythagorean theorem, which is HSG.GPE.A.1's first clause in parametric dress; have the exact-text extraction confirm that link. **Four cases under one name:** eliminate t from a linear pair, substitute x(t) into a line (the reverse), a parabola by substitution, a circle by cos² t + sin² t = 1. One idea rules them all (the path is the positions with the clock forgotten; the point at t = 0 must be on it), but the circle is a different method and gets its own full example.

**Foundations first** (about twenty-five seconds). The picture from the motion lesson, named in one line: t is the clock, (x(t), y(t)) is where the point is, the trace is its path. New word: the *rectangular equation* is the path's own equation, in x and y with no t: the shape, with time forgotten. Built live on x = 3t − 3, y = −6t − 4: the start (−3, −4) at t = 0, the velocity ⟨3, −6⟩, stamps, the trace. Then the one test the whole lesson leans on: the point at t = 0 is on the path, so it must satisfy the right rectangular equation; so must t = 1. "Forget the clock; keep the places it was."

**Read.** Which way is the question going? "Which rectangular equation" wants x and y alone; "which pair of parametric equations, using x = ..." wants y(t), since every choice already shares x(t). At a glance: for a line, the slope is y-rate over x-rate; for a circle, the number multiplying cos and sin is the radius and the constants are the center; for the parabola, the t² equation is the one that stays squared.

**The example (a line, eliminate t), fully worked.** "x = 3t − 3 and y = −6t − 4, where t can be any real number. Which rectangular equation describes its path? Choices: y = −2x + 2, y = −2x − 10, y = −6x − 22, y = −2x − 4." (1) The picture built above; each stamped arrow runs 3 and falls 6: slope −2. (2) Gold on *rectangular*: no t allowed. Solve the simpler equation for t: x = 3t − 3, so 3t = x + 3, t = (x + 3)/3. (3) Substitute into y: y = −6·(x + 3)/3 − 4 = −2(x + 3) − 4; the distribution written in full: −2x − 6 − 4 = −2x − 10. (4) The line y = −2x − 10 is plotted over the trace and lands on it exactly.
Rule out: −6x − 22 has the t-coefficient as slope, not the ratio of rates: dims. The start (−3, −4) must satisfy the rest: −2(−3) + 2 = 8 and −2(−3) − 4 = 2, both wrong, dim together; −2(−3) − 10 = −4: yes.
Check: t = 1 is (0, −10): −2(0) − 10 = −10.

**Second example (a circle), fully worked: the identity instead of solving for t.** "x = 5cos t − 3 and y = 5sin t − 3 for 0 ≤ t < 2π. Which rectangular equation describes its path? Choices: (x + 3)² + (y + 3)² = 5, = 25, = 1, (x − 3)² + (y − 3)² = 25." (1) Picture: center dot (−3, −3), a radius arrow of length 5 turning as t runs (the unit circle of trigonometry, scaled by 5 and slid), the traced circle. The arrow's legs are live: blue 5cos t, rust 5sin t. (2) The legs are the point's distances from the center: x − (−3) = x + 3 = 5cos t, y + 3 = 5sin t. The center's coordinates come out with their signs flipped because a leg is x minus the center. (3) Legs squared add to the radius squared: (x + 3)² + (y + 3)² = 25cos² t + 25sin² t = 25(cos² t + sin² t) = 25, since cos² t + sin² t = 1 (owned from the unit circle, said once). (4) (x + 3)² + (y + 3)² = 25: Pythagoras on the radius arrow, which is why the radius is squared.
Rule out: = 5 and = 1 have the radius not squared: dim together; the arrow is 5 long, so the squares add to 25. (x − 3)² + (y − 3)² puts the center at (3, 3), on the wrong side of the origin from the drawn circle: dims.
Check: t = 0 is (2, −3): (2 + 3)² + 0 = 25.

**Quick cases, the same two methods.** Parabola: "x = t + 5, y = t² + 6. Choices: y = (x − 5)² − 6, (x + 5)² + 6, x² + 6, (x − 5)² + 6." t = x − 5, substitute into the squared one: y = (x − 5)² + 6; the trace is a parabola with its lowest point at t = 0, (5, 6), because t² is smallest at 0; only (x − 5)² + 6 gives 6 at x = 5: the other three dim at once. Reverse: "y = 4x − 4, using x = 2t + 4. Choices: y = 8t − 4, 4t + 12, 8t + 12, 8t − 20." All choices share x, so only y(t) is asked: substitute x into the line, y = 4(2t + 4) − 4 = 8t + 16 − 4 = 8t + 12. At a glance: the y-rate must be 4 times the x-rate, 8 (4t + 12 dims); at t = 0, x = 4 and the line gives y = 12 (8t − 4 and 8t − 20 dim together).

**Fast.** Line or parabola: solve the un-squared equation for t, substitute, distribute fully. Circle: center from the constants with signs flipped, radius squared on the right. Reverse: substitute x(t) into the line's equation. Always: test t = 0 against every choice at once.
**Sense.** The start is on the path; a line's slope is the ratio of the rates; a circle's right side is a square (25, 4), never the radius.
**Check.** t = 0 and t = 1 (or t = 0 and t = π/2 for a circle) satisfy the chosen equation.
**Line.** "Forget the clock. Where it was at t = 0 must be on the path."
**Trap.** Not distributing when substituting (8t − 4 for 8t + 12; −2x − 4 for −2x − 10): the lesson writes the distribution as its own line. The circle's center with the equation's signs ((x − 3)² for a center at −3): the leg is x minus the center, said on the picture. The radius unsquared (= 5): the legs squared add to 25 on the drawn arrow.
**Kit.** `path` tracing a curve and a turning radius as a `vector` with live `legs` and `components` (4.1 and 4.4 as specified); a `plot` of the chosen rectangular equation overlaid on the trace (exists); `dim`; choices carrying \cos and squared binomials in their pills (the wrapped-pill card exists). Nothing new.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, from this concept and the finished kit: three substitutions and one owned identity, with the t = 0 test as the rule-out every time. The circle beat must be staged exactly as written (legs are x and y minus the center), which the concept now spells out.
