# Time and clocks: one concept per problem type

Two things first. `clock-minute-match` is grade-3 work (reading to the minute, 3.MD.A.1) filed under the grade-2 skill `clock-five-minutes` (2.MD.C.7, "to the nearest five minutes"); its concept below is written for grade 3 and it should move there. And the generator showed only an o'clock variant for `read-clock-hour-half`, but the goal and the standard include half past, where the real trap lives, so that lesson works both cases.

Every concept below builds the five habits (read, at a glance, the sure fast way, sense, check) and uses the family's one clock: sixty marks, hands at their true angles, teal for the hour hand and everything read from it, rust for the minute hand and everything read from it, one gold thing at a time.

---

### Grade 1: clock-half-hours (1.MD.B.3)

**`read-clock-hour-half` — Read o'clock and half past.** Foundations 1, 2, 3.

Standard: 1.MD.B.3, "Tell ... time in hours and half-hours using analog and digital clocks." (Multiple choice; the "write" clause is not practiced.)

Foundations first. One sentence of why: a clock tells what time it is, so we know when things happen. Then the two hands by look alone: the short, thick one and the long, thin one. Then the sweep, from 6:00 to 7:00, slow, a counter beside the face: the long hand goes all the way around once; while it does, the short hand moves from the 6 to the 7. "That trip is one hour." Pause at the bottom: the counter reads 30, the long hand has gone halfway: *half past*, thirty minutes; term card. Finish at the top: the counter reads 60, the long hand is straight up again, the short hand points right at the 7: *seven o'clock*, no minutes past yet; term card. Then how it is written: the hour, two dots, the minutes past: 7:00, 7:30, the hour digit teal, the minute digits rust, each dropping from its hand. "Half past seven" and "seven thirty" are the same time said two ways, once. The sweep ends on the example's own clock, so nothing is redrawn.

Read: "What time does the clock show?" with its choices; "What time" gold. The answer is an hour, two dots, and either 00 or 30. At a glance: long hand straight up or straight down; which number the short hand has passed.

Example: 7:00; choices 7:00 | 7:12 | 12:35. (1) "Which hand first?" The short, thick one: it points right at the 7. The wedge closes onto the 7, which goes gold; 7 drops into the display in teal. (2) The long, thin one is straight up: it has not started the hour; 00 drops in rust. (3) 7:00, seven o'clock. (4) Check: a small blank face sets itself to 7:00, hour hand first, then minute hand; it matches the clock.

Second example, fully worked, because the short hand sits between two numbers and children read the wrong one: 11:30, choices on the app's pattern (right, the swap, the raw number). (1) Short hand: between the 11 and the 12, exactly halfway. The sector shades 11 to 12; the 11 goes gold: "the number it has passed." 11 drops in teal. (2) Long hand straight down: half past, 30. (3) 11:30, half past eleven, eleven thirty. (4) Sense: the long hand has gone halfway, and the short hand is halfway between its numbers; the two hands agree. (5) Check by setting a blank face.

Fast: long hand up, 00; down, 30. Short hand: the number it has passed.

Sense: at o'clock the short hand points right at a number; at half past it is exactly halfway between two.

Check: set a blank face to the time read and compare; or read the clock again, long hand first.

Rule out: 12:35: the short hand is nowhere near the 12, and the long hand is straight up, so no minutes have passed; 7:12: the long hand's 12 is a number on the face, not the minutes; at the top the minutes are zero. Both dim in one beat ("the long hand is at the top: no minutes yet"). Then 7:00 is proved by the set.

Line: "Short hand, hour. Long hand, minutes. Straight up: o'clock. Straight down: half past."

Trap: the closest number (12 for 11:30), and the swap. The sweep shows the hour staying six for the whole trip until the long hand is back at the top, and the sector names the passed number; "which hand?" is asked before every reading.

Kit: `clock` with `sweep` (counter, display following, two half-sweeps of 30 so the voice can stop at the bottom), `sector`, `set` from blank, `digital`, `dim`. Nothing beyond the family's tools. No strike-outs; the `watch` card says what to do ("Find the short hand first. Which number did it pass?").

Writer: strongest. The picture is born here, in a six-year-old's words.

**`clock-hour-read` — Name the hour.** Foundation 3.

Standard: 1.MD.B.3, "Tell ... time in hours and half-hours."

Foundations first: one line names the first lesson ("the short, thick hand tells the hour"), then a half sweep from 11:00 to 11:30 with the display showing: the long hand travels halfway and the hour digit stays 11 the whole way. The hour does not change until the long hand is back at the top.

Read: "The clock shows half past an hour. Half past what hour?" or "...a time on the hour. What hour is it?" The answer is one number, no two dots. At a glance: does the short hand point right at a number, or sit between two?

Example: half past, the short hand exactly halfway between the 11 and the 12 (the long hand straight down). (1) Which hand: the short one. (2) It is between the 11 and the 12; the sector shades 11 to 12. (3) The number it has passed is 11: gold. (4) 11. "Half past eleven." Quick case, three beats, because the app's own method singles it out and children get it wrong: 12:30: between the 12 and the 1, the passed number is 12, not 1. On the hour (the first wording) is one beat: the short hand points right at the 4: 4.

Fast: the number the short hand has passed. On the hour, the number it points at.

Sense: the long hand is straight down, so the short hand should be halfway between two numbers; it is.

Check: say "half past eleven" and set a blank face to 11:30: the short hand lands where the clock's is.

Line: "The hour is the number the short hand has passed."

Trap: the nearest number: 12 for 11:30, 1 for 12:30. The sector and the half sweep: eleven stays eleven until the top.

Kit: family tools only.

Writer: a capable cheaper model; one look, one wrinkle (12:30), the first lesson supplies the voice.

**`match-clock-half-hour` — Pick the clock that shows a time.** Foundations 2, 3.

Standard: 1.MD.B.3, "Tell ... time in hours and half-hours using analog and digital clocks" (digital to analog).

Foundations first: a blank face is set to a written time, hour hand first, then minute hand; the lesson says this is how we read a time backward into hands. Both hands must be right.

Read: "Which clock shows 10:00?" Clock A | Clock B | Clock C; "10:00" gold. The hour digit says where the short hand is; the minutes say where the long hand is. At a glance: 00 means the long hand is straight up, so any clock with the long hand elsewhere is out.

Example: 10:00; Clock A shows 10:30, Clock B 10:00, Clock C 12:50 (the swap, drawn truly, with the short hand just before the 1). (1) Set a blank face to 10:00: the short hand turns to point right at the 10 (ten, and no minutes past, so right at it); the long hand to the top. (2) Short hand first: Clock C's short hand is near the 1, not the 10: C dims. (3) Long hand: Clock A's is straight down; that is half past ten: A dims. (4) Clock B: short hand at the 10, long hand at the top: 10:00. (5) Check: the set face slides beside B; the hands match.

Fast: short hand first, then long hand.

Sense: at o'clock the short hand points right at its number and the long hand is at the top; only one face can do both.

Check: read the chosen clock aloud and compare with the written time.

Rule out: short hand not at the hour: gone; long hand not where the minutes say: gone.

Line: "Short hand first. Then the long hand."

Trap: picking the clock that has a hand on the 10 whichever hand it is (Clock C), or the one with the 10 under the short hand and never looking at the long hand (Clock A). Setting hour hand first, then minute hand, makes the second look part of the method.

Kit: three named faces, `set` from blank on a scratch face, `dim` on clocks. Decoys must be real times (the kit draws from the time string, so they will be).

Writer: a capable cheaper model; the first lesson's reading reversed.

### Grade 2: clock-five-minutes (2.MD.C.7)

**`read-clock-five-minutes` — Read to five minutes.** Foundation 4.

Standard: 2.MD.C.7, "Tell ... time from analog and digital clocks to the nearest five minutes."

Foundations first: the rim is made of sixty small marks, one for each minute; an hour is sixty minutes (the grade-1 sweep, named in one line). Count the marks between the 12 and the 1 aloud, one gold step each: one, two, three, four, five. "Each number is five minutes." Count by fives around the whole ring, the gold spot hopping number to number on each word, the rust label appearing under each: 5, 10, 15 ... 55; the 12 is 0, where the minutes start over. Landmarks: the 3 is 15, the 6 is 30 (half past, known), the 9 is 45. The halves line: long hand on the right side, under thirty; left side, over thirty. Then the two-digit rule, once: minutes always take two slots, so five minutes is :05.

Read: "What time does the clock show?" 10:05 | 10:30 | 5:50 | 10:25. The answer is an hour, two dots, two minute digits. At a glance: long hand on the right side, so under thirty; short hand between the 10 and the 11.

Example: 10:25. (1) Which hand: the short, thick one is between the 10 and the 11; the sector shades, the 10 goes gold, 10 drops in teal. (2) The long, thin one points at the 5. Count by fives from the top, the spot hopping on each word, the ring filling in: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. 25 drops in rust. (3) 10:25, ten twenty-five. (4) Sense: twenty-five of sixty minutes gone, so the short hand should be a little short of halfway between the 10 and the 11; it is. (5) Check: set a blank face to 10:25 and compare.

Fast: hour is the number passed; minutes are the ring under the long hand's number. Until the ring is in the head, count by fives from a landmark (from the 3, 15: 20, 25).

Sense: the two hands agree (short hand a little short of halfway at :25); right side means under thirty.

Check: set a blank face; or read again, long hand first.

Rule out: one look at the halves line kills two: the long hand is on the right side, so the minutes are under thirty; 5:50 and 10:30 dim together. Then 10:05 against 10:25: the 5 is the fifth five, and the ring under it reads 25.

Line: "Each number is five minutes. Count by fives from the top."

Trap: the raw number (10:05) and the swap (5:50). The five marks between two numbers counted aloud once, and the ring left on screen after the count so the eye can check.

Kit: `count` by 1 on marks 1 to 5, `count` by 5 with `ring: "hidden"`, `halves`, `digital`, `sector`, `set`. Family tools only.

Writer: strongest; the ring is born here and the two decoys are named for the first time.

**`minutes-past-hour` — Count the minutes past the hour.** Foundation 4.

Standard: 2.MD.C.7, "to the nearest five minutes" (the minute reading alone).

Foundations first: one line and one picture: each number is five minutes; the ring is drawn by counting once, 5 to 55, with the landmarks named (3 is 15, 6 is 30, 9 is 45).

Read: "How many minutes past the hour does the clock show?" The answer is a count of minutes, no hour, no two dots; "minutes" gold. At a glance: the long hand is straight left, on the 9: the 45 landmark; over thirty.

Example: long hand on the 9 (short hand three quarters of the way from one number to the next). (1) Which hand: the long, thin one, the minutes. (2) Count by fives from the top, one hop per word, the ring labels appearing: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45. (3) 45. "Forty-five minutes past." (4) Sense: the long hand has gone three quarters of the way around; the short hand is three quarters of the way to its next number; they agree.

Fast: count on from a landmark: the 6 is 30, so 35, 40, 45. Always works; once the ring is memorized, it is the label under the number.

Sense: left side, so over thirty; not yet at the top, so under sixty.

Check: count back from the top: 55, 50, 45, three numbers back.

Line: "Each number is five minutes."

Trap: writing the number the hand points at (9), or the hour. The ring sits under the 9 reading 45, and the answer is read from the ring. Also the short hand ignored in the check: the lesson uses it as the sense check so it is looked at once.

Kit: family tools only.

Writer: a capable cheaper model; one count by fives, typed.

**`clock-am-pm` — Read the clock; the story chooses a.m. or p.m.** Foundations 4, 5.

Standard: 2.MD.C.7, "to the nearest five minutes, using a.m. and p.m."

Foundations first: the day line, midnight to midnight, noon in the middle, a small face reading 12:00 at each end and at noon: the clock goes all the way around twice in one day. The left half is the morning, midnight to noon: *a.m.*; the right half is the afternoon, evening and night, noon to midnight: *p.m.*; term cards for both; noon and midnight said in passing. The face cannot tell which half it is; the story can.

Read: "Student B eats breakfast at the time on the clock. Which time is it?" 7:20 p.m. | 7:04 a.m. | 4:35 a.m. | 7:20 a.m. Two acts: read the clock, then place the activity on the day line; "eats breakfast" gold. The answer is a time with two minute digits and a.m. or p.m.

Example: the clock shows 7:20. (1) Short hand between the 7 and the 8: 7, teal. (2) Long hand on the 4: 5, 10, 15, 20; 20, rust. 7:20. (3) Breakfast: morning. "Breakfast" drops onto the day line in the morning half. (4) a.m. drops into the display. 7:20 a.m. (5) Check: say the whole sentence: "Student B eats breakfast at seven twenty in the morning." True.

Quick second case, four beats, because the other half of the day must be seen once and the face alone cannot tell: "Student A goes to bed at the time on the clock," 7:25. Nearly the same face as breakfast. Bedtime drops into the evening half: 7:25 p.m. "Same-looking clock, different half of the day. The story decides."

Fast: read the clock as always; then one question: before noon or after?

Sense: morning things sit left of noon; evening things right.

Check: the whole sentence aloud, with "in the morning" or "in the evening".

Rule out: 4:35 a.m. is the swap and 7:04 a.m. the raw number; both dim in one beat (the long hand on the 4 is twenty, and the short hand is nowhere near the 4). 7:20 p.m. is breakfast at night; the day line puts breakfast on the left: gone.

Line: "a.m. is the morning: midnight to noon."

Trap: a.m. taken to mean afternoon (children hear the "a"), and 7 taken for bedtime. The day line, labeled in plain words (morning | afternoon, evening, night) under the letters, and the same-face pair of examples.

Kit: `numberline` day line with bands, small faces, `drop`; `digital.ampm`. Family tools.

Writer: a capable cheaper model; one read plus one placement.

**`match-clock-five-minutes` — Pick the clock that shows a five-minute time.** Foundations 3, 4.

Standard: 2.MD.C.7, "from analog and digital clocks to the nearest five minutes" (digital to analog).

Foundations first: one line: the short hand moves as the hour goes by; at :25 it is a little short of halfway to the next number, late in the hour it is close to the next number. Shown by setting a blank face to the written time, hour hand first, then minute hand.

Read: "Which clock shows 10:25?" Clock A | Clock B | Clock C. "10:25" gold. At a glance: the long hand must be on the 5 (five fives) and on the right side; the short hand must be past the 10, not yet at the 11.

Example: Clock A 10:25, Clock B 5:50 (the swap, drawn truly, short hand just before the 6), Clock C 10:05 (short hand barely past the 10, long hand on the 1). (1) Set a blank face to 10:25: hour hand past the 10, a little short of halfway to the 11; minute hand: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, to the 5. (2) Short hand first: Clock B's short hand is near the 6, not the 10: B dims. (3) Long hand: Clock C's is on the 1, that is five minutes: C dims. (4) Clock A: short hand past the 10, long hand on the 5. 10:25. (5) Check: the set face beside A; the hands match.

Fast: short hand first, then long hand.

Sense: the short hand should be a little short of halfway between the 10 and the 11 at twenty-five past; A's is.

Check: read Clock A aloud: ten twenty-five.

Rule out: short hand not between the 10 and the 11: gone; long hand not on the 5: gone.

Line: "Short hand first. Then the long hand."

Trap: picking the clock whose hands touch the two numbers in the time, whichever hand is which (Clock B has a hand on the 10 and a hand near the 5-ish side); and expecting the short hand to point right at the 10 (which pulls toward Clock C). The set motion puts the hour hand into its true, between-numbers place before anything is compared.

Kit: three named faces, scratch `set`, `dim`. Family tools.

Writer: a capable cheaper model; `read-clock-five-minutes` reversed, with its voice.

### Grade 3 (filed grade 2): `clock-minute-match` — To the minute, both ways. Foundations 3, 4, 6.

Misfiled: this is 3.MD.A.1 content under 2.MD.C.7. Move it to grade 3.

Standard: 3.MD.A.1, "Tell ... time to the nearest minute."

Foundations first: the ring in one pass (each number is five; count by fives around once, fast); then the marks: the five small marks between the 2 and the 3, each one minute. "Fives, then ones": count by fives to the last number the long hand has passed, then step mark by mark to the hand, starting at the mark *after* the number. Two minute digits always; under ten, a 0 first: 4:07.

Read: three wordings, three acts, each on its own screen with its choices: "Which digital time matches the clock?" (read); "Which clock shows 12:52?" (set); "How many minutes after the hour?" (minutes alone). At a glance for a read: the half (right side, under thirty), the hour passed, the nearest five.

Example 1, read: 9:14; choices 9:15 | 9:14 | 8:14 | 3:45. (1) Short hand between the 9 and the 10: 9, teal. (2) Long hand just short of the 3. Fives to the last number passed, the 2: 5, 10. (3) Ones from the next mark, gold spot stepping: 11, 12, 13, 14; it lands on the hand, one mark before the 3. 14, rust. (4) 9:14. (5) Sense: fourteen minutes in, the short hand is about a quarter of the way to the 10; it is. (6) Check: from the 3 back one mark: 15, 14.

Example 2, set, fully worked because the act is the reverse and the hour is the hard one: "Which clock shows 12:52?" Clock A 1:52 (short hand just before the 2), Clock B 10:04 (the swap), Clock C 12:52. (1) Set a blank face: hour 12, fifty-two minutes in, so the short hand has passed the 12 and is almost at the 1; the wedge 12 to 1 shades. (2) Minute hand: fives to the 10 is 50; two more marks: 51, 52. (3) Short hand first: B's is near the 10: dims. A's has passed the 1: dims. (4) C: short hand between 12 and 1, long hand two marks past the 10. (5) Check: read C aloud: 50, 51, 52; twelve fifty-two.

Quick case, four beats, "How many minutes after the hour?" 54; choices 6 | 55 | 54 | 11: fives to the 10, 50; ones 51, 52, 53, 54; 54. Rule out: 11 is the number the hand is near, not minutes; 55 is the 11 itself and the hand has not reached it; 6 is the marks *to* the top, minutes to the next hour, not after this one.

Fast: last number passed times five, then count the marks. Near the top, count the marks back from 60 as a check only.

Sense: the hands agree: at :52 the short hand is almost at the next number; at :14 about a quarter of the way.

Check: count from the next number backward; or set a blank face.

Rule out: hour wrong (3:45 the swap, 8:14 an hour the short hand has left) dim together; then the ones decide 9:15 against 9:14.

Line: "Fives, then ones. Start the ones at the next mark."

Trap: counting the number's own mark as "one" (9:16, or 9:13 from the other side); taking the nearest five (9:15); reading a short hand almost at the 1 as one o'clock (1:52 for 12:52). The gold spot lands on the number's long mark for the fives and takes its first step onto the next mark; the sector names the passed number.

Kit: `count` by 1 along marks, `ring` fast pass, three named faces with `dim`, scratch `set`. The face must be large enough that single marks read on a phone; `--dry` should note a hand tip that lands ambiguously between marks.

Writer: strongest; three acts, the hardest reads, the first grade-3 voice.

### Grade 3: elapsed-time (3.MD.A.1)

**`clock-elapsed-minutes` — Minutes between two times.** Foundations 6, 7, 8.

Standard: 3.MD.A.1, "measure time intervals in minutes." Answer in minutes alone.

Foundations first: read the start clock to the minute (one pass, fives then ones). Then the unroll: the rim straightens into a line under the face, the 12 becoming 8:00 at the left, 9:00 and 10:00 on, a small tick every five minutes; the start time is a dot on it. "Times are points on a line. The time between two times is the hop from one to the other." Sixty minutes make one hour, said once with the full turn of the long hand laid flat as a 60-minute stretch. Then the three-hop habit: to the next o'clock, then whole hours, then the rest. A cast fits: the student's first thought is the real one ("nine twenty take away eight ten: one ten... a hundred ten minutes?"), and the teacher unrolls the clock to answer it.

Read: "A lesson starts at the time shown on the clock and ends at 9:20 later that same morning. How many minutes does it last?" "How many minutes" gold: a count of minutes, no hours. At a glance: 8:10 to 9:10 would be exactly one hour, and 9:20 is a bit later: more than sixty, under a hundred twenty.

Example: start clock 8:10 (short hand just past the 8, long hand on the 2), end 9:20. (1) Read the clock: 8, then 5, 10: 8:10. (2) Unroll; 8:10 drops as the start dot, 9:20 as the end dot, "?" on the arc between. (3) Hop to the next o'clock: 8:10 to 9:00; count the five-minute ticks: 15, 20 ... 60; fifty minutes; the arc lands labeled +50 min. (4) Hop on to the end: 9:00 to 9:20, +20 min. (5) Add in the margin: 50 + 20 = 70. (6) 70 minutes. (7) "Seventy minutes is one hour and ten. The question asked minutes: 70." Quick case, three beats, because the learner will meet it: both times in the same hour, 3:10 to 3:45: one hop, 35, counted by fives; 45 − 10 gives the same only because the hour is the same, said once.

Fast: to the next o'clock, then on; add the hops. For the glance: same minutes an hour later is 60, then the rest.

Sense: more than an hour, less than two; the start is before the end.

Check: hop back from 9:20 by 70: sixty back to 8:20, ten more to 8:10. Lands on the start.

Line: "Hop to the next o'clock, then on. Count jumps, never numbers."

Trap: subtracting the clock times as numbers (9:20 − 8:10 = 1:10, then 110 or 10); leaving the answer as 1:10 when minutes were asked. The line never subtracts; hops are in minutes; the unit word is gold and "sixty make one" is said both ways at the end.

Kit: `unroll` into a `numberline` in time mode, `hop` with its label, `sum` in the margin. Addition: a hop may tick the five-minute marks it passes (`count: true`) so the fifty is counted on screen, not asserted. Start clocks in this type should sometimes show odd minutes (design note).

Writer: strongest; the unroll and the story reasoning carry the explanation.

**`elapsed-time-hm` — End time, start time, or how long.** Mixed type. Foundations 5, 7, 8.

Standard: 3.MD.A.1, "Solve word problems involving ... time intervals ... e.g., by representing the problem on a number line diagram." 4.MD.A.2 only in its words "intervals of time"; the type does not serve the rest of it.

Foundations first: this is three problems under one name, and the lesson says so. Every story has three parts: when it started, how long it lasted, when it ended. The problem gives two and asks the third. The time line is drawn with the "?" at the missing part: at the end, on the arc, or at the start (three small lines side by side, one beat each). Hours and minutes stay apart on the line (teal hour arcs, rust minute arcs) and in the answer; sixty minutes make one hour. a.m. and p.m. are on the line as labels, and 12:00 noon is just another o'clock on it. Never "these use the same steps"; what they share is the line. A cast fits: the student subtracts digits; the teacher draws the line.

Read: the whole story, with its choices; the question word gold ("At what time does it return", "How long does it last", "At what time did it begin"). One beat says the shape of the answer (a clock time with p.m.; hours and minutes) and the glance bound (about three hours after 4:45 is around eight).

Kind 1, end time: "A class trip leaves at 4:45 p.m. It lasts 3 hours 15 minutes. At what time does it return?" 8:00 p.m. | 7:45 p.m. | 5:00 p.m. | 1:30 p.m. Line 4:00 to 9:00 p.m., start dot 4:45, "?" at the end. Glance: forty-five and fifteen make sixty, so the end lands on an o'clock. (1) Hop the hours, one teal arc each: 5:45, 6:45, 7:45; +3 h. (2) Hop the minutes: 7:45 to 8:00, +15 min; the minutes reach sixty, the long hand is back at the top. (3) 8:00 p.m. Rule out: 1:30 p.m. is before leaving, and 5:00 p.m. is only fifteen minutes later; both dim ("about three hours after a quarter to five"). 7:45 p.m. forgot the fifteen. Check: hop back 3 h from 8:00: 5:00; back 15: 4:45.

Kind 2, how long: "A soccer game starts at the time shown on the clock, in the morning, and ends at 11:10 a.m. How long does it last?" Clock 9:15. 2 h 55 | 1 h 55 | 55 min | 2 h 5. Read the clock: 9:15. Line 9:00 to 12:00, dots at 9:15 and 11:10, "?" on the arc. Glance: nine-something to eleven-something is more than one hour and less than two. (1) To the next o'clock: 9:15 to 10:00, +45 min. (2) A whole hour: 10:00 to 11:00, +1 h. (3) The rest: 11:00 to 11:10, +10 min. (4) Margin: hours 1; minutes 45 + 10 = 55. 1 hour 55 minutes. Rule out: 55 min, 2 h 55 and 2 h 5 all fall outside "between one hour and two"; they dim in one beat. Check: 9:15 + 1 h = 10:15, + 45 = 11:00, + 10 = 11:10. Quick case, four beats, because crossing noon loses children: movie from 9:30 to 2:00 p.m.: the line runs a.m. | noon | p.m.; +30 to 10:00, then 10, 11, 12, 1, 2: four hour arcs; 4 hours 30 minutes; nothing happens at 12 but a label.

Kind 3, start time: "A play ends at 6:00 p.m. It lasted 4 hours 25 minutes. At what time did it begin?" 5:35 | 2:00 | 1:35 | 10:25 p.m. Line 1:00 to 7:00 p.m., end dot 6:00, "?" at the start, arcs going left. (1) Back the hours: 5, 4, 3, 2:00; −4 h. (2) Back the minutes from 2:00, counting the ticks: 1:55, 1:50 ... 1:35; −25 min; into the hour before. (3) 1:35 p.m. Rule out: 10:25 p.m. is after the end, and 5:35 is only twenty-five minutes before; dim together ("a start comes before, and about four hours before"). 2:00 forgot the minutes. Check forward: 1:35 + 4 h = 5:35, + 25 = 6:00.

Fast: hours first, then minutes, forward or back; for how long, next o'clock, whole hours, the rest.

Sense: an end is after the start; a start is before the end; a duration lies between the hour gap minus one and plus one; minutes in a time are under sixty.

Check: hop the other way and land on the given time.

Rule out: wrong direction and wrong size go together in one beat; the one that forgot a part dims when that part is hopped.

Line: "Hours and minutes stay apart. Sixty minutes make one hour."

Trap: subtracting clock times as numbers (11:10 − 9:15 as 2:05 or "1:95"); writing 7:60 instead of 8:00; counting the o'clocks instead of the jumps ("5, 6, 7, 8: four hours" from 4:45); getting lost at 12.
