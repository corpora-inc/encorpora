# Counting and cardinality: one concept per problem type

Each concept stands on the family design and keeps its habits (read, at a glance, the sure way, sense, check). Nothing is struck out anywhere in this family; choices that are wrong dim together with the reason said once.

---

### Kindergarten: count-to-20 (K.CC.B.4, K.CC.B.5)

#### `count-objects` — How many are there?

**First:** the only generated wording is "How many dots are shown?" and the seed answer is 1, a weak main example. Confirm the generator makes all four arrangements the standard names and keeps scatters at or below ten; confirm it varies the objects. The lesson's foundations use things; the worked example uses the problem's own dots.

**Standard.** K.CC.B.5: "Count to answer 'how many?' questions about as many as 20 things arranged in a line, a rectangular array, or a circle, or as many as 10 things in a scattered configuration." K.CC.B.4a and b: "pairing each object with one and only one number name," "the last number name said tells the number of objects counted. The number of objects is the same regardless of their arrangement."

**Foundations first.** Why we count: to answer "how many?" (how many dogs are in the yard; how many cups do we need). How: touch a thing, say the next word; one word for one thing; never a thing without a word, never a word without a thing. Build it: six dogs in a scatter; the gold ring lands on the nearest dog on "one", the dog slides into a line, the count label reads 1; and so on to six. The pile empties as the line grows, so nothing can be counted twice. Settle: the label holds at 6, then drops under the line: "6  six. The last word tells how many. We do not count again to answer." Rearrange: the same six dogs bunch up, then make a circle, the label never moving: "Move them. Still six." Count the circle from a different dog: six again. Then "A line, a circle, a scatter: counting is the same job. The hard part is keeping track."

**Read.** "How many dots are shown?" "How many" goes gold; the answer is a number. At a glance: a group of two or three is seen without counting; eight scattered dots are too many to see, so touch them.

**The example.** Eight dots in a scatter (the hardest arrangement to keep track of, and the problem's own picture). (1) Pick the nearest dot to start. (2) Ring on the word, dot slides into a row, label ticks: one ... eight. The pile shrinks to nothing; the row holds eight equal dots at equal spacing. (3) Settle: 8 drops under the row, "eight" beside it. (4) Check: count the row from the other end: eight.

**Quick second case, a circle** (a different way to lose track): twelve dots in a circle. Mark the start dot with a gold tick first; count clockwise; stop when the ring comes back to the marked dot: twelve. Four beats. The circle is the arrangement where children go around twice or stop early; the marked start ends it.

**Fast.** There is no shortcut past counting; the fast way is keeping track: move each counted thing out of the pile, or in a circle mark where you began. See two or three without counting.

**Sense.** The answer is the last word said, and eight is a row not quite as long as ten.

**Check.** Count again from the other end, or in a different order. The same word must come out.

**Line.** "One word, one thing. The last word tells how many."

**Trap.** Words running ahead of fingers; one dot skipped and another counted twice in a scatter; recounting to answer "how many?". The ring, one word per ring, with counted dots leaving the pile, sets the pace; the settle gives the answer without a recount.

**Kit.** Object counters with `scatter`, `circle` and `line` layouts, `count` with the touch ring and slide into a line, `settle`, `rearrange`: all in the family's 4.1. Nothing beyond.

**Writer.** Strongest. It is the family's first lesson, built from nothing in a five-year-old's words, and every later lesson refers back to it.

### Kindergarten: count-to-100 (K.CC.A.1, K.CC.A.2)

#### `count-sequence-missing` — Fill the gap in a count.

**First:** the "by tens" variants all start on a decade (20, 30), which is right for K; if any variant ever counts by tens off a decade (23, 33, 43), that is 1.NBT.C.5 and should move.

**Standard.** K.CC.A.2: "Count forward beginning from a given number within the known sequence (instead of having to begin at 1)." K.CC.A.1: "Count to 100 by ones and by tens."

**Foundations first.** The number path: a row of equal cells, each with its number, a token that hops one cell per word. The missing number was there and got hidden; the numbers beside the box are its neighbors. The key idea, said once and shown: you do not go back to one. Start at the number you know; the next word is one more. The token starts at 35 and hops: thirty-six, thirty-seven. Then the words' pattern: one to nine, then a new ten; "after nine comes a new ten", shown as the path from 36 to 45 with 40 marked where the row of words starts over.

**Read.** "Count by ones" or "by tens" goes gold: that is the step. The box's answer is a number that sits right between its neighbors. At a glance: the number before the box; its next word is the answer.

**The example.** "Count by ones: 37, 38, 39, □." The decade crossing, where children stall. (1) Path 35 to 44; the box cell is empty with a gold border at 40. (2) Token hops on the given words: thirty-seven, thirty-eight, thirty-nine, each cell lighting. (3) "Thirty-nine. What comes next? After nine, a new ten: forty." The token hops; 40 is written in the cell: a four and a zero. (4) Say the run: thirty-seven, thirty-eight, thirty-nine, forty. (5) Check: hop back one from forty: thirty-nine.

**Quick second case, by tens:** "Count by tens: 30, □, 50, 60." Arcs of ten cells labeled "+10" from 30: thirty, forty. The box is 40. The check lives here: one more arc from forty lands on fifty, the number after the box. "By tens the ones stay zero; the words go thirty, forty, fifty, sixty."

**Fast.** Say the next word after the number before the box (by ones) or the next tens word (by tens).

**Sense.** The answer sits between its neighbors; by tens it ends in zero.

**Check.** The number after the box must be the word after your answer. Say the whole run aloud with the answer in it.

**Line.** "Start where you are. After nine, a new ten."

**Trap.** "Thirty-nine, thirty-ten," a skipped decade ("thirty-nine, fifty"), or 40 written as 310; and going back to one to find the next word. The path shows forty in the cell right after thirty-nine, written as two digits in one cell, and the token never visits one.

**Kit.** `numberline` path mode with `hide`, `token`, `hop` by 1 and by 10 with arc labels (family 4.3). Nothing beyond.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model from this concept: one method on one component, the decade case already specified as the main example.

#### `hundred-chart-missing` — The empty square of the chart.

**First:** a fragment whose only filled neighbor is above or below asks for "ten more," a grade 1 idea (1.NBT.C.5). Either guarantee a left or right neighbor or accept the stretch; the lesson teaches the column pattern either way because the chart shows it.

**Standard.** K.CC.A.1: "Count to 100 by ones and by tens." K.CC.A.2: counting forward from a given number.

**Foundations first.** What the hundred chart is: every number from 1 to 100, in rows of ten. Build it: the number path 1 to 30 drawn in one row, then folded: it breaks at 10 and at 20 and the pieces stack, and the fold runs on to 100. The pattern, read off the chart: across a row the ones word changes, one more each square; at the end of a row, after nine, a new ten starts the next row ("fifty-nine, sixty"); down a column the ones word stays and the tens word goes up by one: 6, 16, 26, 36 all end in six, and "down one square is ten more", shown as a "+10" arrow between 46 and 56 (ten squares along the path). The "+1" arrow goes to the right neighbor.

**Read.** "What number belongs in the empty square?" "Empty square" goes gold. The answer is a number sitting between the numbers beside it and ending like the numbers above and below it. At a glance: the square to its left, plus one.

**The example.** A fragment: rows 44 to 48, 54 to 58 with 56 empty, 64 to 68. (1) The full chart ghosts in faintly around the fragment once, so the child sees where the piece came from; then it fades. (2) Left neighbor 55; the "+1" arrow: fifty-five, fifty-six. (3) 56 is written in the square. (4) Check from above: 46; "+10" arrow down; forty-six, fifty-six; same column, same ending. (5) Check from the right: 57 is the word after fifty-six.

**Quick second case, the row's first square:** 60 empty with 50 above and 61 to its right, no left neighbor. The square above plus ten: fifty, sixty. Or: the row above ended at fifty-nine, and after nine a new ten. Check: 61 is the word after sixty.

**Fast.** Left neighbor plus one. With no left neighbor: above plus ten. Faster still, no counting: the column gives the ones digit, the row gives the tens word.

**Sense.** The answer ends like everything in its column and sits between its row neighbors.

**Check.** The second neighbor: left plus one and above plus ten must agree.

**Line.** "Across is one more. Down is ten more."

**Trap.** Counting from 1 to reach the square; losing the ten at the end of a row. The step arrows replace the long count; the fold shows why the rows start over.

**Kit.** `chart` with `window`, ghost, `hide`, `step`, `fill`, `fold` from a path part (family 4.4). Nothing beyond.

**Writer.** Strongest. The chart is built live by the fold and "down a row is ten more" must be taught without place-value words the child does not have.

### Kindergarten: write-numerals (K.CC.A.3)

#### `numeral-for-quantity` — Count the frames, write the number.

**First:** one wording seen, answer 3. Confirm the type reaches 20 (two frames) and includes 0 (an empty frame), which K.CC.A.3 names.

**Standard.** K.CC.A.3: "Write numbers from 0 to 20. Represent a number of objects with a written numeral 0–20 (with 0 representing a count of no objects)." K.CC.B.5 for the counting.

**Foundations first.** The ten-frame: ten places, five on top and five below, filled left to right, top row first. Build it: counters land one at a time with the label ticking; at five, "the top row is full: five, you can see it without counting"; at ten, "the frame is full: ten." A numeral is the mark we write for a count: the word, the group and the numeral are one number three ways; 3 is written under three cups while "three" is said. An empty frame is zero: nothing to count, and we still write a number, 0.

**Read.** "How many counters are in the frames? Write the number." "How many" and "write" go gold; the answer is a number from 0 to 20, written in digits. At a glance: a full frame is ten; a full row is five; count on from there.

**The example.** A full frame and a second frame with 4 counters (the teen case, where children write the digits backward). (1) The full frame: "ten," its label 10, no counting. (2) Ring the four loose counters, counting on: eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen. (3) Settle: 14 drops under the frames, the 1 under the full frame, the 4 under the four. (4) Write it: the 1 first, then the 4. "Four-teen says four first. We write the ten first." Handoff said once: "a full frame is ten", which is where place value begins.

**Quick cases:** one frame with a full top row and two more: five, six, seven; write 7. An empty frame: no counters; write 0. Three beats each; both are different cases (under ten with the five; none).

**Fast.** Full frame ten, full row five, count on. Always works.

**Sense.** One full frame and some loose ones is a teen; a frame not yet full is under ten.

**Check.** Touch every counter from one, or count the loose ones alone and match the second digit.

**Line.** "A full frame is ten. Count on from there."

**Trap.** 13 written as 31; "fourteen" heard as forty; counting all fourteen from one and losing the place. The numeral drops ten first under the full frame, and the lesson never counts the full frame.

**Kit.** Frames and counters exist; `count` with the ring; `write-numeral` strokes are wanted but a dropped label is acceptable until they exist. Nothing beyond.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one component, one idea, short.

### Kindergarten: compare-small-groups (K.CC.C.6)

#### `compare-groups` — More, fewer, or the same.

**First:** both generated wordings answer "The same number" (the seed). Confirm variants with a real difference exist; the main example must have one.

**Standard.** K.CC.C.6: "Identify whether the number of objects in one group is greater than, less than, or equal to the number of objects in another group, e.g., by using matching and counting strategies."

**Foundations first.** "More" means a bigger group; "fewer" (term card) means not as many; "the same number" means neither group has anything left over when they are paired up. The length trap, drawn on purpose: five cups spread wide over seven socks bunched tight. "Which is more?" Pause. The rows slide onto one left edge at one spacing and pairs are matched with lines, cup to sock; the cups run out and two socks are left: seven is more. "A longer row is not a bigger number. Pair them up."

**Read.** "Are there fewer circles or fewer squares?" "Fewer" goes gold: it wants the row with less. The answer is one of three; "the same number" is gone the moment one leftover appears.

**The example.** 6 circles over 8 squares, the problem's own rows. (1) Rows aligned on the left. (2) Match lines, one per word: this circle and this square ... six lines. (3) The circles run out; two squares are left, gold, with "2 left". (4) Squares have more. The question asks fewer: fewer circles. "The same number" dims (there are leftovers) and "fewer squares" dims (squares are the row with leftovers), together, with that one reason. (5) Check by counting: six circles, eight squares; six comes before eight in the count, so six is less.

**Quick second case, equal rows:** 7 and 7; every one has a partner, no leftovers: "The same number." Three beats; it is the only case where the third choice is right.

**Fast.** Line them up, pair them, look at what is left.

**Sense.** After lining up, the row that sticks out has more.

**Check.** Match in the other direction, or count both rows.

**Rule out.** "The same number" at the first leftover; then the gold word chooses between the two rows.

**Line.** "Pair them up. Leftovers mean more."

**Trap.** Longer means more; and answering the "more" row when asked "fewer." The aligned rows with match lines end the first; the gold word and the beat "it wants the row with less" come before the answer for the second.

**Kit.** `match` (family 4.2), `dim` on board choices, the `term` card. Nothing beyond.

**Writer.** Strongest. The motion proves, the trap is deep, and a weak author describes matching instead of staging it.

#### `dot-group-compared-to-number` — Which lettered group fits the question?

**First:** groups "of up to 20 dots" above ten must be arranged in frames, lines or arrays, or they break K.CC.B.5's scatter limit and cannot be counted reliably. The "exactly 11 dots" wording is a counting question, not a comparison. The answer is a letter, and the choice order is scrambled (B | D | C | A): the lesson says the letter.

**Standard.** K.CC.C.6 as above, with one side a numeral; K.CC.B.5 for counting each group.

**Foundations first.** One side is a number, so there is nothing to pair with: count each group instead, and compare words. Said once, over the path: "more than eight" means a count you say after eight; "fewer than thirteen" means a count you say before thirteen; "the same" means the very same word. The groups are drawn at one dot size, so a group that is clearly tiny is known at a glance, and groups near the target are counted, never guessed.

**Read.** "Which group has more dots than 8?" "More" goes gold; the answer is a letter; the chosen group's count must come after eight.

**The example.** Four groups, same dot size: A, nine in a frame; B, three; C, eight in a frame; D, six in a frame. (1) At a glance: B is three, D is a full row and one, six; both come before eight. They dim together: "three and six are not more than eight." (2) Two remain near eight; count both. C: full row five, six, seven, eight. Eight is not more than eight; it is the same. C dims. (3) A: five, six, seven, eight, nine. Nine comes after eight. (4) The answer is A. (5) Check: count A from the other end; say "nine is more than eight."

**Quick cases.** "Fewer than 13" with groups in two frames: a group whose first frame is not full is under ten, so under thirteen at a glance; a group with a full frame and five more is fifteen, not fewer; the direction flips, the method does not. "The same number as the Start group": count the Start group first (seven), then look for seven; a match line can confirm. Three or four beats each. "Exactly 11" is counting each group until one ends on eleven; the method covers it and it gets no screen of its own.

**Fast.** Dim what is obviously too small or too big; count the rest; later in the count is more.

**Sense.** For "more than 8" the chosen group must look at least as big as a group of eight.

**Check.** Count the chosen group again from the other end and say it with the target: "nine comes after eight."

**Rule out.** Visibly tiny groups for "more," visibly big ones for "fewer"; for "the same," anything that does not count to the exact word.

**Line.** "Count the group. Later in the count is more."

**Trap.** Choosing the group that looks most spread out; choosing the group with exactly eight for "more than eight." Equal dot size, and the counted eight that is "the same, not more."

**Kit.** `dim` must also act on figure groups (letters and dots fading muted together), not only on board choices.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model once `compare-groups` has established comparing; the dim with its reason is the only craft.

### Kindergarten: compare-numerals (K.CC.C.7)

#### `greater-numeral` — Which number is greater, or less?

**First:** 0 lies outside K.CC.C.7's "between 1 and 10" but is a good trick case; keep it, and define zero as none.

**Standard.** K.CC.C.7: "Compare two numbers between 1 and 10 presented as written numerals."

**Foundations first.** The number path 0 to 10 with a token: farther along the path is more things, said as "bigger number," with the word "greater" beside it; nearer the start is "smaller," with "less." Under the path, a group of two and a group of seven so the numerals mean something: the token on 2 beside two cups, on 7 beside seven. Zero is the first cell: an empty frame, 0, none.

**Read.** "Which number is less: 6 or 1?" "Less" goes gold, and the beat before any thinking: "It wants the smaller one." The answer is one of the two numbers given.

**The example.** 6 or 1. (1) Both numerals on the board. (2) Count up the path from zero; the token reaches 1 first, then goes on to 6. "The number you reach first is less." (3) Beside the path, one dot and six dots, same size. (4) The answer is 1. (5) Read it back as a sentence: "One is less than six."

**Quick second case:** "Which is greater: 8 or 0?" Zero is none; an empty frame; it sits at the very start of the path, so anything is more than none: 8.

**Fast.** Count up; the one said first is less, the one said later is greater.

**Sense.** The smaller number has the smaller group beside it.

**Check.** Say the answer as a sentence with the question word in it and ask if it is true.

**Line.** "Later in the count is greater."

**Trap.** Answering the bigger number out of habit when asked for less; treating zero as not a number. The gold word and "it wants the smaller one" come first; the empty frame with 0 under it makes zero a count.

**Kit.** Path with token (family 4.3), counters. Nothing beyond.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one idea, the path, the gold word; short.

### Grade 2: skip-counting (2.NBT.A.2)

#### `skip-count-missing` — What comes next by 5, 10 or 100?

**First:** only one variant seen (725, 730, 735 by 5). The standard also asks for 10s and 100s; check they are generated, that some variant crosses a hundred (the case that trips children), and whether any starts off a multiple of the step (that turns the ending rule from a shortcut into a check).

**Standard.** 2.NBT.A.2: "Count within 1000; skip-count by 5s, 10s, and 100s."

**Foundations first.** Counting by fives is counting groups of five, saying only the last word of each group: three hands of five fingers, "five, ten, fifteen" (the handoff to equal groups, one line). The step is how much is added each time. On a number line from 720 to 750 with a tick every five, arcs of equal size labeled "+5" are hops along the line (the path's cells became hops in grade 1). The endings: by fives they go five, zero, five, zero; by tens the ending stays; by hundreds the last two digits stay. That pattern is the check.

**Read.** "Count by 5: 725, 730, 735, □. What comes next?" "By 5" goes gold: the step. The answer is one step past 735 and must end in zero. At a glance: 735 and five more is 740.

**The example.** (1) The line, the three numbers dropped as dots. (2) Arc 725 to 730, "+5"; arc 730 to 735, "+5": the step is five. (3) One more arc from 735 lands on 740. (4) The endings: five, zero, five, zero; 740 fits. (5) Check: 740 take away 5 is 735; or count by ones through: 736, 737, 738, 739, 740.

**Quick second case, crossing a hundred:** 785, 790, 795, □. Ninety-five and five is one hundred: ten tens make a hundred (family 2, named once), so the hundreds word goes up and the rest is zero: 800. The ending still fits.

**Fast.** Add the step to the last number; the ending must follow the pattern.

**Sense.** The answer is just one step past the last number and ends the way the pattern says.

**Check.** Subtract the step from the answer; you land on the last given number.

**Line.** "Add the step. Check the ending."

**Trap.** Continuing by ones (736), the wrong step (745), or losing the hundred at the crossing ("seven hundred ninety-five, seven hundred one hundred", written 7100). The arc with its step, the ending check, and the 795 to 800 case.

**Kit.** `numberline` with step arcs and drops (specified). Nothing beyond; a chart hop view of 701 to 800 is optional.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one method, the hundred crossing already named as the second case.

### Grade 2: odd-and-even (2.OA.C.3)

#### `pairing-leftover` — How many are left without a partner?

**First:** the seeds, 2 counters and 1 counter, are degenerate main examples; use 13. The type asks only for the leftover count, never for "odd" or "even" by name or for the equation 8 = 4 + 4; both clauses of the standard are unpracticed and two small new types would close it (the second hands doubles to family 6).

**Standard.** 2.OA.C.3: "Determine whether a group of objects (up to 20) has an odd or even number of members, e.g., by pairing objects or counting them by 2s."

**Foundations first.** A pair (term card) is two together. Put counters into pairs: they slide together two at a time under an arch while the label counts by twos. What can be left over: zero or one, never two, because two leftovers would make another pair. Then the names: a number whose counters pair off with none left is even; one left over, odd.

**Read.** "Put 13 counters into pairs. How many counters will be left without a partner?" "Pairs" and "left without a partner" go gold; the answer is a count of leftovers, so it is 0 or 1, nothing else.

**The example.** 13 counters in a line, label 13. (1) Pair on the words: two, four, six, eight, ten, twelve; six arches. (2) One counter left, gold, "1 left". (3) The answer is 1; thirteen is odd. (4) Check: count the pairs by twos and add the leftover: twelve and one is thirteen. (5) Why the ones digit is enough: the first ten counters are five whole pairs, so only the loose ones decide; thirteen is ten and three, and three is a pair and one. So 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 at the end mean none left; 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 mean one left.

**Quick second case:** 14; the four past the ten is two pairs: zero left; even. Three beats, since the method was shown fully and this is the other answer.

**Fast.** Look at the ones digit: even digits leave none, odd digits leave one. Always works, and the lesson has just shown why.

**Sense.** The answer can only be 0 or 1.

**Check.** Pairs counted by twos plus the leftover gives back the total.

**Line.** "Pairs, no leftover: even. One left over: odd. Only zero or one can be left."

**Trap.** Answering the number of pairs (6), or splitting 13 into two groups and answering a group's size (6 or 7). The arches over twos and the single gold counter make "pair" and "left over" unmistakable; the sense check catches both.

**Kit.** `pair` (family 4.2). Nothing beyond.

**Writer.** Strongest. The order pairs, then why, then rule must be staged so the ones-digit rule is earned rather than announced.
