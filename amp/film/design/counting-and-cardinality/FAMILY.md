# Counting and cardinality, K to 2: lesson design

Seven skills, nine problem types. Planning snapshot: the catalog and the engine templates; `film/facts.ts` for every variant before a lesson is written. [The place-value design](../place-value/DESIGN.md) is the model for depth; this family is smaller and earlier, and it is where every later picture is born.

---

## 1. The idea underneath the whole family

**A number is how many.** Counting is how you find out: one word for each thing, in the same order every time, and the last word is the answer. Everything else in this family is counting done cleverly: counting on from where you already are, counting in fives so you need fewer words, counting in pairs, or not counting at all because you matched the two groups and looked at what was left over. A child who owns the first three foundations below owns every "how many" question for the rest of school; the rest of the ladder is what the counting words are good for.

The foundations, in the order they are built. The kindergarten ones stand on nothing; this is the bedrock family in the curriculum's ladder.

1. **One word, one thing.** (K) Touch a thing, say the next word. Never a thing without a word, never a word without a thing. The job of counting is keeping track of what has been counted, which is why we line things up, move them, or mark them. This is one-to-one correspondence, and it is the only hard part of counting.

2. **The last word tells how many.** (K) After the last touch, the word just said is the answer to "how many?". You do not start over to answer. The count label that ticks with every touch and then drops under the group as the numeral is this foundation made visible.

3. **Move them, count again: the same.** (K) Spread out, bunched up, in a circle, counted from the other end, the group still has the same number. A longer row is not a bigger number. Children who do not own this will tell you a spread-out five is more than a bunched-up seven, and they will say it with confidence. This foundation is what makes comparing possible.

4. **The next word is one more.** (K) Add one thing and the count moves to the next word; take one away and it moves back. So you can start at thirty-seven and count on: thirty-eight, thirty-nine. You never need to go back to one. This is K.CC.A.2 and the seed of all addition (given to family 6).

5. **The words have a pattern: after nine, a new ten.** (K) One to nine, then a ten word, then one to nine again. The teens are the exception that children must simply learn (eleven and twelve do not say their ten; "four-teen" says its ones first). From twenty on the pattern is regular, and the hundred chart lays it out: every row is one tens word, every column one ones word. Down a column, the ones word stays and the tens word goes up one. This is K.CC.A.1 (to 100 by ones and tens). The full row of ten is handed to family 2, where it becomes "ten is a thing".

6. **A numeral is a name for a count.** (K) The mark 3 means three things; 0 means none. Say it, see it, write it: the word, the group and the numeral are one number three ways. A full ten-frame and three more is 13, and the numeral is written with the ten first even though "thirteen" says the three first (the realistic writing trap: 31).

7. **To compare, match.** (K) Pair one thing from each group with one from the other. Leftovers mean more; no leftovers mean the same. Only after matching does counting enter: if you know both counts, the number you say later when counting is the greater one (K.CC.C.6, then C.7). The number path makes this a picture: farther along the path is greater. Zero is at the very start: anything is more than none.

8. **Counting groups: say only the last word of each group.** (Grade 2) Five, ten, fifteen is counting three groups of five by naming only each group's last member. The words make a pattern (by fives the ending flips five, zero; by tens the ending stays; by hundreds the last two words stay), and the pattern is the check. This stands on family 2 (tens and hundreds as units: 795 to 800 crosses a hundred) and is given to family 7 (equal groups) and family 20 (fives around the clock).

9. **Pairs with no one left over: even.** (Grade 2) Put the counters in twos. Nothing left over, the number is even; one left over, odd. Only zero or one can ever be left, since two leftovers would make another pair. An even number is two equal groups (6 is 3 and 3), which hands the idea to family 6 as doubles. The ones-digit rule (0, 2, 4, 6, 8 are even) is taught as what the pairs show, after the pairs.

## 2. The visual language: counters that are things, frames, the path, the chart

One picture system, P1 in the curriculum plan, with the number path from P3 and the hundred chart, which is the path folded. A second grader skip counting on the hundred chart must recognize the frame and the counters from kindergarten.

### The shapes

| Shape | Form | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **counter** | a disc one cell wide, or any object drawn to the same size (a dog, an apple, a house, a cup) | one thing |
| **ten-frame** | 5 by 2 equal cells, filled left to right, top row first | a full top row is five; a full frame is ten |
| **number path** | a row of equal cells numbered 1, 2, 3 ... | each cell is a thing to count; a token hops cell to cell |
| **hundred chart** | 10 by 10 equal cells, 1 to 100, left to right, top to bottom | the path folded at every ten: rows are tens words, columns are ones words |
| **pair** | two counters touching under one small arch | two |
| **match line** | a line from one thing in a row to one thing in the other row | partners |
| **count label** | a large numeral beside the group | ticks with each touch; its last value is the answer |

Counters never stay dots for a whole lesson. The foundations are built with things a child counts in life (dogs, cups, socks), drawn plainly at one size, and the worked example uses the problem's own picture (dots, counters in frames, circles and squares) so the film looks like the problem. Whatever the object, one of it is one; a big dog and a small dog each count once.

Scale for discrete things means equal size and equal spacing. Two rows being compared are drawn on the same left edge with the same spacing, so that after matching, a longer row really is a bigger count. When the lesson shows the length trap, it deliberately draws unequal spacing first and then slides the rows into equal spacing. A chart fragment is drawn at the same cell size as the full chart, and the full chart is ghosted faintly around it once, so the child sees where the piece came from. Frames always have exactly ten cells, every cell drawn.

### Color means one thing each

- **teal**: the group being counted, or the first group in a comparison, and its numeral.
- **rust**: the second group in a comparison; counters that leave.
- **gold**: the one thing being looked at now: the ring on the counter being touched, the word in the question that says what is asked ("how many", "more", "fewer", "the same", "missing", "next", "left over"), the empty square of the chart, the leftover counter, the landing square of a skip hop. One gold thing at a time; the touch ring moves, it does not multiply.
- **muted**: a counter that has already been counted (a small tick on it), the chart outside the window, choices that have been ruled out.
- Never a tinted answer, never a counter colored for decoration.

### The motions (each one proves a foundation)

- **Touch.** A gold ring lands on a counter as its word is said; the counter takes a muted tick (or slides into the next frame cell, or into a line) and the count label ticks by one. One word, one ring, one tick, always in step. In a scatter, counted counters slide into a row so the uncounted pile shrinks visibly; in a circle, the starting counter is marked first. Foundation 1.
- **Settle.** After the last touch the count label holds, then drops under the group as the numeral, with its word beside it ("7  seven"). Nothing is recounted. Foundation 2.
- **Rearrange.** The same counters spread out, bunch up, or form a circle while the count label never changes. Counting again from the other end lands on the same word. Foundation 3.
- **One more.** A counter arrives and the label ticks to the next word; on the path, a token hops one cell and lands on the next number. Foundation 4.
- **Fold.** The number path 1 to 30 is drawn in a row, then breaks at every ten and the pieces stack under each other: the hundred chart appears from the path. Shown once, in the chart lessons; after that the chart is just the chart. Foundation 5.
- **Step.** On the chart, an arrow from a square to its right neighbor labeled "+1", or to the square below labeled "+10"; the ones word stays as the column lights, the tens word changes as the row lights. Foundation 5.
- **Write.** The numeral is drawn stroke by stroke under the group as the word is said. Foundation 6.
- **Match.** Two rows slide into alignment on the same left edge; match lines draw pair by pair on the words ("this one and this one"); whatever is left unmatched glows gold with its own small count. Then, and only then, the two numerals appear and the path shows which is farther along. Foundation 7.
- **Pair.** Counters slide together two at a time under arches while a label counts "two, four, six"; a leftover glows gold alone. Foundation 9.
- **Hop.** On the path or the chart, arcs of equal size leap five (or ten, or a hundred) cells at a time with the step written on the arc; landing squares light in turn and the ones digits of the landings are read aloud as a pattern. Foundation 8.
- **Dim.** Choices that cannot be right fade to muted together, in one beat, with the one reason said aloud. Nothing is struck out: in K to 2 nothing is ever struck out.

### The handoffs this family says aloud, once each

A full frame becomes a rod (family 2: "ten is a thing"). The number path's cells become the hops of a number line in grade 1 (family 6): the thing counted is no longer the cell but the jump. A pair with no leftover becomes a double, 3 + 3 (family 6). Hops of five around a circle are the clock (family 20). Counting by fives is counting groups of five (family 7). One counter is one thing on a line plot (family 32).

## 3. How lessons in this family should go

### Foundations first, in a five-year-old's words

Before any problem: why we count (to answer "how many?"; later, "how far?" along a path), how we count (one word for each thing; the last word is the answer), and what the thing on screen is (a ten-frame has ten places; a hundred chart is all the numbers to one hundred in rows of ten). Then the picture is built in front of the child: counters land in a frame one at a time, the path is drawn cell by cell, the chart folds from the path. Sentences of five to eight words, one idea per beat, a pause after each key idea, motions that finish before the voice moves on. `make-ten-remainder` is the voice to match. At most two `term` cards; "fewer" (not as many) and "pair" (two together) are the words a child at this grade may not know; "greater" and "less" are said as "bigger number" and "smaller number" and the formal word given beside it.

### The habits, in this family's form

1. **Read.** The question is read aloud exactly as shown, and the word that says what is asked goes gold. One beat says what kind of answer is coming: "a number", "a letter", "zero or one".
2. **At a glance.** What is known with no counting: a full frame row is five; a group of two or three is seen, not counted; a row that sticks out after matching has more; the missing number sits between its neighbors; a skip count ends the way its pattern ends.
3. **The sure way.** Count with touches, one word per thing. Count on from what is already known (the five, the full frame, the neighbor on the chart). Match before comparing. Add the step and check the pattern. Pair, then look at what is left.
4. **Sense.** The answer must be a counting word (and a count in two frames is at most twenty); a comparison answer is the row with leftovers; a missing number is said right between its neighbors; a leftover is zero or one and nothing else.
5. **Check.** Count again from the other end or in a different order. Match in the other direction. Count the whole run of numbers aloud with the answer in it. On the chart, confirm from both neighbors (left plus one, above plus ten). Count the pairs by twos and add the leftover to get back the total.

### The five questions this family asks

**How many?** (`count-objects`, `numeral-for-quantity`.) Thinking: see what can be seen (a full frame row is five, a full frame is ten, small groups at a glance), then count on by touching the rest. In a frame, the answer is the full frames times ten plus the loose counters, and the numeral is written ten first. In a scatter, the counted ones move so nothing is counted twice; in a circle, the start is marked. The answer is the last word, written as its numeral. Check: count the other way. Line: "One word, one thing. The last word tells how many."

**What is missing, or what comes next?** (`count-sequence-missing`, `hundred-chart-missing`.) Thinking: never go back to one. Take the number just before the gap and say its next word; the number just after the gap must be the word after that, which is the check. When the gap is after a nine (37, 38, 39, □) the next word starts a new ten: forty, a 4 and a 0, one digit per place (the place-value family owns the fuse; this family owns the word). By tens, the ones word stays and the tens word moves. On the chart: the square to the left is one less, the square above is ten less and sits in the same column, so its ones digit is the answer's ones digit. Fast: read the column for the ones digit and the row for the tens word. Sense: the answer is between its neighbors. Line: "After nine, a new ten." and "Down a row is ten more."

**Which group has more, fewer, or the same?** (`compare-groups`, `dot-group-compared-to-number`.) Thinking: match first, count second. Pair the two rows; the row with leftovers has more; the other has fewer; no leftovers is the same. When one side is a number instead of a group ("more than 8"), count the group with touches and say which comes later in the count. For a multiple-choice set of lettered groups, a group that is visibly tiny next to the target dims at once with the reason ("three is not more than eight"); groups near the target are counted, never guessed. Three-choice problems rule out "the same" the moment a leftover appears; then the gold word ("fewer") picks the row without leftovers. Line: "Pair them up. Leftovers mean more."

**Which number is bigger?** (`greater-numeral`.) Thinking: the number you say later when counting is greater, and on the path it is farther along. Zero is none, so it is less than every other number. The trap is the question word: children answer the bigger number out of habit even when asked "which is less", so the gold word and the beat "it wants the smaller one" come before the answer. Check: count up from one; the number you reach first is less. Line: "Later in the count is greater."

**Count by fives, tens, hundreds: what comes next?** (`skip-count-missing`.) Thinking: the step is the gap between two given numbers; add it to the last one. The pattern is the check: by fives the ending flips between five and zero (or between two other endings if the count starts off a multiple); by tens the ending stays; by hundreds the last two digits stay. The one hard moment is crossing a hundred (795, then 800; 990, then 1,000), where family 2's "ten of these make one of those" is named in one line. On the chart the hops show as every other square in two columns (fives) or a column walked down (tens). Line: "Add the step. Check the ending."

**Any left over?** (`pairing-leftover`.) Thinking: pairs first, then the rule. Counters slide into twos; what is left is zero or one, never more. Then the picture is read as a fact about the number: 14 pairs off completely, 13 leaves one, and the ones digit says which (0, 2, 4, 6, 8 pair off; 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 leave one). The rule always works and is taught as what the pairs show. Sense: the only possible answers are 0 and 1. Check: count the pairs by twos and add the leftover: the total comes back. Line: "Pairs, no leftover: even. One left over: odd. Only zero or one can be left."

### Realistic misconceptions, and the picture that ends each

These are what children in this band actually do, from classrooms and the assessment literature, not slots to fill.

- **Words faster than fingers.** The child says "one-two-three" over two objects, or skips one in a scatter and double-counts one in a circle. The touch ring, one word per ring, with counted objects moving out of the pile, makes the pace the picture.
- **Recounting to answer "how many?"** or answering with the last object instead of the total. The settle: the label stops, drops, and becomes the numeral; the voice never counts again.
- **Longer means more.** Five spread out beats seven bunched up. The match lines on aligned rows end it; the lesson shows the unequal spacing first on purpose.
- **Teen trouble.** "Fourteen" heard as "forty"; 13 written as 31 because the word says the three first; eleven and twelve not recognized as ten and some. Frames: a full frame and the loose counters, the numeral written ten first under the full frame.
- **Stalling at the decade.** "...thirty-nine" and then silence, "thirty-ten", or a skipped decade ("thirty-nine, fifty"). The chart row ends and the next row begins; the fold shows why the words start over.
- **Reading the chart one square at a time from 1.** The step arrows (+1 across, +10 down) and the column's shared ones digit replace the long count.
- **Answering the bigger number when asked for the smaller.** The gold word "less" and the beat that says what kind of answer is wanted come before any thinking about the numbers.
- **Continuing a skip count by ones** (735, 736) or by the wrong step (735, 745), or losing the hundred at the crossing ("eight hundred ninety-five, eight hundred one hundred"). The step on the arc, the ending check, and the one named line about ten tens making a hundred.
- **A pair means two groups.** Asked to put 13 into pairs, some children split 13 into two groups and answer with a group size, or answer with the number of pairs. The arches over twos and the lone gold counter make "pair" and "left over" unambiguous; the sense check (zero or one) catches both errors.
- **Zero is not a number.** "Which is greater, 8 or 0" confuses a child for whom zero is nothing to compare. The empty frame with the numeral 0 under it: none is less than any.

### Lines worth remembering

"One word, one thing." "The last word tells how many." "Move them, count again: the same." "The next word is one more." "After nine, a new ten." "Down a row is ten more." "Pair them up. Leftovers mean more." "Later in the count is greater." "Add the step. Check the ending." "Pairs, no leftover: even. One left over: odd."

## 4. Tools to build

What exists: `grid` (a ten-frame is `[5, 2]`, a hundred chart `[10, 10]`, a path `[n, 1]`), `counters` on a grid (round or square), labels in cells, the `@n` live count of a grid, point moves into cells one at a time, `label` sizes up to `xl`, `term`, `watch`, `recap`, the app's ten-frame scene and the problem's own figure through `example: true`. Specified by the place-value design: `numberline` (arcs with the step, drop, zoom) and `dim` for choices. This family needs five additions.

### 4.1 `counters` that are things, in any arrangement, with a `count` that touches

```json
{ "id": "c", "counters": "fa", "n": 7, "shape": "dog", "ink": "accent",
  "layout": "scatter", "region": "R", "seed": 3 }
```

- `shape`: `round`, `square`, or an object from a small plain library drawn at one size (`dog`, `cat`, `apple`, `cup`, `sock`, `house`, `car`, `star`, `fish`, `flower`); the kit refuses a name it lacks. One object per group; a group is one kind of thing.
- `layout`: `frame` (cells of a grid, as now), `line`, `array` (`[cols, rows]`), `circle` (radius from the region), `scatter` (seeded positions inside `region`, never overlapping). The standard names exactly these four arrangements.
- `{"do":"count","target":"c","words":["one","two","three"],"to":"fb","mark":"tick"}`: on each listed word of the beat's `say`, the gold ring lands on the next counter (reading order for lines and arrays, clockwise from a marked start for circles, nearest-first for scatters, or an explicit `"order"` of ids), the counter takes its mark (`tick`, or slides to the next free cell of grid `fb`, or into a `line`), and the group's count label ticks. A `settle` option drops the final label under the group as the numeral with its word.
- `{"do":"rearrange","target":"c","layout":"circle"}`: the same counters move to a new arrangement; count labels do not change.
- Parts: `c.3` a counter, `c.ring` the ring, `c.count` the label.

### 4.2 `match` and `pair`

- `{"do":"match","groups":["a","b"],"words":["this","this","this"]}`: rows `a` and `b` slide onto one left edge at one spacing; a match line draws between the k-th counters on each word; when one group runs out, the unmatched counters of the other glow gold and a small label counts them. Optional `"then":"numerals"` writes both counts under the rows and lights the farther one on a path part if one is on screen.
- `{"do":"pair","target":"c","words":["two","four","six"]}`: counters slide together two at a time under arches, one pair per word, the label counting by twos; a leftover counter glows gold with the label "1 left". Parts: `c.pair.3`, `c.left`.

### 4.3 `numberline` in path mode

```json
{ "id": "p", "numberline": true, "kind": "path", "from": 1, "to": 20, "hide": [13], "token": 7 }
```

- `kind: "path"` draws equal numbered cells instead of ticks. `hide` leaves a cell empty with a gold border until `{"do":"fill","target":"p.cell.13"}` writes the number. `token` places a counter on a cell; `{"do":"hop","target":"p","by":1,"n":3,"words":[...]}` hops it one cell per word, each landing lit; `"by":5` draws the arc with "+5" on it, as the place-value spec draws jumps. The arcs, step label and lit landings are the same objects the number line uses, so grade 1's handoff (cells become hops) is a change of `kind`, not of picture.
- Parts: `p.cell.13`, `p.token`, `p.arc.k`.

### 4.4 `chart`: the hundred chart with a window, steps and the fold

```json
{ "id": "h", "chart": true, "to": 100, "window": { "rows": [5, 7], "cols": [4, 8] }, "hide": [56] }
```

- Ten columns, 1 to `to` (100, or 701 to 800 by `from: 701` for grade 2), equal cells; `window` shows a fragment at full cell size with the rest ghosted muted (the ghost can be hidden); `hide` leaves cells empty with a gold border.
- Actions: `{"do":"row","target":"h.row.5"}` and `{"do":"col","target":"h.col.6"}` light a row or column; `{"do":"step","from":55,"to":56,"tex":"+1"}` draws the arrow between neighbors with its label; `{"do":"fill","target":"h.cell.56"}` writes the number; `{"do":"hop","by":5,"from":5,"n":6,"words":[...]}` lights landing squares in turn with arcs; `{"do":"fold","from":"p"}` breaks a path part into rows of ten and stacks them into this chart, once, in the foundations shot.
- Parts: `h.cell.56`, `h.row.5`, `h.col.6`, `h.ghost`.

### 4.5 `numeral` strokes (last, optional)

`{"do":"write-numeral","value":13,"at":"P"}` draws the digits as handwriting strokes, ten first, under the group. Serves `numeral-for-quantity` and the settle; a static `label` is acceptable until it exists.

### Build order

1. Object counters, layouts and `count` with the touch ring and settle: five of the nine types use them.
2. `match` and `pair`: the comparison and parity types, and the proving motions of the family.
3. `numberline` path mode: counting on, missing numbers, skip hops; a small extension of a component specified by the place-value design.
4. `chart`: the one new figure; also serves family 2's grade 1 tens-and-ones and family 7's multiples later.
5. Numeral strokes.

## 5. Which lessons need the strongest writer

The kindergarten voice is the risk in this family more than the mathematics: plain words, a slow hand, nothing assumed. A cheaper writer drifts toward a third grader's register without noticing. So the blind test for this family is run on a K lesson, not a grade 2 one.

**Strongest writer:**
- `count-objects`: the family's first lesson. It must say when we count and what counting is, build the touch, the settle and the rearrange from nothing, and do it in a five-year-old's words. Everything after it refers back to it.
- `compare-groups`: the motion proves (match lines on aligned rows), the trap is deep (longer means more), the word "fewer" must be defined, and the three choices are reasoned about. A weak author describes matching instead of staging it.
- `hundred-chart-missing`: the chart is built live by the fold, the word pattern is named, and "down a row is ten more" is taught at the top edge of K without leaning on place value the child does not have yet.
- `pairing-leftover`: a picture that becomes a rule; the author must sequence pairs first, rule second, and keep the sense check (zero or one) from sounding like a trick.

**Capable cheaper writer, from a concept, once the kit is complete (the seam):**
- `numeral-for-quantity`: one component (frames), one idea (see the five, count on, write ten first), short.
- `greater-numeral`: one idea (later in the count is greater), the path, the gold word; short.
- `count-sequence-missing`: one method (the neighbor's next word) on the path, with the decade case as the quick second case.
- `skip-count-missing`: one method (add the step, check the ending) with hops, the hundred crossing as the quick second case.
- `dot-group-compared-to-number`: once `compare-groups` has established matching, this is the multiple-choice reading of the same idea; the dim with its reason is the only craft.

Why the seam sits there: these five are one-method types with the picture in a single named component, the concept fully written, the pitfalls already shown in the strong lesson of the same kind, and the slips (a wrong count, a word unsynced) caught by `--dry` and a strong review. The first four are where the design *is* the screenplay.

## 6. What looks badly conceived, misfiled, or off grade; and what the standards ask that no problem practices

> Base cases and trick questions are wanted in the app. Flagging a seed below means it is a weak main example, not that the app should drop it.

**Problem types.**
- `count-objects`: the film seed is one dot. The main example should be seven to ten things in a scatter (the hardest arrangement to keep track of), with a line, an array and a circle as the arrangements the rearrange motion visits. Check that the generator produces all four arrangements the standard names, and that scatters stop at 10 while lines, arrays and circles reach 20; a scatter of 20 is above grade. Vary the objects, not only dots.
- `numeral-for-quantity`: confirm it reaches 20 (two frames) and includes 0 (an empty frame), which K.CC.A.3 names explicitly.
- `dot-group-compared-to-number`: groups "of up to 20 dots" must be arranged (frames, lines, arrays) above 10, or they exceed K.CC.B.5's scatter limit and the child cannot count them reliably.
- `hundred-chart-missing`: a fragment whose only given neighbor is above or below asks for "ten more", a grade 1 idea (1.NBT.C.5). Either guarantee a left or right neighbor, or accept the stretch; the lesson teaches the column pattern either way because the chart shows it.
- `count-sequence-missing` "by tens": if it ever counts by tens from a number that is not a multiple of ten (23, 33, 43), that is grade 1; K.CC.A.1's "by tens" is the decade sequence.
- `greater-numeral`: 0 is outside K.CC.C.7's "between 1 and 10" but a good trick case; keep it, and let the lesson define zero as none.
- `skip-count-missing`: starts at 725 and counts by 5; check whether any variant starts off a multiple (23, 28, 33), which breaks the ending rule as a shortcut and leaves it as a check, and whether any variant crosses a hundred, which is the case that trips children and must appear.
- `pairing-leftover`: seeds of 2 and 1 counter are degenerate as main examples; use a teen (13 or 14) so the pairs are many and the ones-digit rule earns its keep. The type asks only for the leftover count; it never asks the child to say "odd" or "even".
- Family placement: `skip-counting` and `odd-and-even` sit with the kindergarten types because they are counting done in groups, and that is right; their lessons name family 2 (hundreds) and family 6 (doubles) as what they stand on.

**Standard clauses no problem practices.**
- K.CC.B.5: "given a number from 1–20, count out that many objects." Nothing asks the child to make a group of a given size. This is a different act from counting a group shown, and tests ask it.
- K.CC.B.4c: "each successive number name refers to a quantity that is one larger." Only implicit in `count-sequence-missing`; no type puts one more object with a group and asks for the new count.
- K.CC.B.4b: "the number of objects is the same regardless of their arrangement." Not askable as a problem in our format, so it must be taught in the lesson (the rearrange), and it is.
- K.CC.C.7 is practiced; K.CC.C.6's "equal to" is practiced by `compare-groups` ("the same number") and the "same as the Start group" variant.
- 2.NBT.A.2: "Count within 1000" by ones (398, 399, □; 699, 700) has no type at grade 2; `count-sequence-missing` stops at 100.
- 2.OA.C.3: "determine whether a group ... has an odd or even number" by name, and "write an equation to express an even number as a sum of two equal addends" (8 = 4 + 4). Neither is practiced; `pairing-leftover` asks only the leftover count. Two small new types would close the standard and the second one is the natural handoff to doubles in family 6.
