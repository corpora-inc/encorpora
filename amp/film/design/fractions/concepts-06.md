# Fractions, part 6: multiplication and division (grades 5 to 6)

Ten problem types across three skills. Every film here opens on the picture that *is* the idea (the cross-cut square, the scaled bar, the fit, the share), because at this level the foundation is the whole lesson. The fraction is already a number to these children; what is new is what multiplying and dividing *do* to it, and the pictures prove that before any symbol is written. Each concept names the family foundation it stands on (§1 of the design).

---

### Grade 5: fraction-multiplication (5.NF.B.4, B.5, B.6)

Shared idea: times a fraction is "of" after the shares are drawn. Of a square: cut both ways. Of a length: cut into bottom-many, keep top-many; the bar gets shorter, stays, or grows depending on whether the top is under, equal to, or over the bottom.

**`multiply-fractions` — A fraction times a fraction.** Foundation 11.
Standard: 5.NF.B.4a, "(2/3) × (4/5) = 8/15. (In general, (a/b) × (c/d) = ac/bd.)" This is also the app's only brush with B.4b ("represent fraction products as rectangular areas"); the lesson leans on it.
Foundations first: a square labeled **1** on both sides: "this square is one whole." "4/5 of the square": cut into 5 columns, name the first 1/5, shade 4 rust. "2/3 of *that*": cut into 3 rows, name 1/3, shade 2 teal. Where both colors fall it turns gold: a piece of a piece. Handoff, once: "the hundredths grid was this square cut into tenths both ways."
Read: "What is 5/10 × 2/4?" Times a fraction is "of": 5/10 of 2/4 (the order does not matter; the overlap is the same). At a glance: both factors are under 1, so the product is smaller than either; both factors *are* one half, so the answer is a half of a half.
Example A, the definition: 2/3 × 4/5. (1) The whole square. (2) 5 columns, 4 rust: 4/5 across the top. (3) 3 rows, 2 teal: 2/3 down the side. (4) Gold overlap: count it, 2 × 4 = 8 pieces; count the square, 3 × 5 = 15 pieces; so each gold piece is 1/15. (5) 8/15. Board: 2/3 × 4/5 = (2 × 4)/(3 × 5) = 8/15, tops in gold with the overlap, bottoms with the whole square.
Example B, the app's case, because it adds simplifying: 5/10 × 2/4. (1) 10 columns, 5 rust: the left half. (2) 4 rows, 2 teal: the top half. (3) Overlap: 5 × 2 = 10 pieces of 4 × 10 = 40: 10/40. (4) The gold is one corner of the square: four equal corners: merge: 1/4. (5) Board: 10/40 = 1/4; then the cancel-first way: 5/10 is 1/2, 2/4 is 1/2, 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4: "simplify each factor first and the numbers stay small", the same square cut 2 by 2.
Fast: tops times tops, bottoms times bottoms; simplify the factors first when you can.
Sense: a half of a half is a quarter; two factors under one make something smaller than either.
Check: the gold region is visibly one quarter of the square; 1/4 is 10 of 40 pieces.
Line: "A piece of a piece: cut both ways. Tops count the pieces; bottoms name them."
Trap: crossing, top times the other bottom, remembered from comparing fractions; or adding tops and bottoms (7/14). On the square the tops count the overlap and the bottoms count the whole; nothing crosses, nothing adds. And the product sits inside both colors, so it cannot be bigger than either factor.
Kit: `area` cross-cut (design 4.4), plus a merge beat: `{"do":"merge","target":"a","into":4}` fades the fine cuts and outlines the overlap as one of four equal parts of the whole.
Writer: strongest; this lesson defines the cross-cut picture for the family.

**`mcas-product-factor-size` — Is the product bigger, smaller or the same?** Multiple choice. Foundation 11's last clause.
Standard: 5.NF.B.5a, exactly: "Comparing the size of a product to the size of one factor on the basis of the size of the other factor, without performing the indicated multiplication."
Foundations first: what "times a fraction" does to a length. A bar is the first factor, 1/3 (drawn to scale as a third of a faint whole above it). Under it, three aligned copies built live: × 2/3 cuts it into 3 and keeps 2: shorter. × 8/8 cuts it into 8 and keeps 8: the same bar. × 9/8 cuts it into 8 and keeps 9, a ninth piece added on the end: longer. Then the words: look at the other factor and compare its top with its bottom. Top under bottom: under 1: shrinks. Equal: 1: same. Over: grows. One line: the order of the factors does not matter (9/8 × 1/7 is 1/7 × 9/8).
Read: the asked word goes gold ("less than", "greater than", "equal to"). The first factor sits in every choice, so only the *other* factor matters. The answer must be the product whose other factor has the size the word asks for.
Example: "Without multiplying, which product is less than 1/3?" Choices 1/3 × 9/8, 1/3 × 5/3, 1/3 × 2/3, 8/8 × 1/3. (1) The 1/3 bar. (2) Circle the other factor in each choice: 9/8, 5/3, 2/3, 8/8. (3) 8/8 is one: the same length: dim. (4) 9/8 and 5/3: top over bottom: longer: dim together. (5) 2/3: cut the 1/3 bar into 3, keep 2: shorter. 1/3 × 2/3.
Fast: find the other factor; top against bottom.
Sense: two thirds *of* something is less than that something.
Check: the scaled bar is shorter than the original; said once, never by multiplying.
Rule out: for "less than", every choice whose other factor has top ≥ bottom dims in one beat; for "equal", every choice whose other factor has top ≠ bottom; for "greater", top ≤ bottom.
Line: "Look at the other factor. Under one shrinks, one keeps, over one grows."
Trap: "a fraction always makes it smaller," so 9/8 × 6/7 is called less; or "6/6 × 1/7 is bigger because six pieces." The × 9/8 bar grows by one eighth on screen; the × 6/6 bar is the same bar.
Kit: `scale` (design 4.1), `dim`.
Writer: strongest; the whole lesson is the stretch-shrink choreography reading cleanly to scale.

**`scaling-direction-choice` — Compare the product with the starting number.** Multiple choice; two shapes.
First: the catalog's choice text is truncated ("multiplying always makes a numb") and the correct answer "Equal to 52, because 9/9 equals 1" is not among the three choices the app shows. Fix before the lesson ships. Two shapes under one name: (A) how does n × q compare with q, choose the reason; (B) which product is greater (or less) than q. Same idea, so one lesson, two examples.
Standard: 5.NF.B.5b: "explaining why multiplying a given number by a fraction greater than 1 results in a product greater than the given number... less than 1 results in a product smaller... and relating the principle of fraction equivalence a/b = (n×a)/(n×b) to the effect of multiplying a/b by 1."
Foundations first: the same scale picture on a whole-number bar, 52, amount scale under it. Two familiar cases first: × 2 is two copies (longer, grade 3's meaning); × 1/2 is one of two shares (shorter). Then 9/9: cut into 9 equal pieces and keep all 9: nothing changed. "9/9 is nine ninths. That is one whole. Times one changes nothing."
Read: "Without calculating, how does 9/9 × 52 compare with 52?" The answer is a direction *and* a reason, and the reason must be about the multiplier. For shape B: circle the multiplier in each choice.
Example A: 9/9 × 52. (1) Bar 52. (2) Cut 9, keep 9: the same bar. Equal. (3) The reasons: "always greater" is killed by 1/2 × 52 = 26; "always smaller" by 2 × 52 = 104; "a fraction does not change the amount" by that same 26. The three dim in one beat: "'always' and 'any fraction' are both false; one half of 52 is 26." (4) "Equal to 52, because 9/9 equals 1."
Example B, brief: "Which product is greater than 64?" 64 × 2/3, 4/3, 4/5, 1/2. Only 4/3 has its top over its bottom; cut 64 into 3, keep 4: longer. 64 × 4/3.
Fast: top against bottom of the multiplier; top equals bottom is one.
Sense: 4/3 is more than one whole, so more than one copy of 64.
Check: compute once, privately, to see the picture was honest: 64 × 4/3 = 85 1/3.
Rule out: A, any reason with "always" or "any fraction"; B, every multiplier with top ≤ bottom (or ≥, for "less") dims together.
Line: "Times b/b is times one. Over one grows, under one shrinks."
Trap: "multiplying always makes a number bigger," which is a literal decoy, and its mirror for fractions. The half-of-52 and two-copies bars, drawn once at the top, answer both.
Kit: `scale` on a bar with a whole-number amount; `dim`; the board check should report a choice whose text is cut off.
Writer: strongest; the reasoning sentences are the content.

**`fraction-of-quantity` — A fraction of an amount.** Foundation 9.
First: this duplicates `fraction-of-set` with a measure in place of a set (design §6); keep one under 5.NF.B.4a. Its seed, 1/2 of 8, hides the method: a child says "half of eight" and never meets the top. The main example is 3/4 of 8 liters; the app's 1/2 case is one beat.
Standard: 5.NF.B.4a: "Interpret the product (a/b) × q as a parts of a partition of q into b equal parts; equivalently, as the result of a sequence of operations a × q ÷ b." B.6 for the story.
Foundations first: the whole is the amount: a bar labeled **8 liters** with the amount scale 0 to 8 under it. "The bar you drew for stories: now its parts are fourths." "3/4 of 8 liters": cut the 8 into 4 equal shares, each 8 ÷ 4 = 2 liters, the 2 written in every piece; take 3 shares. "Of" is pointed to as the reading cue only after the shares are drawn.
Read: "You use 3/4 of its water. How many liters?" Gold on "3/4 of". At a glance: less than 8, because it is a part; more than half of 8, because 3/4 is more than 1/2; a whole number, because 8 splits into 4 evenly.
Example: 3/4 of 8 liters. (1) Bar 8 liters. (2) Cut into 4; 2 in each piece. (3) Shade 3, the amount scale reading 2, 4, 6. (4) 6 liters. Board: 8 ÷ 4 = 2, 3 × 2 = 6; then the other order on one line, 3 × 8 = 24, 24 ÷ 4 = 6, "the same; use it when the first division is not whole." Quick beat, the app's case: 1/2 of 8: cut in 2, keep 1: 4.
Fast: divide by the bottom, multiply by the top.
Sense: between half of 8 and all of 8.
Check: 6 of the 8 liters is 6/8 = 3/4; four shares of 2 make 8.
Line: "Cut the amount into bottom-many shares; take top-many."
Trap: 8 × 3 = 24, or 8 ÷ 3; or stopping at one share (2). The bar holds four shares of 2 with three shaded, and the answer is read off the scale.
Kit: none beyond the existing `bar` (whole, unit, per-piece values, counted shade).
Writer: a capable cheaper model; one method on a finished tool.

**`share-remainder-fractions` — Two parts and the rest; one part, many weeks.** Mixed; two stories. Foundations 9 and 7.
First: two genuinely different stories under one name (parts of one whole with "the rest"; a periodic part copied over periods). It teaches reading. Consider splitting; until then the lesson has one example per kind.
Standard: 5.NF.B.6, "Solve real world problems involving multiplication of fractions... by using visual fraction models or equations"; B.4a for each part.
Foundations first: read the whole story. The whole is a bar; every named fraction is "of the whole" (cut, shares, take); "the rest" is what the bar has not shaded; "each week, for 5 weeks" is the same part five times. One question tells the stories apart: is the whole being *cut up*, or *copied*?
Read: "Of the 120 students, 2/5 walked and 1/2 rode a bus. The rest rode in a car." Whole: 120; two parts, each of 120; the answer is what is left, and it is small (2/5 and 1/2 is nearly everyone). "A shop earns 225 dollars each week and puts 2/3 of each week's amount toward repairs. How much in 5 weeks?" Whole: one week's 225; the fund gets 2/3 of it every week; the answer is five copies, so more than three weeks' pay.
Example 1, the rest: (1) Bar 120 students. (2) 2/5: cut into 5, 24 each; shade 2 from the left, teal: 48. (3) 1/2: 60, shaded rust from the right end. (4) The unshaded middle is the rest: 120 − 48 − 60 = 12. (5) 12 students. Check beat: the rest is 1 − 2/5 − 1/2 = 1/10 of the bar, and 1/10 of 120 is 12.
Example 2, the periods: (1) Bar 225, cut into 3, 75 each, shade 2: 150 toward repairs this week. (2) "Each week, for five weeks": the bar is stamped five times along a chain, the total counting 150, 300, 450, 600, 750. (3) 750 dollars. Check beat: 2/3 of all five weeks (1,125) is also 750.
Fast: kind 1, each part from the whole, then whole minus both. Kind 2, one period's part, times the periods.
Sense: the rest is less than either part here; five weeks' fund is more than three weeks' pay and less than five.
Check: 48 + 60 + 12 = 120; 150 × 5 = 750.
Line: "The rest is what the bar has not shaded." "One week's share, then one copy per week."
Trap: stopping early: 120 − 48 = 72 (one part subtracted), or 150 as the fund total (the weeks forgotten). The full bar shows three regions; the chain shows five copies.
Kit: `bar` shading in two inks from opposite ends (a second shaded interval with its own ink); `copy` of a whole bar n times with a running total.
Writer: strongest; mixed reading, and the example per kind must expose the reading.

### Grade 5: unit-fraction-division (5.NF.B.7)

Shared idea: division's two meanings from family 7, now with a fraction in one slot. Share: a piece split among people; each gets a smaller piece. Count: how many pieces fit in some wholes; each whole holds bottom-many. In both, the amount you start with is written first.

**`unit-fraction-share` — Share a unit fraction among people.** Foundation 12, first half.
Standard: 5.NF.B.7a: "Interpret division of a unit fraction by a non-zero whole number... explain that (1/3) ÷ 4 = 1/12 because (1/12) × 4 = 1/3"; c for the liters story.
Foundations first: "shared equally" is division, and the amount shared comes first: 1/4 ÷ 2. The whole is 1 liter, a bar; cut into 4, one quarter shaded: 1/4 liter. A piece's name is how many of it fill the whole, so a new piece is named by cutting the whole the same way and counting.
Read: "Share 1/4 liter equally among 2 people. How many liters each?" Gold on "each". At a glance: each gets less than 1/4; the answer is a unit fraction.
Example: (1) 1-liter bar, fourths, one shaded. (2) Two people: the shaded quarter is cut in 2; one half of it lights for one person. (3) Name that piece: the same cut runs across the other quarters: 8 equal pieces fill the liter: it is 1/8. (4) 1/8 liter each. Board: 1/4 ÷ 2 = 1/(4 × 2) = 1/8. Quick second case, so "double the bottom" is not what the child learns: 1/3 ÷ 4: each third cut in 4, 12 pieces in the whole: 1/12.
Fast: keep the 1; multiply the bottom by the number of people.
Sense: a share of a quarter is smaller than a quarter.
Check: two shares of 1/8 are 2/8 = 1/4, the standard's own check.
Line: "A share of a piece is a smaller piece: bottom times the people."
Trap: 1/4 ÷ 2 = 1/2 (the bottom divided), a share bigger than what was shared; or 2/4. The share is visibly smaller than the quarter it came from, and the sense beat says so.
Kit: `share` (the shaded amount cut into n, one share lit, the cut extended across the whole to count the new piece).
Writer: a capable cheaper model, as the design's §5 lists it.

**`whole-unit-groups` — How many unit fractions fit in some wholes.** Foundation 12, second half.
Standard: 5.NF.B.7b: "Interpret division of a whole number by a unit fraction... 4 ÷ (1/5) = 20 because 20 × (1/5) = 4"; c: "How many 1/3-cup servings are in 2 cups?"
Foundations first: "how many containers can be filled" is division asking how many fit; the amount comes first: 3 ÷ 1/8. The whole is 1 liter; a 1/8-liter container is one of 8 equal pieces of it, so one liter fills 8 containers. The bottom says how many fill one whole.
Read: gold on "how many... can be filled". At a glance: more than 3, because each container holds less than a liter; 8 per liter.
Example: 3 liters, 1/8-liter containers. (1) A chain of three whole bars, 1 liter each. (2) The first liter cut into 8; rust copies of the container fill it, counting to 8. (3) The second: to 16. (4) The third: to 24. (5) 24 containers. Board: 3 ÷ 1/8 = 3 × 8 = 24. Quick beat, the base case: 1 ÷ 1/4 = 4: one liter, four containers.
Fast: wholes times the bottom.
Sense: more than 3, eight to a liter.
Check: 24 containers × 1/8 liter = 24/8 = 3 liters.
Line: "Dividing asks how many fit. Each whole holds bottom-many."
Trap: 3 ÷ 1/8 = 3/8, from "dividing makes smaller." Twenty-four containers stand filled on screen, and the check multiplies back to 3.
Kit: `wholes` chain, `fit` with a unit piece.
Writer: strongest; this film defines the fit that `divide-fractions` extends.

**`unit-fraction-division-slots` — Write the division for a share story or a fit story.** Mixed; slots.
First: two stories under one name, and the "complete the sentence" variant (the 1/2-liter jug) is the share kind in different clothing. Three slot layouts; one lesson, two examples.
Standard: 5.NF.B.7a, b and c: "use visual fraction models and equations to represent the problem."
Foundations first: both stories are division because both start with an amount and ask about equal pieces; in a division, the amount you start with is written first. Share: a piece is split among people; each gets a smaller piece, 1/(a × b). Count: how many pieces fit in some wholes; the answer is a count, more than the wholes. Both pictures from the two lessons above, twenty seconds each.
Read: the cue words. "Shared equally among" is a share, and the amount is the fraction (1/5 of a cake). "How many laps make 2 miles" is a count, and the amount is the whole number (2 miles). At a glance: a share answer is a unit fraction smaller than the piece; a count answer is a whole number bigger than the wholes.
Example 1, share: 1/5 of a cake among 3 people. The cake, a fifth shaded, cut in 3, the cut run across: 15 pieces. Slots fill on the words: 1/5 ÷ 3 = 1/15.
Example 2, count: a 2-mile trail, 1/6-mile laps. Two wholes cut into sixths, laps counted 6, 12. Slots: 2 ÷ 1/6 = 12.
Fast: find the amount you start with and write it first; share: bottom times the people; count: wholes times the bottom.
Sense: a share is smaller than the piece; a count is more than the wholes.
Check: 1/15 × 3 = 3/15 = 1/5; 12 × 1/6 = 2.
Line: "What you start with comes first. Sharing makes smaller pieces; fitting makes more of them."
Trap: the division written backwards (3 ÷ 1/5) because the bigger number "should" go first; or 2 ÷ 6 for the laps. The first slot is the story's starting amount, pointed to on the bar before any number is written.
Kit: board slot lines: an equation with empty boxes that fill on the word, by id; `share`, `fit`.
Writer: strongest; mixed reading.

### Grade 6: fraction-division (6.NS.A.1)

**`divide-fractions` — A fraction divided by a fraction.** Foundation 12, whole.
First: the app's method text is keep-change-flip stated as a rule. The lesson derives the flip from fitting and only then names the reciprocal; it never says the chant.
Standard: 6.NS.A.1: "Interpret and compute quotients of fractions... use the relationship between multiplication and division to explain that (2/3) ÷ (3/4) = 8/9 because 3/4 of 8/9 is 2/3. (In general, (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = ad/bc.)"
Foundations first: one line owned from grade 5: dividing asks how many fit, and a piece 1/d fits d to a whole, so ÷ 1/d is × d. New today: the divisor is several pieces, c/d. Build a bar, one whole, cut into tenths; shade 1/2 and rename it on the cut: 5 tenths, the same amount.
Read: "What is 1/2 ÷ 4/10?" Gold on ÷: how many 4/10s fit in 1/2? At a glance: 4/10 is less than 1/2 (5/10), so more than one fits; a little more than one.
Example: (1) The tenths bar, 1/2 = 5/10 shaded. (2) A rust copy of 4/10 laid from the left: fits once; one tenth left over. (3) The leftover laid against a whole copy of 4/10: one of its four tenths: 1/4 of a copy. (4) 1 1/4 copies fit: 1 1/4 = 5/4. (5) Why the flip: how many tenths in 1/2? Times 10: five. Group them in fours: divided by 4: 5/4. "Dividing by 4/10 is times 10, then divided by 4: times 10/4." The divisor turned upside down: name it the reciprocal. (6) The symbols: 1/2 × 10/4 = 10/8 = 5/4, the same as the bar. Three lines on the board agree.
Fast: multiply by the divisor's reciprocal; simplify.
Sense: divisor smaller than dividend: quotient over 1; 4/10 is a bit under a half: a bit over 1.
Check: 5/4 × 4/10 = 20/40 = 1/2, the standard's own check.
Line: "Dividing asks how many fit. Times the bottom, divided by the top: that is times the flip."
Trap: three real ones. Flipping the first fraction or both: the flip is derived from fitting the divisor, so it belongs to the divisor alone. Naming the leftover 1/10 (of the whole) and answering 1 1/10: the leftover is laid against a copy of the divisor and named as a fraction of *it*. Multiplying instead, 1/2 × 4/10 = 1/5: the sense beat kills it, since more than one 4/10 fits.
Kit: `fit` with the partial copy compared to a whole copy; `recut` to tenths.
Writer: strongest; the pilot's hardest lesson, and the proof of the design's method.

**`reciprocal-division-choice` — Which multiplication equals the division?** Multiple choice; several looks.
First: three looks under one name (fraction ÷ fraction; whole ÷ unit fraction and fraction ÷ whole, which are grade 5's 5.NF.B.7; a batches story whose decoys include an addition and multiplying by the divisor itself). One method, so one lesson; one quick beat per look.
Standard: 6.NS.A.1 "(a/b) ÷ (c/d) = ad/bc"; 5.NF.B.7a and b for the whole-number looks.
Foundations first: from `divide-fractions`, quoted in one fit beat, not re-derived: ÷ c/d is × d ÷ c, which is × d/c, because pieces of 1/d fit d to a whole and it takes c of them to make the divisor. Define reciprocal: top and bottom swapped. A whole number n is n/1, so its reciprocal is 1/n; the reciprocal of 1/n is n. Only the divisor flips: it is the thing being fitted.
Read: find the ÷. The number after it is the divisor, gold; the first number stays as it is. In a story, "how many batches from 9 cups, each batch uses 1/2 cup": the amount first, ÷ the batch size: how many fit. At a glance: the right choice begins with the first number unchanged and multiplies by the divisor upside down.
Example: "Which multiplication equals 2/3 ÷ 2/5?" Divisor 2/5, reciprocal 5/2: 2/3 × 5/2. Dim in one beat: 3/2 × 2/5 flipped the first; 2/3 × 2/5 flipped nothing; 3/2 × 5/2 flipped both. "Only the divisor flips." Quick beats: 3/5 ÷ 6: 6 is 6/1, flip to 1/6: 3/5 × 1/6 (sharing among 6: smaller). 10 ÷ 1/2: flip to 2: 10 × 2, not 10 ÷ 2 (twenty halves in ten). The story: 8 meters of ribbon, 5/8 meter a batch: 8 ÷ 5/8 = 8 × 8/5; the + and the × 5/8 dim: adding is not fitting, and × 5/8 takes a part of 8, giving fewer when there must be more.
Fast: point at the divisor, flip it, change ÷ to ×.
Sense: dividing by less than one gives more (8 × 8/5 is more than 8); dividing by a whole gives less.
Check: multiply the chosen product by the divisor to get the first number back, once: 2/3 × 5/2 = 5/3, and 5/3 × 2/5 = 10/15 = 2/3.
Rule out: anything with a + or ÷ sign; anything whose first number changed; anything whose second number is the divisor, unflipped.
Line: "Only the divisor flips."
Trap: flipping the first fraction (the 3/2 × 2/5 decoy exactly), flipping both, 10 ÷ 2 for 10 ÷ 1/2, and multiplying by the batch size in the story. Each is a named decoy, dimmed with its reason.
Kit: `dim`; one `fit` beat.
Writer: a capable cheaper model, after `divide-fractions` has set the derivation this concept quotes.
