# Fractions, grades 1 to 7: family design

Nineteen skills, 63 problem types, from "which shape is cut into halves" to a complex fraction with a negative numerator. Planning snapshot: the catalog, the engine templates and `facts.ts`, snapshot 2026-11-09. This document is the family's idea, its picture system, how its lessons go, the kit it needs, who should write what, what in the app is off, and the pilot. The per-type concepts use these foundations and pictures.

---

## 1. The idea underneath the whole family

A fraction is a **count of equal pieces of a stated whole**. The bottom number names the piece (how many of them make the whole); the top number counts how many of those pieces we have. A child who asks three questions in that order, every time, owns the family:

> **What is the whole? What is one piece? How many pieces?**

Everything from grade 1 to grade 7 is this idea and its consequences. The same amount can be made of more, smaller pieces (equivalence). Pieces of the same size can be compared and counted together (comparison, addition). A whole is *b* pieces of *b*, so wholes and pieces trade (mixed numbers, borrowing). A fraction is a number, a place on the line. A fraction is copies of its unit piece (multiplication by a whole). A piece of a piece is a square cut both ways (multiplication). The fraction bar is a divide sign (division). Dividing by a fraction asks how many fit (the flip, as a consequence, not a chant).

This is the same shape of idea as place value: there, ten of these make one of those; here, *b* of these make one whole. The lessons say so once, when wholes and pieces first trade.

### The foundations, in the order they are built

Each later lesson stands on the ones above it and names them in one line rather than re-teaching them.

1. **Equal shares.** (Grade 1) A whole cut into pieces of the same size. Two equal shares are halves; four are fourths, also called quarters. The word that matters is *equal*: a shape cut into one big piece and one small piece has no half in it. The whole is two halves, or four quarters. Cut a half in two and each piece is a quarter, smaller than the half: more shares, smaller shares.

2. **Same size, not same shape.** (Grade 2) Thirds join halves and quarters. A square cut down the middle gives two rectangles; cut corner to corner, two triangles. Each is one of two equal shares of the same whole, so each is a half, the same amount. Equal means the same amount of the whole, whatever the shape.

3. **The bottom names the piece; the top counts the pieces.** (Grade 3) Cut one whole into *b* equal pieces. Each piece is one *b*-th, written 1/*b*; the bottom number says how many such pieces make the whole. *a*/*b* is *a* of those pieces. "Three eighths" is three of the pieces called eighths. The whole is *b*/*b*. The unit fraction 1/*b* is the fraction's "one", the way the square is place value's one.

4. **A fraction is a number: a place on the line.** (Grade 3) The whole is the stretch from 0 to 1. Cut it into *b* equal steps and take *a* steps from 0; the place you land is the number *a*/*b*. Past 1 the counting keeps going: 4/4 lands on 1, 6/2 lands on 3. Steps are counted, never tick marks. (Takes the number line and hops from family 6.)

5. **The same amount can be made of more, smaller pieces.** (Grades 3 to 4) Cut each piece into *n*: there are *n* times as many pieces, each 1/*n* as big, and the shaded length has not moved. *a*/*b* = *na*/*nb*. Two fractions are equal when they are the same length, the same point. Merging pieces back is simplifying. This one move is the engine of every comparison and every sum with unlike pieces.

6. **Comparing: same whole, then same-size pieces.** (Grades 3 to 4) Only fractions of the same whole can be compared. Same bottom: more pieces is more. Same top: the smaller bottom has the bigger pieces, so it is more. Otherwise re-cut both to the same piece. A benchmark (1/2, 1) decides only when one fraction is above it and the other below. Record it with <, =, >.

7. **Only same-size pieces can be counted together.** (Grades 4 to 5) Adding joins pieces of the same whole: add the counts, keep the piece. 1/3 + 1/3 is two thirds; adding never changes the name of the piece. Different pieces are re-cut to a common piece first; the product of the two bottoms always works, and a smaller common piece often exists. Subtracting separates pieces. (The kinship with place value, ones with ones, is said aloud once.)

8. **A whole is *b* pieces of *b*, so wholes and pieces trade.** (Grade 4) 1 2/6 is 6/6 + 2/6 = 8/6. 4/3 is 3/3 + 1/3, one whole and a third. To take 3/4 from 4 1/4, trade one whole for four fourths first: 3 5/4. The amount never changes; only the pieces do.

9. **A fraction is copies of its unit piece.** (Grade 4) *a*/*b* = *a* × 1/*b*, so *n* × *a*/*b* is *n* copies of *a* pieces: (*n* × *a*)/*b*. "*a*/*b* of a set": cut the set into *b* equal groups and take *a* of them. (Takes equal groups from family 7.)

10. **A fraction is a division.** (Grade 5) Share *a* wholes equally among *b* people: cut each whole into *b*, deal one piece from each, and each person holds *a* pieces of size 1/*b*. So *a*/*b* = *a* ÷ *b*; the fraction bar is a divide sign. (Takes division as equal sharing from family 7.)

11. **A fraction of a fraction: cut both ways.** (Grade 5) On a square that is one whole, *c*/*d* is *c* columns of *d*; *a*/*b* of that is *a* rows of *b*; where they overlap are *a* × *c* small pieces, each 1/(*b* × *d*) of the square. Tops multiply, bottoms multiply. Multiplying by less than 1 shrinks, by more than 1 grows, by *b*/*b* changes nothing, which is why equivalence works: *na*/*nb* is *a*/*b* times 1. (Takes the area model from family 7.)

12. **Dividing asks how many fit, or how big a share.** (Grades 5 to 6) *a* ÷ 1/*b*: each whole holds *b* pieces, so *a* × *b* fit. 1/*b* ÷ *n*: one piece cut into *n* is 1/(*bn*). *a*/*b* ÷ *c*/*d*: how many *c*/*d* fit in *a*/*b*? Pieces of size 1/*d* fit *d* to a whole, so times *d*; it takes *c* of them to make one *c*/*d*, so divide by *c*. Times *d*, divided by *c*: times *d*/*c*. The flip is what fitting looks like in symbols; it is taught after the fit, never before. The partial fit at the end is a fraction of the divisor, not of the whole. Check: quotient × divisor = dividend.

13. **A complex fraction is one division.** (Grade 7) The long bar divides the top by the bottom. Make the top one fraction, make the bottom one fraction, then divide. Signs follow family 3.

### What this family takes, and gives

Takes: counting pieces (family 1); part-whole and the number line (6); equal groups, arrays, division as sharing and as "how many groups", the area model (7); the story bar (8); "ten of these make one of those" and the tenths-hundredths flat (2, which also owns the decimals that `fraction-term-chain` and `fraction-total-minus-parts` mix in); signed numbers (3, for complex fractions). Gives: decimals as fractions (2), the ratio tape and percent bar (10), rationals on the line (3), probability as a share (34), slope as rise over run (14), rational expressions (17). A fractions lesson may say "the bar you drew for stories" and a ratio lesson may say "the bar you cut into fifths".

## 2. The visual language

Three pictures carry the family, and they are the curriculum's own: the **bar** (P4) from grade 3 to grade 7, the **number line** (P3) as the bar's bottom edge, and the **unit square** (P5) for products in grade 5. Grades 1 and 2 use the app's own circles and rectangles, because the problems draw them and because a child that age cuts a cookie, not a strip. Counters in groups (P1) serve "a fraction of a set". Pies are never used after grade 2: shares of a circle cannot be compared to scale by eye. Every picture is what the child should sketch on scratch paper: a bar with cuts, a line with 0 and 1, a square cut both ways, dots in rings.

### The whole comes first, always

Nothing is shaded, cut or counted before the whole is drawn and named. A bracket above the bar reads **1 whole**, or the story's unit (**1 liter**, **1 hour**, **the 40 stickers**). Two bars that stand for the same whole are exactly the same width and share their left edge; they are drawn under each other, never side by side. A bar is never drawn narrower or wider than the amount it claims; a chain of wholes (for 5/4, or 4 1/4 liters) is whole-sized bars end to end with a heavier line at each whole boundary.

### The bar and its pieces

Cuts are thin vertical lines, equally spaced to the pixel. The first piece carries its name inside it (**1/8**); when the pieces are too narrow (twelfths, hundredths), only the first piece is labeled, outlined gold while it is named. Shaded pieces are teal; empty pieces are paper with a muted outline. A count label at the bar's right end ticks as pieces shade one at a time, and the fraction is written under the bar in the bar's color. The shaded part is the fraction; the bar is the whole; the label says which.

### The number line is the bar's bottom edge

In grade 3 the line is introduced under a bar: 0 under the left edge, 1 under the right, a tick under every cut, the first step's arc labeled **1/*b***, and a dot that drops from the shaded end onto the line. From then on the line can stand alone, with whole numbers at heavier ticks, step arcs on the jumps that are counted, two dots and a bracket for a gap, and a second row of finer ticks under the first when a point is being renamed (thirds over sixths over twelfths).

### The unit square

A square labeled **1** on both sides. Vertical cuts into *d* columns with *c* of them shaded rust; then horizontal cuts into *b* rows with *a* of them shaded teal; the overlap, where both colors fall, turns gold. The side labels read *c*/*d* across the top and *a*/*b* down the side; the small pieces are counted (*bd* in the square, *ac* in the overlap). The hundredths flat of place value is this square cut into tenths both ways, and the grade-5 lesson says so.

### Shapes, grades 1 and 2

The app's circle, square and rectangle, cut into two, three or four shares: circles by straight cuts through the center, rectangles by vertical, horizontal, both, or corner-to-corner cuts. Decoys are drawn too: a shape cut into four unequal pieces, so "equal" is tested by eye. One share is shaded teal. Equality of shares is proved by motion, not asserted: one share lifts and flips or turns onto another and fits.

### Counters in groups

The app's counters, *n* of them in rows. *b* rings appear; counters slide one at a time, round the rings, until each ring holds *n*/*b*; *a* rings turn teal; the count label reads the answer. The rest stay muted.

### Color means one thing each

- **teal** (accent): the fraction being named, its pieces and its numeral.
- **rust** (warm): the second fraction (compared, added), pieces being taken away, the divisor's copies in a fit, the second direction on the unit square.
- **gold**: one thing at a time: the asked word in the question, the piece while it is named, the cut being made, the deciding ends of two compared bars, the overlap on the square, the whole being traded.
- **muted**: empty pieces, "the rest", ruled-out choices, the dimmed board in a recap.
- Never a different color per piece, never a tinted background on an answer.

### The motions, and what each proves

- **Cut**: *b* − 1 lines grow one at a time, equally spaced, on the counting words; the first piece gets its name. Proves foundation 3: the bottom names the piece.
- **Shade** (count): pieces fill left to right, one per counted word, the count ticking. Proves: the top counts.
- **Drop**: the dot falls from the shaded end onto the line below. Proves foundation 4: a fraction is a place.
- **Re-cut**: every piece is cut into *n*; the shading does not move; the count label rewrites *a* → *na*, the piece label 1/*b* → 1/*nb*, the fraction under the bar rewrites. Proves foundation 5. **Merge** is the reverse, for simplifying.
- **Align**: a second bar, same width, same left edge, appears under the first; a gold marker drops at each shaded end and the gap between them is drawn. Proves foundation 6, and "same whole" by construction. The one time two wholes of different widths are shown (grade 3), the lesson says why: a half of a short bar can be less than a quarter of a long one, so comparisons need the same whole.
- **Join**: the second fraction's pieces, rust, slide onto the end of the first bar's shading and snap to its cuts; the count continues; the piece label never changes. Proves foundation 7, and kills "add the bottoms". **Separate**: pieces turn rust and slide off the right end.
- **Trade a whole**: *b* pieces fuse into one whole (a heavy outline wraps them), or a whole splits into *b* pieces, on the word; the total label never changes. Proves foundation 8 and the borrow in mixed subtraction.
- **Copy**: the shaded *a* pieces are stamped *n* times end to end along a chain of wholes. Proves foundation 9.
- **Group**: counters into rings, *a* rings lit. Proves "*a*/*b* of".
- **Deal**: *a* whole bars each cut into *b*; one piece from each bar slides to each of *b* people; each person holds *a*/*b*. Proves foundation 10.
- **Cross-cut**: the unit square cut one way, shaded; cut the other way, shaded; the overlap counted. Proves foundation 11.
- **Stretch, shrink**: a bar of length *q* beside a bar of *q* × *a*/*b*, built by cutting *q* into *b* and keeping *a* (more than *b* when *a* > *b*: longer). Proves 5.NF.B.5 by eye.
- **Fit**: a rust copy of the divisor is laid along the dividend from the left, again and again, counting; the last partial copy is compared to a whole copy and named as a fraction of it. Proves foundation 12, and that dividing by less than one gives more. **Share** is the partitive case: the shaded amount re-cut and dealt into *n* equal shares, one share named.
- **Dim** (multiple choice): the choices that cannot be right fade together, in one beat, with one reason said aloud. Nothing is struck out one by one.

### The handoffs, said aloud once

Grade 3: "A fraction strip is a shape laid flat. Its length is the whole." and "The number line is the strip's bottom edge, with 0 at the left end." Grade 4: "The bar you drew for stories: now each part is a fraction." Grade 4, first trade: "Four fourths make one whole, the way ten ones make a ten." Grade 5: "The hundredths grid was this square cut into tenths both ways."

## 3. How lessons in this family go

### The opening every grade-3-and-up film shares

Twenty seconds, drawn live before any problem: a bar, "this is one whole"; cut into *b* equal pieces, "each piece is one *b*-th, written 1/*b*; the bottom number tells how many pieces make the whole"; shade *a*, "the top counts the pieces we have". The *b* changes to suit the lesson. Grades 1 and 2 open instead on the word *equal*: a shape cut into equal shares and the same shape cut unequally, named and refused. Grade 5 and 6 films that multiply or divide open on the fit or the cross-cut, since at that level the foundation is the whole lesson.

### The five habits, with fraction content

1. **Read.** First: *what is the whole?* (the strip, the pizza, the stretch 0 to 1, the 40 stickers, the 6.7 gallons, one container). Then: which piece, how many. The word that says what is asked goes gold ("greater", "less", "of the same whole", "how many fourths make one whole", "how much is left", "how many containerfuls", "the rest"). One beat says what the answer must look like: a fraction less than one; a count of pieces; a number of liters less than 4 1/4; more than one container because the container is smaller than the amount.
2. **At a glance.** Top smaller than bottom: less than one; equal: exactly one; bigger: more than one. Top less than half the bottom: below one half. Same bottom: just count. Same top: the smaller bottom wins. A factor of 9/9 or 6/6 is a 1 and does nothing. Dividing by less than one gives more than you started with. A share is less than the total; the rest is the set minus the part. The gap between two points is a count of steps.
3. **The sure fast way.** Make the pieces the same size, then count. Multiply top and bottom by the same number. Add the counts, keep the piece. Trade one whole for *b* pieces. *n* copies of *a* pieces. Cut the set into *b* groups, take *a*. Tops times tops, bottoms times bottoms. Divide: times the bottom, divided by the top of the divisor, which is times the flip. Each of these is shown on the picture before it is said as words.
4. **Sense.** A benchmark estimate before the answer is boxed: 2/5 + 1/2 must be more than 1/2; 7/16 − 5/16 is a little; 2 × 3/4 is between 1 and 2; 1/2 ÷ 4/10 is more than 1 because 4/10 is less than 1/2; a product of two fractions under one is smaller than either. Standardized tests write the wrong answers to fail exactly this test.
5. **Check.** The picture, then the exact check: both numbers scaled by the same factor; both fractions over the same bottom and the tops compared; subtract back; convert the mixed answer back; part plus rest equals the set; quotient times divisor equals the dividend. For multiple choice the ruled-out choices dim together with the reason, then the chosen one is proved.

### By grade

- **Grades 1 to 2.** Short sentences, no notation (the problems use words: "one quarter", "two thirds"). The lesson is about *equal*: cut the shape live, count the shares, name them, cut a half into quarters and watch the pieces shrink, flip one share onto another to show they match. The decoy shape with unequal pieces is on screen and refused by name ("that is not cut into quarters").
- **Grade 3.** The bar is born, then the line under it. Fractions are counted pieces and places; comparing is counting pieces or looking at piece size; the whole must match. Denominators stay within 2, 3, 4, 6, 8.
- **Grade 4.** The re-cut is the move of the year: equivalence, then comparing unlike fractions, then adding like fractions as joining, then wholes and pieces trading, then copies. Every story is a bar from family 8 with fractional parts.
- **Grade 5.** Common pieces for unlike sums; the fraction bar as a divide sign; a fraction of a set by grouping; the cross-cut square; scaling by eye; division as sharing a piece and as fitting pieces into wholes.
- **Grade 6.** Division of fractions: fit first, then the flip as what the fit looks like in symbols; the partial fit named as a fraction of the divisor; the share meaning for "each person's share".
- **Grade 7.** The long bar is a division; simplify top, simplify bottom, divide; signs as in family 3.

### Mixed types teach reading

`like-fraction-sum-story`, `like-fraction-difference`, `mixed-fraction-sum`, `mixed-fraction-difference`, `fraction-term-chain`, `fraction-total-minus-parts`, `share-remainder-fractions`, `unit-fraction-division-slots`, `fraction-choice-compare`, `reciprocal-division-choice` and the two benchmark types each hold more than one story or shape. Their lessons open with the one thing true for all: read the whole story, draw the whole as a bar, and the operation falls out of the picture. "Together" joins; "left", "pours out", "sands off" separate; "each", "shared equally" deals; "each week for 5 weeks" copies; "the rest" is what the bar has not shaded. One example per kind, acted out on the bar; the recap names each kind by what happened, never as steps.

### Fast ways that always work, and the ones not taught

Taught, because they never fail and the picture explains them: re-cut to a common piece (the product of the bottoms always works); the same-bottom count; the same-top piece size; the half-the-bottom test for "above or below one half"; top versus bottom for "above or below one"; wholes times bottom plus top for a mixed number; divide by the bottom then multiply by the top for a fraction of a set; the flip after the fit; multiply back to check any division.

Not taught, or fenced: a benchmark decides only when the two fractions sit on opposite sides of it (said aloud); "bigger bottom means smaller fraction" only with the same top; cross products are not used in this family (they are the common denominator *bd* with the bottoms left unwritten, and a grade-4 child should see the bottoms); "keep, change, flip" and "of means multiply" are never said as rules, though "of" is pointed to as the reading cue after the groups have been drawn.

### Ruling out nonsense choices

- Greater than 1: any choice whose top is not bigger than its bottom dims at once.
- Equivalent to *a*/*b*: a flipped fraction, a fraction with only the top or only the bottom scaled, and one with a different factor on top and bottom dim together ("not the same number on top and bottom").
- Nearest benchmark: compare the top with half the bottom first; the far benchmarks dim.
- Product size: a factor of 1 means equal; a factor over 1 means bigger; under 1, smaller; the choices that say otherwise dim with one reason.
- Division expression: the fraction's top is divided by its bottom, in that order; the reversed and multiplied choices dim.
- The flip: only the divisor flips; choices that flip the first fraction, both, or neither dim together.
- Estimates of a sum or difference of two fractions under one: anything over 2 for a sum, over 1 for a difference, is impossible.
- Points on a line: letters on the wrong side of the halfway mark dim; then steps are counted.

### The misconceptions, and the picture that makes each impossible

These are what children actually do, in rough grade order.

1. **Unequal pieces counted as fractions.** A shape cut into one big and two small pieces called "thirds". The lesson draws it and refuses it; the cut motion spaces its lines equally on purpose.
2. **Equal means same shape.** The flip of one share onto the other proves same size; the lesson says equal means the same amount of the whole.
3. **Counting ticks, or taking the whole line as the whole.** 1/2 placed at the first tick, 3/4 placed three quarters of the way to 2. The bar lies over the line from 0 with its right end at 1; steps are counted on arcs; the whole is marked before anything else.
4. **Bigger bottom, bigger fraction.** 1/8 called more than 1/4 because 8 is more than 4. Two aligned bars; "more pieces, smaller pieces" said on the cut.
5. **Adding tops and bottoms.** 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/6; 2/5 + 1/2 = 3/7. The join motion: the piece label never changes; and the sense check: 3/7 is less than 1/2, so it cannot be 1/2 plus something.
6. **Equivalence by adding the same number.** 2/3 = 3/4. The re-cut multiplies; aligned bars show 2/3 and 3/4 are different lengths.
7. **A mixed number as glued digits.** 1 2/6 read as 12/6 or 3/6. The whole bar splits into six pieces on screen; six and two is eight.
8. **Subtracting the small fraction from the big one, whichever side it is on.** 4 1/4 − 2 3/4 becomes 2 2/4. The trade of a whole for four fourths happens before any piece leaves.
9. **"Of" done by dividing by the top, or stopping at one group.** 2/5 of 40 answered 20 or 8. Five rings, two lit, counted.
10. **Multiplying always grows; dividing always shrinks.** The stretch and shrink bars; the fit that counts more than one.
11. **Flipping the wrong fraction, or both.** The flip is derived from fitting the divisor, so it belongs to the divisor and nothing else.
12. **The leftover as a fraction of the whole.** 2 1/2 ÷ 3/4 answered "3 and a quarter". The last partial fit is laid against a whole copy of the divisor and named as a fraction of it.
13. **A fraction read as the big number divided by the small.** 3/4 "is 4 ÷ 3, because you can't divide 3 by 4". The deal: three bars shared by four people, each person's pile is 3/4.
14. **Different wholes compared.** A half of one pizza called more than three quarters of another. Said once in grade 3 with two bars of different widths, then every comparison lesson draws the same whole.
15. **Nearness judged by the top alone.** 2/5 called "close to 1/4 because 2 is small". Half the bottom is 2 1/2, and 2 is just under it, so 2/5 is just under one half.

### Lines worth remembering

- "First find the whole."
- "The bottom names the piece. The top counts the pieces."
- "Equal means the same size, not the same shape."
- "More pieces, smaller pieces."
- "Cut 0 to 1 into *b*. Count *a* steps. Steps, not ticks."
- "Same amount, smaller pieces: times the same number on top and bottom."
- "Only same-size pieces can be counted together."
- "Add the counts. Keep the piece."
- "A whole is *b* pieces of *b*."
- "*n* copies of *a* pieces."
- "Cut the set into bottom-many groups; take top-many."
- "The fraction bar is a divide sign."
- "A piece of a piece: cut both ways."
- "Times less than one shrinks. Times more than one grows. Times *b*/*b* is times one."
- "Dividing asks how many fit."
- "Times the bottom, divided by the top: that is times the flip."
- "Check a division by multiplying back."

## 4. Tools to build

### What exists

`bar` (`film/bar.ts`): one whole at a named corner, `partition` into *n* with boundary-keeping refinement, `shade` to a fraction, a share scale from 0 to 1 (which is already the number line under the bar) and an amount scale, interior marks with TeX labels, the gold unit outline on the first piece, per-piece values. `circle`, `poly`, `seg`, `flip`, `turn`, `slide`, `morph`, `grid`, `counters`, cell moves and `@n` counts. Board TeX with `\frac` and colors (`\frac{\cg{3}}{4}` lights the top). `table`. The app's own scenes: fraction strips, number line, unit array. Specified in the place-value design: `numberline` (ticks, step arcs, drop, halfway, catchment, zoom), `dim`, `area` on the hundredths flat.

### 4.1 `bar`: the fraction extensions

- `pieces` labels: `"labels": "first"` writes 1/*b* in the first piece (gold outline while named), `"all"` in every piece when they fit; `--dry` reports a label that does not fit.
- `"fraction": true`: the fraction written under the bar in the bar's ink, rewriting on re-cut and merge; `"count": true`: a live piece count at the right end (size xl in grades 3 to 4) that ticks as pieces shade.
- `{"do":"shade","target":"b","pieces":3,"at":"one"}`: shade one piece per counted word (one, two, three) instead of a smooth fill.
- `{"do":"recut","target":"b","each":3}` and `{"do":"merge","target":"b","each":3}`: `partition` refinement with the labels, count and fraction rewriting on the word; the shading does not move.
- `"wholes": 3`: a chain of equal wholes with heavier boundaries and a bracket over each; `b.whole.2` names the second whole, `b.piece.7` the seventh piece of the chain. Fractions past one are drawn here, never on a stretched single bar.
- `"align": "b1"`: a bar that takes its width and left edge from another bar (the same whole by construction). `{"do":"compare","targets":["b1","b2"]}` drops gold markers at both shaded ends and draws the gap.
- `{"do":"join","from":"b2","to":"b1"}`: the shaded pieces of one bar slide onto the end of another's shading and snap to its cuts (both bars must share the piece size; `--dry` refuses otherwise). `{"do":"separate","target":"b","pieces":5}`: that many pieces turn rust and leave off the right end.
- `{"do":"trade","target":"b","whole":2,"into":4}` splits a whole into *b* pieces; `{"do":"trade","target":"b","pieces":"4-7","into":"whole"}` fuses *b* pieces into a whole. The chain's total label never changes.
- `{"do":"copy","target":"b","times":4}`: the shaded pieces stamped *n* times along the chain, counting.
- `{"do":"fit","target":"b","piece":"3/4","ink":"warm"}`: copies of a piece of that length laid along the shaded amount from the left, counting one by one; the last partial copy is drawn beside a whole copy and labeled as a fraction of it. `{"do":"share","target":"b","among":3}`: the shaded amount re-cut as needed and dealt into *n* rows, one row named.
- `{"do":"scale","target":"b","by":"4/3"}`: a second aligned bar built from the first by cutting into *b* and keeping *a*, so the eye sees longer or shorter.
- `"line": true`: the share scale drawn as the number line (0 and 1 under the ends, a tick under each cut, whole numbers at the chain's boundaries, the first step's arc labeled 1/*b*) with `{"do":"drop","target":"b"}` to fall from the shaded end to the line. Scale labels in fractions, `"as":"fractions"`, replacing the percent form.

### 4.2 `numberline`: fractions

Extends the place-value spec: tick labels as fractions (every tick, or only named ones), heavier ticks and labels at whole numbers, several wholes, two points with a gap bracket (`nl.gap`), a second finer tick row for renaming a point (`{"do":"refine","each":2}` draws sixths under thirds and labels the point's new name), and `drop` of a point at *a*/*b* counted on step arcs. The same component serves `fraction-line-position`, the two select types and `fraction-distance-gap`.

### 4.3 `shares`: equal shares of a shape, grades 1 and 2

```json
{ "id": "s", "shares": "circle", "at": "C", "r": 3, "cuts": 4, "shaded": 1 }
{ "id": "r", "shares": "rect", "at": "TL", "w": 6, "h": 4, "cuts": "diagonal", "shaded": 1 }
```

- `shares`: `circle`, `square` or `rect`, matching the app's figures. `cuts`: 2, 3 or 4 (equal, through the center or straight across), `"both"` for a 2 × 2 square, `"diagonal"`, or an explicit list of unequal positions for a decoy. `shaded`: how many shares, teal.
- `{"do":"cut","target":"s"}` draws the cuts live on the counting words; `{"do":"match","target":"s.share.1","onto":"s.share.2"}` lifts one share and flips or turns it onto another to show same size (the kit's `flip` and `turn` under the hood); `s.share.k` for highlight and labels.

### 4.4 `area`: the unit square

The place-value `area` generalized: a square labeled 1 on both sides; `{"do":"cut","dir":"cols","into":5,"shade":2,"ink":"warm"}` then `{"do":"cut","dir":"rows","into":3,"shade":1,"ink":"accent"}`; the overlap turns gold and `{"do":"count","target":"a.overlap"}` counts its pieces while the total `bd` is written at the corner. Side labels in fractions. The hundredths flat is the 10 × 10 case.

### 4.5 `group` for counters

`{"do":"group","target":"c","into":5,"at":"five"}`: rings appear and counters slide round them one at a time until equal; `{"do":"highlight","target":"c.ring.1-2"}` lights *a* rings; the `@n` label counts the lit counters. Rings are addressable so "the rest" can be named and dimmed.

### 4.6 Board

`dim` as specified for place value. Mixed numbers in TeX (`2\tfrac{3}{4}`) and the long fraction bar for complex fractions (`\dfrac{\frac{3}{2}-\frac{14}{9}}{\frac{1}{6}}`) already render; the board check must accept mixed-number lines and fraction equalities in a chain (`\frac{8}{12}+\frac{15}{12}=\frac{23}{12}`), and recognize the problem's canonical answer in any equivalent form (2/4, 1/2, 1 2/4, 3/2) so a lesson can say the answer the way a child would and still pass.

### Build order

1. `bar` extensions (labels, counted shading, recut, chain, align, join and separate, trade, line and drop): grades 3 to 4, the bulk of the family (about 35 types), and the pilot's first four lessons.
2. `numberline` fractions: the two select types and the gap type ride on it; the pilot's line lesson needs `line: true` on the bar first and the stand-alone line second.
3. `fit`, `share`, `copy`, `scale`, `group`, `area`: grades 4 to 6 multiplication and division (about 20 types).
4. `shares`: grades 1 and 2 (7 types), built with the early-grades wave of families 6 and 1 so the voice is tested together.

## 5. Which lessons need the strongest writer

One-method types can be assigned from a complete concept when the concept is strong and the kit fits. In this family the seam runs between lessons whose quality is in the picture's choreography and the reading, and lessons that are one method on a finished tool.

**Strongest writer:**
- Every grade 1 and 2 lesson. The idea is one word, *equal*, carried by a cut and a flip; the voice is for six-year-olds; define the foundations explicitly and make counters represent recognizable objects.
- The lessons that define a picture for the first time: `fraction-model` (the bar), `fraction-line-position` (the line under the bar), `equivalent-numerator` (the re-cut), `fraction-of-set` (groups), `multiply-fractions` (the cross-cut), `whole-sharing-fraction` (the deal), `whole-unit-groups` and `divide-fractions` (the fit and the derived flip). The screenplay *is* the design in these; a weak author describes the motion instead of staging it.
- `mixed-fraction-difference` (the trade before the take), `compare-unlike-fractions` (the reference lesson, redone on the new bar), both benchmark types, the two `scaling` and `product-size` reasoning types, `compare-fractions-reason-blanks`, and `complex-fraction` (signs and a long bar).
- Every mixed reading type listed in section 3: the quality is in choosing the one example per kind that exposes the reading.

**A capable cheaper model, from the written concept, once the kit above exists:**
`unit-piece-size`, `fraction-unit-count`, `fraction-whole-as-parts`, `compare-like-denominators`, `compare-like-numerators`, `compare-fractions`, `equal-area-fraction`, `equal-area-part-size`, `equivalent-denominator`, `fraction-equivalence-choice`, `equivalent-fractions-select`, `compare-fractions-symbol-blanks`, `order-fractions-slots`, `like-fraction-missing-part`, `fraction-decompositions-select`, `mixed-to-improper-numerator`, `improper-whole-part`, `improper-fractional-part`, `unit-fraction-multiple-blanks`, `add-fractions`, `unlike-fraction-difference`, `fraction-distance-gap`, `fraction-division-expression`, `fraction-of-quantity`, `unit-fraction-share`, `fraction-group-count`, `fraction-share-amount`, `reciprocal-division-choice` (after `divide-fractions` has set the derivation the concept will quote). Each is one method, its picture is one component with named actions, its opening is the shared twenty seconds, and `--dry` plus the board check catch the arithmetic. Require independent instructional and visual review.

## 6. What in the problem types should change, and what the standards ask that the app never practices

> Base cases and trick questions (2/2, 9/9 × 52, 2/3 ÷ 2/3) are wanted in the app. A flag here means a weak choice for a lesson's main example, or a mismatch with the standard, never that the app should drop the case.

### Misfiled, off grade, duplicated, or badly displayed

- **Grade leaks.** `eighths-and-parts-beyond-one` sits in grade 2 under 2.G.A.3, which stops at "two, three, or four equal shares"; eighths and amounts past one whole are 3.NF.A.1 and 3.NF.A.3c. Keep the "how many fourths make one whole" variants in grade 2 and move the rest to grade 3 `unit-fractions`. `fraction-benchmark-reasonable` (grade 4, `like-fraction-addition`) is 5.NF.A.2 estimation and a twin of grade 5's `benchmark-fraction-estimate` with a different benchmark set; put both in one grade-5 skill. `fraction-of-set` ("2/5 of 40") is strictly 5.NF.B.4a, not 4.NF.B.4, which is *n* × *a*/*b*; fine as a grade-4 bridge if the template says so.
- **Denominators outside the grade.** Common Core limits grade 3 to denominators 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 and grade 4 to 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 100. Offenders: `fraction-line-names-select` (8/24 and a 2/7 decoy, grade 3); `compare-fractions` (ninths), `equivalent-numerator` and `equivalent-denominator` (ninths), `like-fraction-difference` (sixteenths), `compare-fractions-reason-blanks` (sevenths), all grade 4. Not fatal, but the lesson examples should use in-grade pieces and the templates should know the limits.
- **Duplicates.** `compare-fractions` (grade 4, same denominator, judged 3.NF.A.3) is `compare-like-denominators` again; merge. `fraction-of-quantity` (grade 5) is `fraction-of-set` with a measure instead of a set; keep one under 5.NF.B.4a. `mixed-to-improper-numerator`, `improper-whole-part` and `improper-fractional-part` are three types for one trade; fine as three short films sharing one concept.
- **Answers displayed in forms the grade does not use.** Grade-4 story answers shown as 3/2 liters, 7/2 feet, 25/6 hours and 313/50 liters (`mixed-fraction-difference`, `whole-times-fraction`, `mixed-fraction-sum`); 1/8 and 2/3 for 2/16 and 4/6 in `like-fraction-difference`; 1/2 for 2/4 in `like-fraction-missing-part`. 4.NF.B.3 and B.4 never ask for lowest terms, and a fourth grader answering a liters story says "one and a half liters", which 4.NF.B.4c's "between what two whole numbers" also wants. The checker must accept every equivalent form (the lesson will say 1 2/4, 1 1/2 and 3/2 once each), and the displayed canonical answer in grades 3 and 4 should be the mixed number or the problem's own pieces, as the decimal lesson needed "0.10" to accept "0.1".
- **Weak or degenerate seeds.** `fraction-unit-count` asks "how many pieces are shaded" and takes a count (1) as the answer; it is a counting problem until it asks for the fraction. `fraction-group-count` seeds 2/3 ÷ 2/3 and `fraction-share-amount` seeds 2/2 shared by 3; the lessons' examples need a partial container (2 1/2 ÷ 3/4) and a real fraction shared (3/4 among 3). `fraction-line-position` seeds a whole-number point (6/2 = 3); the lesson shows that case second, after 5/2.
- **A display bug.** `scaling-direction-choice` prints a choice cut off ("multiplying always makes a numb") and the correct answer "Equal to 52, because 9/9 equals 1" is not among the three choices shown; the catalog's choice text is truncated.
- **A generator check.** `order-fractions-slots` claims to order "by comparing each with one half"; three fractions can be ordered by one benchmark only when they fall in three different bands. Either the generator guarantees that, or the lesson's method is common pieces with the benchmark as a first look.

### Standards clauses no problem type practices

- **1.G.A.3**: "Partition circles and rectangles" and "use the phrases *half of*, *fourth of*, and *quarter of*": every grade-1 problem is recognition of a cut already made, and the phrasing "half of the circle" never appears. "Describe the whole as two of, or four of the shares" appears only in the grade-2 eighths type.
- **2.G.A.3**: "Partition" again never asked (recognition only); "describe the whole as two halves, three thirds, four fourths" only through "how many fourths make one whole".
- **3.NF.A.2a, b**: "represent fractions on a number line diagram", "Represent a fraction 1/b on a number line diagram", "marking off a lengths 1/b from 0": the app only reads a marked point; no fractions problem asks the learner to locate a fraction (place value's `decimal-fraction-locate` does, for decimals).
- **3.NF.A.3b**: "Recognize and generate simple equivalent fractions, e.g., 1/2 = 2/4, 4/6 = 2/3": grade 3's `equivalent-fractions` skill holds only `fraction-whole-as-parts`; equivalence generation starts in grade 4.
- **3.NF.A.3c**: "Express 3 in the form 3 = 3/1; recognize that 6/1 = 6": the *n*/1 form is never practiced.
- **3.NF.A.3d** and **4.NF.A.2**: "Recognize that comparisons are valid only when the two fractions refer to the same whole": every problem says "of the same whole", and none ever presents different wholes, so the recognition itself is untested.
- **4.NF.B.3b**: decomposing a mixed number, "2 1/8 = 1 + 1 + 1/8 = 8/8 + 8/8 + 1/8": `fraction-decompositions-select` decomposes only fractions under one.
- **4.NF.B.4c**: "Between what two whole numbers does your answer lie?": never asked, and the displayed improper answers hide it.
- **5.NF.A.2**: "recognize an incorrect result 2/5 + 1/2 = 3/7": no variant presents a given answer to judge.
- **5.NF.B.3**: "answers in the form of fractions or mixed numbers", "9 people want to share a 50-pound sack of rice", "Between what two whole numbers": `whole-sharing-fraction` shares fewer wholes than people, so the answer is always under one.
- **5.NF.B.4b**: "Find the area of a rectangle with fractional side lengths by tiling it with unit squares of the appropriate unit fraction side lengths... represent fraction products as rectangular areas": no problem type at all. The lesson's cross-cut square supplies the idea; the app should have the problem.
- **5.NF.B.6**: "multiplication of fractions and mixed numbers": no mixed-number factor anywhere.
- **6.NS.A.1**: "How wide is a rectangular strip of land with length 3/4 mi and area 1/2 square mi": the missing-side meaning of division is never practiced.

## 7. The pilot

Five problem types, covering the picture born, bent into a line, re-cut, traded, and used to divide: every new `bar` action, the number line, grades 3, 4 and 6. They are built in this order, one at a time through the whole loop.

| # | Problem type | Grade | Why it is in the pilot |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `fraction-model` | 3 | The picture is born: one whole, cut into equal pieces, the piece named, the pieces counted, the fraction written under the bar. The opening every later film reuses. The realistic traps: 5/3 (shaded over unshaded) and unequal pieces. If this is not beautiful, nothing after it will be. |
| 2 | `fraction-line-position` | 3 | The bar laid on the line, 0 to 1 as the whole, steps counted on arcs, past one whole (5/2, then 6/2 = 3). It exercises `line`, `drop` and the fraction `numberline`. The tick-counting trap. |
| 3 | `equivalent-numerator` | 4 | The re-cut: 2/3 into ninths, and the pizza story (each half cut into six). "Same amount, smaller pieces." Exercises `recut` with labels, count and fraction rewriting; kills "add the same number to top and bottom". |
| 4 | `mixed-fraction-difference` | 4 | 4 1/4 liters, pour out 2 3/4: a chain of wholes, the trade of a whole for four fourths before any piece leaves, the separate, the answer said as 1 2/4, 1 1/2 and 3/2. Exercises `wholes`, `trade`, `separate` and the story bar; surfaces the answer-display issue; the wrong-way-subtraction trap. |
| 5 | `divide-fractions` | 6 | 1/2 ÷ 4/10: the fit, more than one because the divisor is smaller; the flip derived from fitting; multiply back to check. Exercises `fit`. The family's hardest idea, and the one most often taught as a chant; if this lands, the method of the whole design is proven. |

If the five land, the second wave is `find-equal-shares` (the grade-1 voice and `shares`), `multiply-fractions` (the cross-cut square), `fraction-of-set` (`group`), `compare-unlike-fractions` redone on the new bar as the anchor, and `fraction-term-chain` as the first mixed reading lesson with decimals from family 2. Then the cheaper-writer seam is tested on the one-method types of section 5, blind, before batch production.
