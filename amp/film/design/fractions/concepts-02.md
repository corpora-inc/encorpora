# Fractions, part 2: concepts for the grade 3 to 4 foundation types

Ten problem types from five skills. Each stands on the family design's foundations and pictures; the opening twenty seconds (one whole, cut into *b* equal pieces, the piece named, *a* shaded) is drawn live in every grade-3-and-up film and is defined in full only in `fraction-model`. Three notes that cut across several types are said once here: the board check must accept every equivalent form of an answer (2/16 for 1/8, 6/2 for 3); grade-3 lessons keep to denominators 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 and grade-4 lessons to 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 100 unless the app's variant forces otherwise, in which case the concept says so; and "of the same whole" is shown to matter exactly once, in `compare-like-denominators`, and named in one line everywhere else.

---

### `fraction-model` — Name the fraction shaded (grade 3)

**Standard.** 3.NF.A.1, both clauses: "a fraction 1/b as the quantity formed by 1 part when a whole is partitioned into b equal parts" and "a/b as the quantity formed by a parts of size 1/b." The app's one variant reads a shaded shape; the lesson also plants "equal", which the clause says and children skip.

**Foundations first.** This is the picture's birth, so it is slow. (1) *The whole.* A bar appears with a bracket, **1 whole**: "First find the whole. Here, it is the whole shape." (2) *Equal pieces.* Seven cut lines grow one at a time while the voice counts the pieces, not the cuts: "one, two ... eight equal pieces. Equal means the same size." A second bar, cut into eight unequal pieces, shows for a beat and is refused by name: "This one is cut into eight too, but not equal. No eighths here." It fades. (3) *Naming the piece.* The first piece outlines gold and is labeled **1/8**: "one eighth. The bottom number tells how many pieces make the whole." (4) *Counting.* Pieces shade one per word, the count label ticking: "The top number counts the pieces we have." The fraction is written under the bar in teal.

**Read.** "What fraction of the whole is shaded?" The three questions in order: what is the whole (the shape, gold), what is one piece (count all the pieces), how many pieces are shaded. At a glance: not every piece is shaded, so the answer is less than one; 5 of 8 is more than half the pieces (half would be 4), so it is more than one half.

**The example.** The app's shape, eight equal pieces, five shaded (if the app shades non-adjacent pieces, the count ticks on each wherever it sits). (1) The whole is named and bracketed. (2) Every piece counted, 1 to 8, outlined as it is counted; the bottom is written first: □/8. "Eighths." (3) The shaded pieces counted, 1 to 5; the top: 5/8. (4) "Five eighths: five of the pieces called eighths." (5) The empty pieces: 3. "Five shaded and three empty make eight, the whole bar."

**Fast.** Count every piece: that is the bottom. Then count the shaded: that is the top. Bottom first, so the piece has a name before anything is counted.

**Sense.** More than half the bar is shaded, and 5/8 is more than 4/8; three pieces are empty, so it is less than one.

**Check.** Shaded plus empty equals the bottom: 5 + 3 = 8. If the total does not match, a piece was skipped or a cut line was counted.

**Line.** "The bottom names the piece. The top counts the pieces."

**Trap.** 5/3: shaded over unshaded. The lesson writes the bottom while all eight pieces are counted, before the shading is looked at, and the check names the 3 as "the rest", not a number in the fraction. Smaller: counting the seven cut lines. The cut motion counts pieces aloud as each one appears.

**Kit.** `bar` with `labels: "first"`, counted `shade`, `count`, `fraction`; `shade` by a list of pieces (`"pieces": [1, 3, 4, 6, 7]`) if the app's figure shades non-adjacent ones; a bar with authored unequal cut positions for the decoy.

**Writer.** Strongest. The picture is born here and every later film's first twenty seconds copy it.

### `fraction-line-position` — The number at a point (grade 3)

**Standard.** 3.NF.A.2b: "Represent a fraction a/b on a number line diagram by marking off a lengths 1/b from 0 ... its endpoint locates the number a/b," with 2a's "defining the interval from 0 to 1 as the whole," and 3.NF.A.3c "recognize fractions that are equivalent to whole numbers" for the app's whole-number points (the seed 6/2 = 3). The standard has the learner mark the lengths off; the app only reads a point. The lesson has the eye do the marking, so the learner is ready for both.

**Foundations first.** The handoff, said once: "The number line is the bar's bottom edge." A bar stands on a line with its left end over 0 and its right end over 1: "The whole is the stretch from 0 to 1." The bar is cut into two; a tick drops under the cut; the first step gets an arc labeled **1/2**: "One step is one half." A dot drops from the end of one shaded piece: "One step from 0 lands on one half. Two steps land on two halves, and that is 1." The bar fades; the line keeps going to 2 and 3 as heavier ticks, each whole cut in two: "Past 1 the counting keeps going: three halves, four halves is 2 ..." Steps are counted on arcs, never ticks.

**Read.** "What number is at P? Each whole interval has 2 equal steps." The piece is a half (gold "2 equal steps"); the answer is a count of halves from 0. At a glance: P sits between 2 and 3, so the answer is between 4/2 and 6/2; a point on a heavy tick is a whole number.

**The example.** Line 0 to 3 in halves, P one step past 2. (1) The piece: the first step's arc, 1/2. (2) Arcs from 0, one per counted word: "one half, two halves, that is 1; three halves, four halves, that is 2; five halves." The dot lands on P. (3) 5/2, "five halves: two wholes and one more half" (the mixed name is grade 4 and is only spoken). **Second case, quick, the app's seed:** P on the heavy tick at 3. Six arcs: 6/2. "Six halves is three wholes. 3 and 6/2 are the same point." The app wants 3; the film says both names. It earns its place because a whole-number point looks like it has no fraction at all, and the child must see it is the sixth step.

**Fast.** Start at the whole number just left of P: each whole holds two steps, so 2 is 4/2; one more step is 5/2. Counting every arc from 0 gives the same answer.

**Sense.** P is past 2 and short of 3, so the number is between 4/2 and 6/2.

**Check.** Count back from the whole on the right: 3 is 6/2; one step back is 5/2.

**Line.** "Cut 0 to 1 into b. Count a steps. Steps, not ticks."

**Trap.** Counting ticks from the left, 0 included (6 for a point at 5/2), or taking the whole line as the whole (5/6: five of six ticks). The bar over 0 to 1 is drawn before anything else, and the arcs count steps.

**Kit.** `bar` with `line: true`, `wholes: 3`, `drop`; `numberline` fractions with heavy whole ticks and step arcs; the bar must be hideable while its line stays (`b.whole` hidden, `b.line` kept). The app's real figure should be drawn by the same component so the lesson's line is the problem's line.

**Writer.** Strongest. This lesson defines the line for the whole family and requires clear visual staging.

### `fraction-line-names-select` — Fractions that name one point (grade 3)

**First.** 8/24 and 2/7 are outside grade 3's denominators (2, 3, 4, 6, 8). The standard fit is right; the template should draw choices from the grade's pieces. The lesson's example uses in-grade pieces and teaches a test that handles 8/24 anyway.

**Standard.** 3.NF.A.3a "two fractions as equivalent (equal) if they are the same size, or the same point on a number line," 3b "Recognize ... simple equivalent fractions," with 3.NF.A.2b for reading P.

**Foundations first.** The line from 0 to 1 cut into 6, the step arc 1/6, P read by counting steps (owned by the previous lesson; one line). The new idea: "One point can have more than one name." A second line, the same 0 and the same 1, appears under the first, cut into two; one step; its dot lands straight under P and a dashed line joins them: "Same point. Three sixths and one half are two names for it."

**Read.** "Select the 2 fractions that name point P." Read P first (its name may not be among the choices); then each choice asks one question: does it land on the same point? Gold on "2": exactly two will.

**The example.** 0 to 1 in sixths, P at 3/6; choices 1/2, 4/8, 2/6, 3/4, 6/3; select 2. (1) P read by steps: 3/6, written above P. (2) Rule out at a glance, two choices dimming together: 2/6 has the same pieces and a different count, one step short, a different point; 6/3 has top over bottom, past 1, off this line. (3) Test 1/2: a line under P's with the same 0 and 1, cut in two, one step; lands under P. Yes. (4) Test 4/8: a third row, cut in eight, four steps; lands under P. Yes. (5) 3/4: cut in four, three steps: past P. No. (6) 1/2 and 4/8. **Quick beat for a bottom too big to draw** (the app's 8/24 for a point at 2/6): "Twenty-fourths: cut each sixth into four. The two shaded steps are cut into four each: eight. Same number times top and bottom, same point." One sixth is cut into four on screen; the rest is said.

**Fast.** Same bottom, different top: a different point, out. Top over bottom: past 1, out. For the rest, draw the choice's line under P's with the same 0 and 1 and see where its dot lands; or, when the pieces are too fine to draw, ask whether the same number multiplies P's top and bottom.

**Sense.** P is halfway, so every true name has a top that is half its bottom: 1 of 2, 4 of 8, 3 of 6.

**Check.** Exactly two chosen, as the question asked; each one's dot sits straight under P.

**Rule out.** Same bottom, different top (the neighbor point, one tick off); top bigger than bottom (past 1); a choice that repeats P's top with another bottom (3/4, "P is at 3").

**Line.** "Same point, different names."

**Trap.** 2/6 for a child who counts ticks, and 3/4 for one who keeps the top and guesses the bottom. The aligned lines decide both without argument.

**Kit.** `numberline` rows that share 0 and 1 (`align`), a dot per row, a dashed vertical link between coincident dots, `refine` on one step; `dim`.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model once the aligned rows and `dim` exist: one test applied to each choice, with the reading owned by the previous lesson.

### `fraction-whole-as-parts` — Wholes as a fraction (grade 3)

**Standard.** 3.NF.A.3c: "Express whole numbers as fractions, and recognize fractions that are equivalent to whole numbers ... locate 4/4 and 1 at the same point." The *n*/1 form in the same clause ("3 = 3/1") is never practiced; the template could ask "Write 2 wholes as ?/1".

**Foundations first.** The opening with thirds. Then all three pieces shade, the count ticks to 3, 3/3 is written, and on the line beneath 3/3 drops onto 1: "The whole is three thirds. A whole is b pieces of b." (Foundation 8, in grade-3 words.)

**Read.** "Write 2 wholes as ?/3. What is the missing numerator?" The bottom is given (gold 3): the pieces are thirds; the question asks how many thirds are in two wholes. At a glance: more than one whole, so the top is bigger than the bottom, and it is a multiple of 3.

**The example.** Two whole bars end to end, a heavier line between them, a bracket over each reading **1 whole**. The first is cut into three: "one, two, three"; the second: "four, five, six," the count running on across the boundary. All six shade, the count reads 6, **6/3** is written under the chain. On the line beneath, 6/3 drops onto the point 2: "Two wholes. Six thirds. The same point." Answer 6. **Quick beat, the seed:** "1 whole as ?/2": one whole is two halves, 2/2. "Any whole is its bottom over itself." One beat; it is the same move.

**Fast.** Wholes times the bottom: 2 × 3 = 6, because each whole holds bottom-many pieces.

**Sense.** The top must be bigger than 3 and a multiple of 3.

**Check.** Count back by threes: three thirds make one whole, three more make two. Or 6 ÷ 3 = 2.

**Line.** "A whole is b pieces of b."

**Trap.** Writing the whole count on top (2/3), or adding (5). Six pieces are counted aloud on screen, and the count keeps going across the whole boundary.

**Kit.** `bar` with `wholes: 2`, counted shading across wholes, `line: true` with whole numbers at the chain boundaries, `drop`.

**Writer.** Cheaper: one chain, one count, one drop.

### `compare-like-denominators` — Same pieces, compare the counts (grade 3)

**Standard.** 3.NF.A.3d: "Compare two fractions with the same ... denominator by reasoning about their size. Recognize that comparisons are valid only when the two fractions refer to the same whole." (The symbols clause is practiced by grade-4 types; here the answer is a fraction.)

**Foundations first.** The opening with eighths. Then the one time different wholes are shown: a long bar with 3/8 shaded over a short bar, half its width, with 7/8 shaded; the 3/8 shading is visibly longer. "Three eighths of this one is more than seven eighths of that one. Different wholes cannot be compared. Every problem here says *of the same whole*, so our bars are the same whole." The short bar stretches to match, aligned on the left edge. "Same whole. Same pieces, eighths. Now only the count differs."

**Read.** "Which is greater: 3/8 or 7/8 of the same whole? Enter that fraction." Gold on "greater" (or "less"). At a glance: the bottoms match, so the pieces match; compare the tops; 7 is more than 3. Then answer with the one the question asked for.

**The example.** (1) Two aligned bars, both cut into eight, the first piece of each labeled 1/8: "same pieces." (2) Three shade teal on the top bar, counting; seven shade rust on the bottom bar, counting. (3) Compare: gold markers at both shaded ends, the gap drawn: four pieces. (4) "Seven eighths is greater: more of the same pieces." Answer 7/8. **Quick beat, the seed:** "Which is less: 1/2 or 2/2?" Two halves is the whole bar; one half is half of it. 1/2. One beat, for the *b*/*b* = 1 case.

**Fast.** Same bottom: compare the tops. Then re-read the question for which one it wants.

**Sense.** 7/8 is one piece short of the whole bar; 3/8 is under half, since half is 4/8.

**Check.** The gap: 7/8 is four eighths more than 3/8; the rust shading reaches farther.

**Line.** "Same pieces: just count."

**Trap.** Answering the greater fraction when the question asked for the less. The asked word is gold, and the last beat re-reads it before the answer is boxed. With like denominators the size reasoning itself rarely goes wrong.

**Kit.** `bar` `align` and `compare`; two bars of different widths for the same-whole beat, one stretching to match.

**Writer.** Cheaper.

### `compare-like-numerators` — Same count, compare the pieces (grade 3)

**Standard.** 3.NF.A.3d, the "same numerator" half of the same clause.

**Foundations first.** The opening with fourths. A second aligned bar is cut into eighths while the voice says it: "More pieces, smaller pieces." The first pieces are labeled 1/4 and 1/8. The eighth lifts and lays itself on the fourth, twice: "Two eighths fit in one fourth. An eighth is smaller." Same whole, one line, owned by the previous lesson.

**Read.** "Which is less: 3/8 or 3/4 of the same whole?" Gold "less". At a glance: the tops match, so both count three pieces; the one made of smaller pieces is less; eight cuts the whole into more, smaller pieces, so 3/8 is less.

**The example.** (1) Two aligned bars, cut into four and into eight, as above. (2) Three pieces shade on each, counting; the teal 3/4 runs most of the bar, the rust 3/8 under half. (3) Compare: gold markers, the gap. (4) "Three pieces each. The eighths are smaller, so three eighths is less." Answer 3/8. **Quick beat, unit fractions:** "Which is greater: 1/4 or 1/8?" One piece each; the bigger piece: 1/4.

**Fast.** Same top: the smaller bottom is the greater fraction, because its pieces are bigger. Said with its fence: only when the tops match.

**Sense.** 3/4 is most of the bar; 3/8 is less than half, since half is 4/8.

**Check.** Cut the fourths into eighths: 3/4 becomes 6/8. Six eighths against three eighths: same pieces, count. (3.NF.A.3b's "1/2 = 2/4" seen on the bar.)

**Line.** "More pieces, smaller pieces."

**Trap.** "Eight is bigger than four, so 3/8 is bigger." The cut is watched: the pieces shrink as the count grows, and the eighth fits twice into the fourth.

**Kit.** `bar` `align`, `compare`, `recut` for the check; a piece lifted and laid on a piece of another bar (the `shares` match motion, for a bar piece).

**Writer.** Cheaper.

### `equal-area-fraction` — One equal-area part as a fraction (grade 3)

**Standard.** 3.G.A.2: "Partition shapes into parts with equal areas. Express the area of each part as a unit fraction of the whole," with 3.NF.A.1's 1/*b*. The learner never partitions; the app presents the partition. Noted for the alignment list.

**Foundations first.** The whole is the rectangle, and its area is counted in unit squares (family 24's line, said once: "Area counts the squares inside"). A 4 by 4 rectangle on a grid, sixteen squares. It is split into eight parts, each two squares, some lying flat, some standing up; as each part is named its two squares light and a small count on it reads 2. "Eight parts, two squares each. Equal area. The shapes differ; the area does not." Foundation 2, in area clothes.

**Read.** "The rectangle is split into parts with equal area. What fraction of the rectangle's area is the shaded part?" Gold "fraction". The shaded part is one part, so the top is 1; the bottom is how many equal parts. At a glance: the answer is 1 over the number of parts.

**The example.** The sixteen-square rectangle in eight parts of two. (1) The whole: the rectangle, sixteen squares. (2) The parts counted, one to eight, each outlined as it is counted; two parts of different shape show their 2 and 2: equal area. (3) "One part of eight equal parts: one eighth of the area." Answer 1/8. (4) The shaded part is also two of the sixteen squares; two eighths of sixteen ... no: eight twos make sixteen, so one part is one eighth.

**Fast.** Count the parts; the answer is 1 over that count.

**Sense.** The shaded part is 2 of 16 squares, and eight parts of 2 fill 16, so 1/8 fits.

**Check.** Squares per part times the number of parts equals the whole: 2 × 8 = 16.

**Line.** "Equal area: same squares, not same shape."

**Trap.** Counting the unit squares as the parts (1/16), or refusing that an upright domino and a flat one are equal. The per-part counts read 2 and 2 while both are outlined. A child who writes 2/16 is right; the checker must accept it.

**Kit.** `grid` with `poly` parts over it, a per-part square count that lights as the part is named, `highlight` a part. Board check: 2/16 accepted as 1/8.

**Writer.** Cheaper.

### `equal-area-part-size` — The area of one equal part (grade 3)

**Standard.** 3.G.A.2 with 3.OA.A.3 ("division within 100 ... equal groups"): the parts have equal area and the whole area is given, so one part is the whole shared equally. The app's seed (12 into 2 parts) is fine for the app; the lesson works 12 into 4 so the move is seen as dividing, not halving.

**Foundations first.** One line owns the previous lesson: "Equal area means the same number of squares, whatever the shape." The new idea: when the whole area is known and the parts are equal, the squares are shared out, whole ÷ parts. The picture: a 3 by 4 rectangle, twelve squares, split into four equal-area parts of differing shape (a row of three, an L of three, and two more).

**Read.** "The rectangle's area is 12 square units. It is split into parts with equal area. What is the area of the shaded part, in square units?" Gold "area" and "square units": the answer is a number of squares, not a fraction. At a glance: less than 12; four equal parts, so a quarter of 12.

**The example.** (1) The whole: 12 squares, the area written on the bracket. (2) The parts counted: four. Equal area. (3) Share the area: the twelve squares light one at a time, dealt round the four parts in turn, each part's count ticking to 3; the board writes 12 ÷ 4 = 3. (4) The shaded part: 3 square units. Its squares are counted directly: three. "Divide, or count. They agree."

**Fast.** Area ÷ number of parts.

**Sense.** Four parts, so each is well under half of 12.

**Check.** 3 × 4 = 12; and count the squares inside the shaded part.

**Line.** "Equal parts share the area equally."

**Trap.** Answering 1/4, the fraction from the sister type, when square units were asked; or 4, the part count. The gold "square units", and the answer written with its unit, "3 square units".

**Kit.** As the previous type, plus a deal that lights squares round the parts in turn with per-part counts.

**Writer.** Cheaper.

### `equivalent-numerator` — Rename with smaller pieces (grade 4)

**Standard.** 4.NF.A.1, every clause: "Explain why a fraction a/b is equivalent to a fraction (n × a)/(n × b) by using visual fraction models, with attention to how the number and size of the parts differ even though the two fractions themselves are the same size. Use this principle to ... generate equivalent fractions." The bare rename, the stories whose "cuts each piece into 6" names *n*, and the factor form "□/□" are all this clause. Ninths (2/3 → 9) are off 4.NF's list; the pilot keeps 2/3 → ninths as the design chose, and the same film works with twelfths (each third into four) if the template adopts the limits.

**Foundations first.** The opening: bar, thirds, two shaded, 2/3. Then the move of the year: "Cut each piece into three. The shading does not move." Lines grow inside every third; the count rewrites 2 → 6, the piece label 1/3 → 1/9, the fraction 2/3 → 6/9; a ghost of the original bar stays aligned above, its shaded end exactly over the new one. "Same amount, smaller pieces. Three times as many pieces, each a third as big." Then the symbols: × 3 drawn on the top, × 3 on the bottom. Then why that is allowed: a whole bar labeled 3/3 = 1 for a beat: "Three thirds is one whole. Times one changes nothing."

**Read.** "Rename 2/3 as ?/9." The bottom went from 3 to 9: how many times? That many times on top. Gold "9". In the stories, "cuts each piece into 6" says *n* in words. In the factor form "1/2 × □/□ = 6/12", both boxes hold the same number, *n*. At a glance: 9 ÷ 3 = 3, so 2 × 3 = 6; and 6/9 is more than half the pieces, as 2/3 is more than half the bar.

**The example.** 2/3 = ?/9. (1) Bar, thirds, two shaded, 2/3. (2) "Ninths: nine pieces in the whole. Three become nine when each is cut into three." 9 ÷ 3 = 3 on the board. (3) Re-cut each third into three, on the words; the shaded count ticks to 6; labels rewrite. (4) The ghost above: the shaded ends coincide. 2/3 = 6/9. (5) × 3 top and bottom. Answer 6. **Second example, the factor form with a story:** "Student E has 1/2 of a sheet of paper. Student E cuts each piece into 6 equal smaller pieces. 1/2 × □/□ = 6/12. What number goes in both boxes?" (1) Bar, halves, one shaded. (2) The story names the cut: each piece into six. Re-cut: twelve pieces, six shaded. (3) 1/2 = 6/12: the one number that multiplied both is 6; 6/6 is a whole, times one. Answer 6. A second example because the question's shape is different (it asks for the factor) and the cut is named in the story's words rather than by the new bottom. **Quick beat:** 2/4 → ?/100: each piece into 25; the pieces are too thin to draw, so the numbers carry it: 50. Hundredths, one line, the handoff to decimals.

**Fast.** New bottom ÷ old bottom gives *n*; old top × *n*. Always works when the new bottom is a multiple of the old.

**Sense.** The shaded end has not moved: 6/9 is more than half of nine pieces, as 2/3 is more than half the bar.

**Check.** The same number undoes both: 6 ÷ 3 = 2, 9 ÷ 3 = 3. Or the aligned ends coincide.

**Line.** "Same amount, smaller pieces: times the same number on top and bottom."

**Trap.** Adding the same number: 3 + 6 = 9, so 2 + 6 = 8, 8/9. An aligned bar shaded 8/9 is visibly longer than 2/3. Cutting pieces multiplies them; nothing is added. In the factor form, writing 12, or 6 over 12, in the boxes: the boxes hold the number of cuts per piece, and it is the same on top and bottom.

**Kit.** `bar` `recut` with count, piece label and fraction rewriting; the aligned ghost (`align` with a muted ink); board boxes (`\square`) and the fraction-chain check.

**Writer.** Strongest. The re-cut is defined here and every grade-4 lesson quotes it.

### `equivalent-denominator` — Rename with bigger pieces (grade 4)

**Standard.** 4.NF.A.1, the same clause run the other way: fewer, larger parts, the same size. Ninths again; 2/8 → 1/4 is the in-grade twin if the template adopts the limits.

**Foundations first.** One line owns the re-cut from the previous lesson. The new move is its reverse: "Three ninths. Put every three pieces together: three pieces become one. The shading does not move." The inner lines fade in groups of three; nine pieces become three; the count rewrites 3 → 1, the piece 1/9 → 1/3, the fraction 3/9 → 1/3; the ghost above shows the shaded end has not moved.

**Read.** "Rename 3/9 as 1/?. What is the missing denominator?" The top went from 3 to 1: three pieces merged into one, so the bottom is merged the same way. Gold "1". At a glance: 3 is a third of 9, so the amount is a third of the bar; the piece must be a third.

**The example.** (1) Bar, ninths, three shaded, 3/9. (2) The top says three pieces became one: merge every three. (3) 9 ÷ 3 = 3 pieces in the whole; labels rewrite; 3/9 = 1/3. (4) ÷ 3 on top and bottom. Answer 3. (5) The other direction, one beat: 1/3 × 3/3 = 3/9.

**Fast.** Old top ÷ new top gives *n*; old bottom ÷ *n*.

**Sense.** Three of nine pieces is a third of the pieces, so the shading is a third of the bar.

**Check.** Multiply back: 1 × 3 = 3, 3 × 3 = 9; or the aligned ends coincide.

**Line.** "Same amount, bigger pieces: divide top and bottom by the same number."

**Trap.** Subtracting: 3 − 2 = 1, so 9 − 2 = 7, 1/7. Merging groups pieces; nothing is taken away. An aligned bar shaded 1/7 is visibly a different length from 3/9.

**Kit.** `bar` `merge` with the labels rewriting; the aligned ghost.

**Writer.** Cheaper, from this concept, once `equivalent-numerator` has set the re-cut it reverses.
