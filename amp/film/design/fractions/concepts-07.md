# Fractions, part 7: fraction division stories and complex fractions

Three concepts. The first two are the story faces of the fit and the share that `divide-fractions` (pilot #5) sets up; the third is the last thing the family does with the fraction bar before algebra takes it over. All three stand on foundation 12 (dividing asks how many fit, or how big a share) and name it in one line rather than re-teaching it.

---

### Grade 6: fraction-division (6.NS.A.1)

**`fraction-group-count` — How many containerfuls?** Foundation 12, measurement meaning. Not badly conceived; the seed 2/3 ÷ 2/3 = 1 is degenerate for a main example (already flagged in section 6) and the example below is a partial container.

Standard: 6.NS.A.1, "solve word problems involving division of fractions by fractions, e.g., by using visual fraction models", and its own example, "How many 3/4-cup servings are in 2/3 of a cup of yogurt?"

Foundations first (about thirty seconds, drawn live): a chain of wholes, each bracketed **1 liter**; the total shaded teal. Under it, aligned on the same left edge and the same whole, one rust bar: **1 container, 3/4 liter**. "A containerful is one of these. The question asks how many of these fit along that. Dividing asks how many fit." Then the one condition for counting fits exactly: both amounts in the same pieces. "Same pieces, then count."

Read: the whole is **1 liter**, marked on every whole of the chain; the total is 5/2 liters (two and a half), the container 3/4 liter; "how many containerfuls" goes gold; "count a partial containerful as a fraction" means the leftover is named against the container. At a glance: the container is smaller than a liter, so there are more containers than liters: more than 2 1/2; 3/4 is close to 1, so only a little more: three and something.

The example: 5/2 liters, containers of 3/4 liter. (1) Chain of three wholes labeled 1 liter; 5/2 shaded: two whole bars and half the third, the fraction 5/2 under the bar. (2) The rust container bar, 3/4 of one whole, aligned under the first whole. (3) Re-cut both into fourths: the total's shading does not move; its label rewrites 5/2 → 10/4 and the piece label reads 1/4; the container reads 3/4 = 3 pieces. "Ten fourths. A container holds three of them." (4) Fit: rust copies of the container are laid along the shading from the left, numbered 1, 2, 3 as they land; nine fourths covered; one fourth remains, gold. (5) The leftover fourth slides down beside a whole rust copy of the container, which is three fourths long: it is one of the container's three pieces. Label: **1/3 of a container**. (6) Board: 5/2 ÷ 3/4 = 10/4 ÷ 3/4 = 10 ÷ 3 = 3 1/3 containers. One beat, said once: "The flip gives the same number: 10/4 × 4/3 = 40/12 = 10/3. The fit is what the flip means," quoting `divide-fractions`.

Quick second case, because the learner will meet it and it is the standard's own: 2/3 cup of yogurt in 3/4-cup servings. Twelfths: 8 pieces of yogurt, a serving is 9. The yogurt does not fill one serving; laid against the serving, it covers 8 of its 9 pieces: 8/9 of a serving. Shown because a total smaller than the container is where children say "it can't be done" or answer 9/8.

Fast: write both with the same bottom; divide the tops; a remainder goes over the container's top as the fraction of a container. Always works. (The flip is the same count in symbols; use whichever you own, and multiply back.)
Sense: more containers than liters, since a container is less than a liter; 3/4 is nearly 1, so about three.
Check: 3 1/3 × 3/4 = 10/3 × 3/4 = 10/4 = 5/2 liters. Or: three containers hold 9/4, plus the 1/4 left over is 10/4.
Line: "Same pieces, then count how many fit. The leftover is a fraction of a container."
Trap: 3 1/4, the leftover named as a fraction of a liter (misconception 12). The lesson lays the leftover beside a whole rust container and asks aloud, "a fraction of what? We are counting containers," and the label reads 1/3 of a container. The board never writes 1/4 after the division sign.
Kit: `fit` as specified in 4.1, with the whole labeled by the story's unit, the divisor drawn first as its own aligned bar before it is stamped, each copy numbered as it lands, the leftover's label reading "1/3 of a container"; `recut` on both bars together; the board check accepting 10 ÷ 3 = 3 1/3 inside a fraction chain and 10/3 as the same answer.
Writer: a capable cheaper model, once `divide-fractions` has landed and `fit` exists; the concept names every beat and the picture is one component.

**`fraction-share-amount` — Each person's share of a fraction.** Foundation 12, sharing meaning, with foundation 11 for why the bottom multiplies. Two things off first: the seed, 2/2 liter among 3, is one whole liter shared by three (1 ÷ 3, a 5.NF.B.3 problem), so nothing in it is a fraction being shared; and the link to 5.NF.B.3 is wrong, since that standard divides a whole by a whole. The type serves 6.NS.A.1 (its own example: "How much chocolate will each person get if 3 people share 1/2 lb of chocolate equally?") and 5.NF.B.7a for the unit-fraction case ("division of a unit fraction by a non-zero whole number"). Relink.

Foundations first: a bar labeled **1 pound**, the amount shaded, the piece named. Under it, one empty row per person, aligned to the same whole. "Share equally: every person gets the same amount, and each share is less than the whole amount. The share is one person's row." Sharing is the division the child did with counters in grade 3, named in one line.

Read: the whole is 1 pound; the amount 3/4; "shared equally among 2" goes gold; the answer is one person's share, a fraction of a pound. At a glance: a share is less than 3/4; two shares make 3/4; so each is half of 3/4, a bit less than a half pound.

The example: 3/4 pound of chocolate shared equally by 2 people. (1) The bar, 1 pound, three fourths shaded teal, 1/4 in the first piece, 3/4 under the bar; two empty rows below, **person 1**, **person 2**. (2) Deal whole pieces: one fourth slides to each row. One fourth is left; it hovers over the gap between the rows and returns: it cannot go whole to one person. (3) Cut every fourth into 2: the shading does not move; the piece label reads 1/8, the count 6, the fraction 6/8. "Same amount, smaller pieces." (4) Deal again: each person takes one small piece from every original piece: three eighths each; the rows fill, 3/8 under each. (5) Board: 3/4 ÷ 2 = 3/(4 × 2) = 3/8 pound each. One beat: "Each person has half of the chocolate: 3/4 ÷ 2 is 1/2 of 3/4. Sharing among *n* is taking one *n*-th," the cross-cut from foundation 11 in one sentence.

Quick second case: 6/8 among 3. Deal whole pieces: two eighths to each row, nothing left: 2/8 each. The method agrees: 6/24, and the pieces merge back to 2/8. Shown because dealing whole pieces is the first thing to try and often finishes the problem; the cut is for when it does not.

Fast: keep the top, multiply the bottom by the number of people; then merge pieces if they group. Always works, even when dealing would have.
Sense: a share is less than the total; the shares together make the total.
Check: share × people = the amount: 2 × 3/8 = 6/8 = 3/4.
Line: "Share among *n*: cut each piece into *n*. Keep the top; the bottom times *n*."
Trap: 3/4 ÷ 2 = 3/2, "because 2 goes into 4" (the division done to the bottom alone), a share bigger than the whole bar. The dealt row is visibly half the chocolate, and the sense beat says it before the answer: a share is less than the total.
Kit: `share` from 4.1, extended with a labeled row per person, the failed deal (a piece slides toward the rows and returns before the re-cut), and the row fractions written under each row; `recut` keeping the shading still; the board check accepting 3/8, 6/24 and 2/8 as the same answer.
Writer: a capable cheaper model, from this concept, once `share` exists; one method, one component, the beats are named.

### Grade 7: complex-fractions (7.NS.A.3)

**`complex-fraction` — One division wearing a long bar.** Foundations 10 and 13, with family 3 for signs. Filed correctly: 7.NS.A.3, "solve ... mathematical problems involving the four operations with rational numbers"; the division is 6.NS.A.1 ("(a/b) ÷ (c/d) = ad/bc"), the subtraction 7.NS.A.1c, the sign 7.NS.A.2b ("–(p/q) = (–p)/q"). The phrase "complex fraction" lives in Common Core only at 7.RP.A.1 (unit rates); this type has no rate, so it belongs where it is.

Foundations first: the problem's own shape on the board, top in teal, bottom in rust, the long bar between them gold. "A complex fraction is a fraction with fractions inside it. The long bar is a divide sign: everything above it is one number, everything below it is one number, and the bar divides the top by the bottom." Three facts, each one line: the long bar groups, so finish the top and finish the bottom before dividing; a whole number is a fraction with bottom 1 (2 = 8/4 when fourths are wanted); dividing by a fraction is times the flip, from `divide-fractions`. And the sign: a negative top over a positive bottom is a negative number.

Read: "Simplify. Give an exact fraction or integer": one number. At a glance, before any work: the sign of the top (3/2 is 1.5, 14/9 is a little more, so the top is a small negative), the sign of the bottom (positive), so the answer is negative; dividing by 1/6 is times 6, so the answer is about six times the size of the top. A positive answer, or a large one, is wrong before it is computed.

The example: (3/2 − 14/9) / (1/6). (1) Top: eighteenths. 27/18 − 28/18. A short number line, ticks labeled only at 0, 1, 27/18 and 28/18: start at 27/18, hop left 28/18, land one eighteenth below zero. −1/18; the top rewrites to it, still teal. (2) Bottom is already one fraction, 1/6. (3) The long bar becomes the sign: −1/18 ÷ 1/6. (4) Times the flip: −1/18 × 6/1 = −6/18. (5) Reduce: −1/3. The sign stayed with the top the whole way. Sense: about −0.06 times 6 is about −0.33. Check: −1/3 × 1/6 = −1/18, the top we started from.

Second example, fully worked but brisk, because the whole number inside is a different case: (1/2) / (3/4 + 2). Bottom: 2 is 8/4, so 3/4 + 8/4 = 11/4 (nearly 3). Top is 1/2. 1/2 ÷ 11/4 = 1/2 × 4/11 = 4/22 = 2/11. Sense: a half split into nearly three parts is about a sixth; 2/11 is about 0.18. Check: 2/11 × 11/4 = 22/44 = 1/2. Then one beat for the fast way: multiply top and bottom by 4, which clears every small bottom at once: 2/(3 + 8) = 2/11. "Times 4/4 is times one; the fraction is the same number." Foundation 11, said once.

Fast: top to one fraction, bottom to one fraction, times the flip of the bottom, reduce; always works. Or multiply top and bottom by the common bottom of every small fraction, which also always works and is often shorter; either way, multiply back.
Sense: the signs of top and bottom give the sign; benchmark each as a decimal or a nearby whole number; dividing by a fraction under 1 enlarges, over 1 shrinks.
Check: answer × bottom = top.
Line: "The long bar is a divide sign. One number on top, one on the bottom, then divide."
Trap: 3/2 − 14/9 done as 14/9 − 3/2 because the smaller is taken from the larger whichever side it is on, giving +1/3 (misconception 8 with a sign on it). The number-line hop lands below zero, and the at-a-glance beat said "negative" before the work began. In the second example: 3/4 + 2 = 5/4, the whole number added to the top alone; the lesson writes 2 as 8/4 before adding, and the sense beat says 3/4 + 2 is nearly 3, which 5/4 is not.
Kit: `numberline` with labels only at named ticks (fractions, including a negative) and a hop arc of a fractional length leftward from a fractional start; board TeX for `\dfrac` with `\frac` inside, the top's teal and the bottom's rust carried through every rewrite until the ÷ line; the board check accepting the step from a complex fraction to a ÷ line, negative fractions in a chain, and −9 as −9/1.
Writer: the strongest writer. The quality is in the sign reasoning, in deciding which single beat earns the number line, and in keeping two worked examples from reading as a recipe; section 5 of the design already places it there.
