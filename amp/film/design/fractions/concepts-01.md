# Fractions, part 1: halves, quarters, thirds, unit fractions (grades 1 to 3)

Ten problem types from three skills, written from the family design. Four things hold for all of them before the per-type concepts.

- **Grades 1 and 2 speak in words, not symbols.** The problems say "three quarters"; so do the films. The first written fraction in the family is 1/6 in `unit-piece-size`, and that lesson defines the symbol. Numbers are said as words; nothing is struck out; a wrong shape is refused by name, not crossed.
- **The word of these grades is *equal*, and the test is fairness.** Every grade 1 and 2 film opens the same way, drawn live: a shape cut into equal shares, named; the same shape cut into pieces that are not the same size, refused ("two friends could not share this fairly; it is not cut into halves"). Equal is proved by motion: one share lifts and turns or flips onto another and fits.
- **Same size, not same shape** is proved, never asserted, by a cut-and-turn: a triangle half of a rectangle, cut down its middle and the small piece given a half turn, becomes the rectangle half exactly; a quarter strip cut across its middle and slid side by side becomes a quarter cell. These two motions are the spine of `same-share-different-shape` and `congruent-fourths-pair`.
- **Pieces are counted, never lines.** A rectangle in thirds has two lines; a circle in thirds has three; a strip in eighths has seven lines. Children count lines. Every count in these films lights a piece per counted word.

`find-equal-shares` and `find-equal-thirds` are one lesson at two grades (the grade 2 film adds thirds and the lines-versus-pieces trap); `name-equal-share` and `name-share` likewise. The same writer should do each pair together.

### Grade 1: halves-and-quarters (1.G.A.3)

#### `find-equal-shares` — Which shape is cut into halves, or quarters?

**Standard.** 1.G.A.3: "Partition circles and rectangles into two and four equal shares, describe the shares using the words halves, fourths, and quarters." The app recognizes a cut already made; the film partitions live so the learner sees how a shape cut into quarters is made, which is the clause itself.

**Foundations first.** *Share*: a piece you would get if the shape were shared. *Equal*: the pieces are the same size. A circle (a cookie) is cut through the middle: two shares; lift one, flip it onto the other, it fits: they are equal; two equal shares are called *halves*, each is one half of the cookie. Cut again the other way: four shares; turn one onto the next, it fits: *quarters*, also called *fourths*; each is one quarter of the cookie. Then a rectangle cut into one wide piece and one thin piece: two pieces, but a friend who got the thin one would not get a fair share. Not halves. "Halves and quarters are the same size. That is what equal means."

**Read.** "Which shape is cut into quarters?" The word *quarters* goes gold. Two things must both be true: four shares, and all the same size. At a glance: any shape with the wrong number of shares is out before anything else is looked at.

**The example.** "Which shape is cut into quarters? Shape A | Shape B | Shape C." Drawn to scale with their letters under them: A, a circle cut into four equal shares; B, a rectangle cut by three lines into four pieces, one wide and one thin; C, a square cut down the middle into two. (1) Count the shares of each, lighting a piece per word: A four, B four, C two. (2) C has two shares: that would be halves. C dims. (3) B has four, but look: the wide piece and the thin piece. The thin piece lifts and slides onto the wide one and does not cover it. Not the same size, so not quarters; B dims. (4) A: one share turns onto the next, fits; onto the next, fits. Four equal shares: quarters. Shape A. (If the generator's decoys never include unequal cuts, the lesson keeps its own; without one, *equal* is untested.)

**Fast.** Count the shares. Then ask: are they all the same size? Both must be yes.
**Sense.** A shape cut into quarters could be shared fairly by four friends.
**Check.** Lift one share and lay it on another: it fits.
**Rule out.** Wrong count dims first, in one beat; then any shape whose pieces are not the same size.
**Line.** "Equal shares are the same size. Fair shares."
**Trap.** Counting pieces only: four pieces called quarters when one is wide and one thin. The decoy is on screen, counted to four, and refused by the lay-on.
**Kit.** `shares` with explicit unequal cut positions for a decoy, letter labels under each shape, a count that ticks per lit piece, `match` (lift, turn or slide one share onto another, showing fit or no fit), `dim` for the choices.
**Writer.** Strongest: a six-year-old's voice and a lesson that is one word carried by a cut and a flip.

#### `name-equal-share` — How much of the shape is shaded?

**Standard.** 1.G.A.3: "describe the shares using the words halves, fourths, and quarters, and use the phrases half of, fourth of, and quarter of."

**Foundations first.** A square cut into two equal shares: *halves*; shade one: "one half of the square". Cut into four equal shares: *quarters*. Shade one: "one quarter of the square". Shade a second: "two quarters". A third: "three quarters". Two counts, in order: first count all the shares, which gives the name; then count the colored ones, which gives the number. Say the number, then the name.

**Read.** "The square is cut into equal parts. How much of the square is shaded?" Gold on *shaded*. Two questions hide in one: how many equal parts (the name), how many are colored (the number). At a glance: a shape in two parts can only be "one half" (or the whole); a shape in four can be one, two or three quarters.

**The example.** A square cut into four equal parts, three shaded. Choices: one quarter | three quarters | one half. (1) Count every part, lighting each: one, two, three, four. Four equal parts: quarters. The word *quarters* is written beside the square before any colored part is counted. (2) Count the colored parts: one, two, three. (3) Three quarters of the square is shaded. (4) The white part is one quarter; three quarters and one quarter make the whole square.
A quick second case, three or four beats, because the other name is halves: a circle cut into two equal parts, one shaded. Two parts: halves. One colored. One half of the circle.

**Fast.** Count all the parts: that is the name. Count the colored parts: that is the number. Say number, then name.
**Sense.** More than half the square is colored, and three quarters is more than half.
**Check.** Colored plus white is all four: three quarters and one quarter make the whole.
**Rule out.** Three parts are colored, so anything that starts with "one" is out: "one quarter" is one part, "one half" would be two of the four. Both dim in one beat.
**Line.** "Count all the parts for the name. Count the colored parts for the number."
**Trap.** Naming from the colored count: three colored, so "thirds". The name is written from the count of all parts before a single colored part is counted, and it stays on screen.
**Kit.** `shares` with `shaded`, highlight per share on counting words, a name card beside the shape, a colored-count label.
**Writer.** Strongest (grade 1 voice; do it with `name-share`).

#### `larger-share` — One half or one quarter: which is larger?

**Standard.** 1.G.A.3: "Understand for these examples that decomposing into more equal shares creates smaller shares."

**Foundations first.** Two circles the same size (one slides over the other and matches, slides back: "the same size, so we can compare their pieces"). Cut A into two equal shares: halves; one shaded. Cut B the same way: halves, the same as A. Now cut B again: four quarters. Watch what happened to B's shaded half: it became two quarters, and each is smaller than the half was. "More shares, smaller shares." Lift B's two quarters and lay them on A's half: they cover it exactly. Two quarters make one half.

**Read.** "Shapes A and B are the same size. Which shaded piece is smaller: one half or one quarter?" The asked word, *smaller* or *larger*, goes gold and is read twice, because the question comes both ways. At a glance: the shape cut into more pieces has the smaller pieces; "They are the same size" is only right if both are cut the same way, and they are not.

**The example.** The *smaller* wording, because a child who knows "a half is bigger" answers "one half" to it by habit. A: a circle in two equal shares, one shaded; B: a same-size circle in four, one shaded. (1) Same size: B slides over A and matches. (2) A: two shares, one half of the circle. B: four shares, one quarter of the circle. (3) B's shaded quarter lifts and lies on A's shaded half: it covers only part; a second quarter would be needed. So the quarter is smaller. (4) Reread the question: *smaller*. One quarter.

**Fast.** More shares, smaller shares. Then reread which is asked, smaller or larger.
**Sense.** It takes two quarters to make a half, so a quarter must be smaller.
**Check.** Two quarters laid on the half cover it exactly.
**Rule out.** "They are the same size" dims: one circle is cut into two, the other into four, so the pieces cannot match.
**Line.** "More shares, smaller shares."
**Trap.** Two, both real: "four is more than two, so a quarter is bigger" (the lay-on ends it), and answering the big one when the small one was asked (the gold word is reread before the answer).
**Kit.** `shares` pair, whole-shape overlay to show same size, `match` of one share onto another with partial cover, two shares lifted together.
**Writer.** Strongest.

### Grade 2: thirds-and-partitions (2.G.A.3)

#### `eighths-and-parts-beyond-one` — Pieces that make a whole, and shading past one whole

**Misfiled first.** 2.G.A.3 stops at "two, three, or four equal shares" and "describe the whole as two halves, three thirds, four fourths." Eighths, "one whole and one fourth", and "how many thirds are shaded in all" across bars are 3.NF.A.1 and 3.NF.A.3c. Keep "how many halves, thirds, fourths make one whole" in grade 2; move the eighths and the beyond-one wordings to grade 3 `unit-fractions`. The type as it exists is mixed, two kinds, so the lesson has two examples.

**Standard.** 2.G.A.3: "describe the whole as two halves, three thirds, four fourths"; the second kind serves 3.NF.A.1 ("a parts of size 1/b") for a > b.

**Foundations first.** A bar is a shape too: a rectangle laid flat. A bracket over it says *1 whole*. Cut it into four equal parts on the counting words: fourths. Shade them one at a time: one fourth, two, three, four fourths: the whole bar is colored; a heavy outline wraps it and the label says *1 whole*. "The whole is all of its pieces. Four fourths make one whole." The same with two (halves) and three (thirds) in one beat each.

**Read.** Kind A: "How many eighths make one whole?" asks for the count of equal parts in one whole bar; the name already says it (eighths: eight). Kind B: "Each bar is one whole, cut into fourths. How much is shaded?" and "How many thirds are shaded in all?": every full bar is one whole; the loose pieces in the next bar are counted after it, or counted on without restarting. At a glance: more than one bar has color, so the answer is more than one whole and fewer than two.

**Example A.** A bar cut into eight equal parts. "How many eighths make one whole? 2 | 8 | 7 | 1." Count the pieces, each lit, not the lines: one ... eight. Shade them as they are counted; at eight the bar is full and the heavy outline wraps it. Eight eighths make one whole. Rule out: 1 is one piece; 2 would be halves (dim together); 7 leaves one piece white, and the bar shows the gap. 8.

**Example B.** Two bars, each one whole cut into fourths; the first fully shaded, the second with one piece shaded. "How much is shaded? 5 wholes | 1 whole and 3 fourths | 1 whole and 1 fourth | 2 wholes and 1 fourth." (1) First bar: four fourths, all colored: one whole; the heavy outline. (2) Second bar: one fourth. (3) One whole and one fourth. (4) Counted on without restarting: four, five: five fourths in all, the other wording. Rule out: only two bars exist, so "5 wholes" is impossible; the second bar is not full, so not "2 wholes"; only one of its pieces is colored, not three. All dim with one reason: look at the second bar.

**Fast.** A: the name says the number. B: full bars are wholes; then count the loose pieces.
**Sense.** B: more than one bar is colored, less than two.
**Check.** A: count the pieces again from the other end. B: four fourths made the whole, one more is five fourths.
**Line.** "A whole is all of its pieces. Four fourths make one whole."
**Trap.** Three, all real: counting the lines (7 for eighths; the choice is waiting), counting every colored piece and calling them wholes ("5 wholes"), and restarting the count at the second bar. Pieces light one by one; the heavy outline is what a whole looks like; the count carries across the gap on screen.
**Kit.** `bar` with `wholes: 2`, counted shading per piece, highlight a piece per word, the whole outline on a full bar (the `trade ... into whole` visual), `dim`.
**Writer.** Strongest: a mixed type in a grade 2 voice.

#### `find-equal-thirds` — Which shape is cut into halves, thirds or quarters?

**Standard.** 2.G.A.3: "Partition circles and rectangles into two, three, or four equal shares, describe the shares using the words halves, thirds, half of, a third of."

**Foundations first.** The grade 1 opening, plus thirds: a rectangle cut into three equal shares, *thirds*, each one third of the rectangle; a circle cut into thirds from the center. Count the shares of each, lighting pieces: three and three, though the rectangle has two lines and the circle has three. "Count the pieces, not the lines." Then the decoy: a rectangle cut by two lines into one wide piece and two narrow ones; three pieces, not thirds.

**Read.** "Which shape is cut into thirds?" Gold on *thirds*: three shares, all the same size. At a glance: count first; a shape with two or four shares is out.

**The example.** A: a rectangle in three equal shares; B: a circle in four equal shares; C: a rectangle cut into three unequal pieces. (1) Count each: A three, B four, C three. B has four: quarters; it dims. (2) C: the narrow piece lifts onto the wide one and does not cover it. Not thirds; C dims. (3) A: one share slides onto its neighbor, fits; onto the next, fits. Thirds. Shape A.

**Fast.** Count the shares; then check they are the same size.
**Check.** A share fits onto every other share.
**Rule out.** Wrong count dims first; then unequal pieces.
**Line.** "Count the pieces, not the lines. Then check they are the same size."
**Trap.** Counting lines: a circle in thirds has three cuts, a rectangle in thirds two; children answer from the lines. Pieces are lit, never lines.
**Kit.** `shares` with `cuts: 3` on circle and rectangle, unequal decoy, `match`, `dim`.
**Writer.** Strongest, written with `find-equal-shares`.

#### `name-share` — How much is shaded: halves, thirds or quarters?

**Standard.** 2.G.A.3: "describe the shares using the words halves, thirds, half of, a third of, etc., and describe the whole as two halves, three thirds, four fourths."

**Foundations first.** As `name-equal-share`, with thirds: a circle in three equal shares, *thirds*; shade one, "one third of the circle"; a second, "two thirds"; a third, "three thirds: the whole circle". Two counts, in order: all the parts for the name, the colored parts for the number.

**Read.** "The circle is cut into equal parts. How much of the circle is shaded?" Gold on *shaded*. At a glance: the number of parts fixes the name, so any choice with the wrong name is out before the colored parts are counted.

**The example.** A circle in three equal parts, two shaded. Choices: one third | two thirds | two quarters | two halves. (1) Count all the parts, lit one by one: three. Thirds; the word is written beside the circle. (2) Count the colored parts: one, two. (3) Two thirds of the circle is shaded. (4) The white part is one third; two thirds and one third make the whole.

**Fast.** All parts give the name; colored parts give the number; say number, then name.
**Sense.** More than half the circle is colored, and two of three is more than half.
**Check.** Colored and white together: three thirds, the whole.
**Rule out.** There are three parts, so the name is thirds: "two quarters" and "two halves" dim together. Two colored: not "one third".
**Line.** "Count all the parts for the name. Count the colored parts for the number."
**Trap.** Naming from the colored count: two colored, so "halves". The name is fixed and written before the colored parts are touched.
**Kit.** As `name-equal-share`, cuts of three.
**Writer.** Strongest, written with `name-equal-share`.

#### `same-share-different-shape` — Same whole, cut two ways: are the shaded shares the same?

**Standard.** 2.G.A.3: "Recognize that equal shares of identical wholes need not have the same shape." The second kind (different names) reuses 1.G.A.3's "more equal shares creates smaller shares."

**Foundations first.** Two rectangles the same size (B slides over A, matches, slides back). Cut A down the middle: two shares; flip one onto the other, fits: halves. Cut B from corner to corner: two triangles; turn one onto the other, fits: halves. A tall rectangle and a triangle, different shapes, each one half of the same-size whole. Are they the same amount? Prove it: B's shaded triangle is cut down its middle, and the small triangle makes a half turn about the middle of the cut; it lands beside the big piece and the two together are exactly A's half. "Equal means the same amount of the whole, not the same shape."

**Read.** "Rectangles A and B are the same size. Which is true about their shaded parts?" Two counts: how many equal parts in A, how many in B. Same count: same amount, whatever the shapes. Different count: the whole cut into fewer parts has the bigger share. At a glance: count parts in each before looking at shapes.

**Example 1 (same amount).** A: a rectangle in two equal parts by a vertical line, one shaded; B: the same-size rectangle in two by a diagonal, one shaded. Choices: A's shaded part is larger | B's shaded part is larger | They are the same amount. (1) Same size: overlay. (2) A: two parts, flipped onto each other, fit: halves; one half of A. (3) B: two parts, turned onto each other, fit: halves; one half of B. (4) The cut-and-turn: B's triangle becomes A's rectangle half. Same amount.

**Example 2 (different names), quick.** Squares A and B the same size; A in four equal parts, one shaded; B in two, one shaded. Count: quarters, halves. Two of A's quarters lift onto B's half and cover it exactly, so one quarter is smaller. B's shaded part is larger. Four or five beats.

**Fast.** Count the parts in each. Same count: same amount. Different count: fewer parts, bigger part.
**Sense.** Each is half of the same-size whole; two of either fill the rectangle.
**Check.** Match within each shape; the cut-and-turn.
**Rule out.** Kind 1: both "larger" choices dim together: both are halves of the same whole. Kind 2: "same amount" dims: cut into different numbers of parts.
**Line.** "Equal means the same amount, not the same shape."
**Trap.** Judging by shape: the triangle "is bigger because it is longer", the tall thin rectangle "is smaller". The cut-and-turn turns one into the other. In kind 2, "A has more pieces so A's is bigger": two quarters laid on the half.
**Kit.** `shares` on identical rectangles with vertical and diagonal cuts; whole-shape overlay; `match`; a share drawn as two polys with a hidden cut line so `cut` reveals it and `turn` (half turn about the cut's midpoint) and `slide` move the small piece. The kit's `poly`, `turn` and `slide` suffice once a share can be split.
**Writer.** Strongest: the lesson is a proof by motion.

### Grade 3: unit-fractions (3.NF.A.1)

#### `unit-piece-size` — One piece of a whole cut into b equal pieces

**Standard.** 3.NF.A.1: "Understand a fraction 1/b as the quantity formed by 1 part when a whole is partitioned into b equal parts."

**Foundations first.** The bar is born, the family's twenty-second opening, with the handoff said aloud: "A fraction strip is a shape laid flat. Its length is the whole." Bracket: *1 whole*. Cut into six equal pieces on the counting words. "Each piece is one sixth." Then the first written fraction in the family, defined: a 1, a line, a 6. The bottom number says how many pieces make the whole; the top says how many of them we have. One sixth, 1/6, is written inside the first piece, outlined gold. Six of these pieces make the whole: six sixths.

**Read.** "One whole strip is divided into 6 equal pieces. What fraction of the whole is one piece?" Gold on *one piece* and *fraction*. At a glance: the answer is 1 over the piece count.

**The example.** The strip in six. (1) The whole. (2) Count the pieces, lit one by one: six equal pieces. (3) One piece shades, teal; its label 1/6. "One piece, of the six that make the whole." (4) The answer written under the bar: 1/6. (5) The check as a motion: shade the rest, counting: two sixths ... six sixths; the strip is full; six sixths is one whole.

**Fast.** Count the pieces. Write 1 over that count.
**Sense.** Six pieces make the whole, so each is small: smaller than a half or a third.
**Check.** Six of them fill the strip exactly.
**Line.** "The bottom names the piece: how many make the whole."
**Trap.** Two, both real: answering 6 (the count of pieces, not the fraction one piece is), and writing the bottom from the lines (5). The fraction is written while the voice points: 1 piece, of 6 pieces; pieces are lit, not lines.
**Kit.** `bar` with `labels: "first"`, counted shading, `fraction: true`.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, from this concept, once `fraction-model` has set the opening it copies: one method, one component.

#### `fraction-unit-count` — Shaded pieces across strips

**Badly conceived first.** "How many pieces are shaded?" takes a count (1) as its answer; nothing fractional is tested, and the seed with one shaded piece is degenerate. The standard's clause is "a fraction a/b as the quantity formed by a parts of size 1/b": the app should ask for the fraction shaded (7/6, or 1 whole and 1 sixth) or accept it. The lesson teaches the fraction; the count the app asks for is its top number.

**Standard.** 3.NF.A.1, the second clause.

**Foundations first.** The shared opening, in sixths; then a second strip of the same width under the first, its own bracket *1 whole*. "Each strip is one whole. The whole is one strip, not both."

**Read.** "Each whole is cut into 6 equal pieces. How many pieces are shaded?" Gold on *each whole* and *in all* where it appears. At a glance: if more than one strip has color, the count is more than six.

**The example.** Two strips in sixths; the first fully shaded, the second with one shaded. (1) Count the colored pieces, lighting each, and do not restart at the second strip: one ... six, seven. (2) Seven pieces, each one sixth: seven sixths; six of them made the first whole, so one whole and one sixth. (3) The answer the app wants: 7.

**Fast.** Count every colored piece across the strips, continuing the count. That number over the piece's bottom is the fraction.
**Sense.** More than six, so more than one strip: matches the picture.
**Check.** A full strip is six; six and one more is seven.
**Line.** "The top counts the pieces. Keep counting across the strips."
**Trap.** Restarting the count on the new strip (1), and taking both strips as the whole (7/12). The count carries across the gap on screen; each strip wears its own *1 whole*.
**Kit.** `bar` with `wholes: 2`, counted shading across the chain, count and fraction labels.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, from this concept.

#### `congruent-fourths-pair` — Which two figures each show one fourth?

**Standard.** 3.G.A.2: "Partition shapes into parts with equal areas. Express the area of each part as a unit fraction of the whole." 3.NF.A.1 for 1/4; 2.G.A.3's "need not have the same shape" is the idea carried.

**Foundations first.** One fourth means one part when the whole is cut into four equal parts, equal in how much of the whole each covers, not in shape. A rectangle cut into four equal strips, one shaded: one fourth. The same-size rectangle cut into a two-by-two grid, one cell shaded: one fourth. The strip and the cell do not look alike. Prove they are the same amount: the strip is cut across its middle and its two pieces slide side by side: they make the cell exactly.

**Read.** "The three rectangles are the same size and the same shape. Which two figures each show one fourth shaded? A and B | A and C | B and C | None of the pairs." For each figure: four parts? all the same size? one shaded? At a glance: a figure with unequal parts, or the wrong count, fails, and every pair that names it is out.

**The example.** A: four equal vertical strips, one shaded. B: a two-by-two grid, one cell shaded. C: four strips, one wide and three narrow, the wide one shaded. (1) Count the parts of each, lit one by one: four, four, four. (2) C: the wide shaded part is lifted onto a narrow one and overhangs: not equal parts, so the shaded part is not one fourth; "A and C" and "B and C" dim together. (3) A: a strip flips onto the next, fits: four equal parts, one shaded: one fourth. (4) B: a cell turns onto the next, fits: one fourth. (5) The cut-and-slide: A's strip becomes B's cell. Different shapes, the same amount. "None" dims. A and B.

**Fast.** For each figure: four parts, all the same size, one shaded. Pick the two that pass.
**Sense.** Four copies of each shaded part fill its rectangle.
**Check.** The cut-and-slide; or lay four copies.
**Rule out.** The figure with unequal parts kills both pairs that contain it, in one beat; "None" only if a second figure also fails.
**Line.** "One fourth is one of four equal parts: equal in size, not in shape."
**Trap.** Choosing "None" because the strip and the cell look different, and accepting C because it has four parts. The cut-and-slide answers the first; the overhang answers the second.
**Kit.** `shares` on rectangles with strips, grid and unequal strips; `match`; a strip drawn as two polys with a hidden cut so `cut` and `slide` can rearrange it; `dim`.
**Writer.** Strongest: the proof by motion is the lesson.
