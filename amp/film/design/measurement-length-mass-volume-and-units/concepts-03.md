# Measurement, part 3: concepts for the grade-4 conversion types and the grade-5 reverse

Ten problem types. Four of them (`measuring-tool-choice`, `unit-relative-size`, `reasonable-measure-choice`, `object-for-measure`) are the "benchmarks and tools" skill the family design asked to split out of `unit-conversion`; they share one picture, the benchmark card, and are written here so it is built once. Three are the double ruler (`unit-rename-larger`, `rename-time-units`, `customary-equivalent-select`), one is the chart with unit headers (`metric-length-reverse`), one is the ruler with the cut (`measurement-line-fraction-story`), and one is a mixed story type (`conversion-chain-story`).

---

### `unit-rename-larger` — Rename in a larger unit, no remainder.

Off grade: filed grade 4, but 4.MD.A.1 is "express measurements in a larger unit in terms of a smaller unit" only. This is **5.MD.A.1**: "Convert among different-sized standard measurement units within a given measurement system." Keep it where it is as a stretch; the lesson's voice stays grade 4. Foundations 10 and 12.

**Foundations first.** Define the fact as a group size: "1 yard = 3 feet" means a yard is a group of three feet. Build the double ruler live: a foot ruler 0 to 39 (violet, the smaller unit), then a yard scale above it (blue), one tick every three feet, 1 yd over 3 ft, 2 yd over 6 ft. Say the line before any number: converting changes the count, never the length; the bigger unit gives the smaller count, so to count yards we divide the feet into groups of three.

**Read.** "How many yards" (gold). The answer is a count of yards, and it must be less than 39 because a yard is bigger than a foot. At a glance: the fact is 3, so the answer is 39 ÷ 3.

**The example.** Student B's kite string, 39 feet, how many yards. (1) The fact written as two names: 1 yd = 3 ft; "yard is bigger, so fewer: divide" said aloud. (2) The double ruler; the gold line drops at 39 ft and reads 13 on the yard scale. (3) The board: 39 ÷ 3 = 13, "thirteen groups of three feet." (4) The table row (13, 39) writes itself from the read-across. (5) 13 yards. Quick second case, a different method: 3,000 mL in liters. Milli is the thousandths column, so a liter is 1,000 milliliters; ÷ 1,000 is three shifts right in the chart: 3. Same direction, same line, the shift instead of the division.

**Fast.** Read the fact, say the direction, divide by the group size.
**Sense.** Fewer yards than feet; 39 feet is about 13 big steps.
**Check.** 13 × 3 = 39.
**Line.** "Bigger unit, smaller number: divide."
**Trap.** Multiplying because "convert means multiply" (117), and reaching for 12 because feet are involved. The direction is said before any arithmetic, and the fact is written as two names first.
**Kit.** `ruler` with `scales` and `across`; `table` arrow. Nothing beyond the family's tools.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, named actions, and the unit-aware check catches a wrong direction.

### `measurement-line-fraction-story` — Distance between two marks as a fraction of a mile.

Standard: **4.MD.A.2**, "Represent measurement quantities using diagrams such as number line diagrams that feature a measurement scale," on **3.NF.A.2b**, "marking off a lengths 1/b from 0 ... the resulting interval has size a/b." Filed with `fractional-ruler-length` as one skill in two grades; as the design said, move both or neither. The checker should accept 1½ for 3/2 and 1 9/10 for 19/10.

**Foundations first.** The trail map is a ruler, and a ruler is a number line: 0 at the start, the labeled miles, equal steps between them. One mile is the whole. Cut it live: one mile cut into ten equal steps, each step one tenth of a mile, 1/10, labeled on the word. The distance between two marks is a count of steps, never of ticks: light the spaces, not the lines (from `shifted-ruler-length`: length is end minus start).

**Read.** "How far is it from A to B" asks for the number of steps between the marks, as a fraction of one mile. At a glance: which mile marks is each letter between, and how many steps make one mile?

**The example.** The line runs 0 to 2 miles, 10 steps to a mile; A at the fourth step, B nine steps past the 1-mile mark. (1) Steps in one mile: count them, 10. That is the denominator; said why: each step is a tenth of a mile. (2) Light the spaces from A to B, counting: one, two ... fifteen; the count crosses the 1-mile mark without stopping. (3) Fifteen tenths of a mile: 15/10. (4) Fifteen tenths is one mile and five tenths; five and ten share 5: 3/2. (5) 3/2 miles, said as one and a half miles. A quick beat for A at 0: then the distance is B's own reading, the same count with no subtraction.

**Fast.** Steps in one mile under; steps from A to B on top; simplify.
**Sense.** B is past 1 mile and A is before it, so the distance is more than one mile and less than two; 3/2 is.
**Check.** A's reading plus the distance lands on B: 4/10 + 15/10 = 19/10, where B sits.
**Line.** "The whole is one mile. Count the steps between the marks."
**Trap.** Taking the denominator from the whole line's steps (15/20) because the line goes to 2 miles; counting ticks and getting 16; answering B's reading (19/10) and ignoring A. The mile is cut on screen as the whole, the spaces light with the count, and the count starts at A.
**Kit.** `ruler` with `cut` labels and `light` from A to B across a labeled mile mark; `numberline` zoom if the app's line is dense.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: the ruler with the cut is one picture; the trap is drawn, not reasoned about.

### `measuring-tool-choice` — Which tool measures what the question asks.

No standard; vocabulary. Keep the film short (about a minute). The app's convention must be taught and the template must keep it: "mass" or "compare with known masses" is the **balance**; "weight" or "pounds" is the **scale**.

**Foundations first.** The attribute: something a thing can have more or less of. Each attribute has one tool, shown as icons in a row with the question each answers: ruler, how long; thermometer, how hot; measuring cup, how much liquid; scale, how heavy, read as a number on a dial; balance, how heavy by comparing with known masses, level when they match. The scale and the balance both answer "how heavy"; the balance *compares*, the scale *reads*.

**Read.** Find the attribute word in the question (gold): "temperature", "mass", "weight", "milk", "long". The tool is the one for that attribute. At a glance: any tool for another attribute is gone.

**The example.** "Which tool should you use to compare the mass of a rock with known masses?" Choices: measuring cup, thermometer, scale, balance. (1) "Mass" and "known masses" go gold: how heavy, by comparing. (2) Measuring cup (liquid) and thermometer (hot) dim together: wrong question. (3) Scale and balance both weigh; "compare with known masses" is what a balance does: the rock on one pan, known masses on the other until it is level. (4) Balance. Quick second case: "Student D is measuring the weight of the apples in pounds": weight, a number in pounds, read off a dial: scale.

**Fast.** Attribute word, then its tool.
**Sense.** The tool fits the thing: you do not pour a rock.
**Check.** Say the sentence: "a balance tells how heavy by comparing." True.
**Rule out.** Tools for other attributes dim together with one reason; between scale and balance, the words "compare" or "known masses" decide.
**Line.** "Ask what the question measures; the tool follows."
**Trap.** Scale for a mass question. The lesson shows the two side by side once and says which word picks which.
**Kit.** `icon` set (ruler, thermometer, measuring cup, scale, balance).
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model.

### `unit-relative-size` — Which unit is bigger; which number is bigger.

Standard: **4.MD.A.1**, "Know relative sizes of measurement units within one system of units including km, m, cm; kg, g; lb, oz.; l, ml." The "which statement is true" variants compare across systems (kilogram against pound, quart against liter); off the standard's "within one system." Keep as benchmark knowledge, one quick beat. Three kinds under one name: same amount in two units; order units; a true statement.

**Foundations first.** One bag of rice; two names for its mass. The double ruler: grams on top (blue, the larger unit), milligrams below (violet), 1,000 violet ticks under every blue one. The read-across at 2 g reads 2,000 mg. The amount did not change; the count did. Then the benchmark card for mass: a paper clip is a gram, a slice of bread an ounce, a loaf a pound, a liter bottle a kilogram, a small car a ton.

**Read.** Kind A: "which measurement has the greater number" is not asking which unit is bigger; it asks about the count. At a glance: the smaller unit has the bigger number, with no arithmetic. Kind C: "lightest to heaviest": find the lightest first. Kind B: which fact is true: picture each pair.

**The example.** Kind A: a bag of rice in grams and milligrams; choices: milligrams | grams | grams, because a gram is the larger unit | the two are the same. (1) The double ruler built; the gold line reads 2 g and 2,000 mg at once. (2) "The two numbers are the same" dims: that would mean the units were the same size. (3) The third choice gets the fact right (a gram *is* larger) and the conclusion backwards; named as the trap: the larger unit gives the smaller number. It dims with the second. (4) Milligrams. Second example, a different act, kind C: ounce, pound, ton, lightest to heaviest. Benchmarks beside each: a slice of bread, a loaf, a small car. The lightest first: ounce; then pound; then ton. The lists not starting with ounce dim together. Quick beat, kind B: a kilogram is about two pounds (a liter bottle against a loaf); a liter is a little more than a quart; a mile is longer than a kilometer.

**Fast.** Smaller unit, bigger number, always; for ordering, the lightest first.
**Sense.** 2 against 2,000 on the ruler.
**Check.** Read the chosen statement aloud: "the count in milligrams is greater." True on the ruler.
**Rule out.** "The same" dims first; a reason-choice whose conclusion contradicts its own fact dims next.
**Line.** "Smaller unit, bigger number."
**Trap.** "Bigger unit, bigger number" because bigger feels like more. The read-across ends it: one bag, 2 and 2,000.
**Kit.** `bar` with two scales suffices today (the percent bar's double number line); `icon` for benchmarks.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model once the benchmark card exists; the decoy is named here.

### `conversion-chain-story` — A chain of units, and whole pieces cut from a converted length.

Off grade: decimals, mixed numbers and remainder interpretation under a grade-4 skill; it is **5.MD.A.1**, "use these conversions in solving multi-step, real world problems," with **4.OA.A.3**, "problems in which remainders must be interpreted," and 5.NF.B.6 for 3½ rods. Three kinds: the ribbon and bows; the rod to inches; the cooler through stated equivalents. A mixed type: one example per kind, worked from the story.

**Foundations first.** A chain is renaming more than once; each link's fact is a group size, and the direction is said at each link. Before any dividing to count pieces, the length and the piece must have the same name. "Whole bows" means a leftover shorter than a bow is not a bow.

**Ribbon.** 5½ meters, bows of 30 cm. (1) Two names: meters and centimeters cannot be divided into each other; make them match. Centi is the hundredths column: 5½ m = 5.5 m = 550 cm, two shifts. (2) A centimeter ruler 0 to 550; brackets of 30 lay left to right, counting: 18 brackets reach 540; a 10-cm stretch stays lit, shorter than a bow. (3) 550 ÷ 30 = 18 R 10. (4) 18 whole bows; the 10 cm is ribbon, not a bow.
Sense: about three bows a meter, five and a half meters, about eighteen. Check: 18 × 30 = 540, under 550; 19 × 30 = 570 is too much.

**Rod.** A rod is 5.5 yards; inches in 6 rods. Table rods | yards | feet | inches with "× 5.5", "× 3", "× 12" on arrows above; every link is to a smaller unit, so every number grows. 6 rods → 33 yd → 99 ft → 1,188 in. Sense: 6 rods is about 30 yards, about 1,000 inches. Check: 1,188 ÷ 36 = 33 yards. (3½ rods changes the first product, not the chain.)

**Cooler.** Stated equivalents; 1.25 gallons in cups. The re-cut bar: one gallon bar plus a quarter of one; cut each gallon into 4 quarts: 5 quarts; each quart into 2 pints: 10; each pint into 2 cups: 20. The bar's length never changes. Check back up the chain: 20 ÷ 2 ÷ 2 ÷ 4 = 1.25.

**Fast.** Write each fact; say the direction at each link; for pieces, same unit first, then divide and keep the whole count.
**Lines.** "Say the direction at every link." "Same name before you divide; the leftover is not a piece."
**Trap.** 5.5 ÷ 30; 18.3 or 19 bows; stopping at yards; skipping a link in the cooler. Nothing is divided until the names match, the leftover is drawn shorter than a bow, and the table shows every link.
**Kit.** `ruler` brackets with a decimal length; `bar` piece names for the re-cut gallon; `table` arrows across three columns.
**Writer.** The strongest writer: a mixed type that teaches reading and remainder sense; a weak author writes one recipe.

### `customary-equivalent-select` — Select the measurements equal to a given one.

Standard: **4.MD.A.1**, "express measurements in a larger unit in terms of a smaller unit"; the two-part measures lean on 5.MD.A.1. Select 2 or 3 of 5.

**Foundations first.** The re-cut bar for the given amount: 4 gallons as four bars end to end; cut each into 4 quarts (16), each quart into 2 pints (32), each pint into 2 cups (64), length unchanged. "Same name before you compare": a measure in two units is two counts of two different sizes; nothing is added until both parts have one name. Also: a small part that reaches the group size is a whole big unit (3 feet is a yard).

**Read.** "Select the 2 equal to 4 gallons": each choice is a yes or a no, and exactly two are yes. The smallest unit among the choices (gold) is cups; everything goes to cups. At a glance: 4 gallons is 16 quarts, so "64 quarts" is four times too much, gone.

**The example.** (1) The given to cups on the bar: 64. (2) Each choice in turn, on its own line with units: 15 qt 1 pt = 60 + 2 = 62, no. 14 qt 8 cups = 56 + 8 = 64, yes. 64 quarts: the right number with the wrong name; dimmed already. 30 pt 4 cups = 60 + 4 = 64, yes. 2 gal 31 cups = 32 + 31 = 63, no. (3) b and d. Quick second case, two ideas the first lacks: 7 yards. "6 yards 3 feet": three feet is a full yard, so 7 yards with no arithmetic. "19 feet 25 inches": 228 + 25 = 253 inches against 252: one inch off, so a near miss must be computed to the end. "252 feet" is the inch count named as feet: gone.

**Fast.** Smallest unit; convert the given once; convert each choice; match exactly.
**Sense.** The right ones land on 64 exactly; a near miss is a no.
**Check.** Exactly as many chosen as asked; convert one chosen answer back up the bar.
**Rule out.** The given's number under the wrong unit name; a two-part measure obviously over or under (2 gallons and 31 cups is under 4 gallons on sight if 31 cups is under 2 gallons: 32).
**Line.** "Same name, then compare."
**Trap.** Adding the two parts' numbers (14 + 8 = 22), the family's concatenation; stopping at "close." The bar shows quarts and cups as different sizes, and every line carries its unit.
**Kit.** `bar` with piece names and re-cut; `dim`; the unit-aware board check.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, heavy arithmetic the check verifies.

### `reasonable-measure-choice` — The unit or the measure that fits an object.

Standard: **2.MD.A.3** "Estimate lengths using units of inches, feet, centimeters, and meters"; **3.MD.A.2** "estimate liquid volumes and masses of objects"; **4.MD.A.1** relative sizes. Two shapes: the object and a number, choose the unit; the object, choose among four measures. Both are the benchmark card.

**Foundations first.** The benchmark card for the attribute, drawn once: a gram is a paper clip, an ounce a slice of bread, a pound a loaf, a kilogram a liter bottle, a ton a small car. Estimating is repeating the benchmark along the object. "Reasonable" means the number with that unit could really be the object.

**Read.** The object and the number are given; the unit is the question (gold). At a glance: the two units far too big or far too small for the thing are gone before any picture.

**The example.** "The mass of a student is about 75. Which unit?" Choices: ounces, tons, grams, pounds. (1) Try each with the number, pictured: 75 paper clips is a handful (grams, gone); 75 small cars is a parking lot (tons, gone); these two dim together. (2) 75 slices of bread is about four loaves, a cat, not a student (ounces, gone). (3) 75 loaves of bread: that is a ten-year-old. (4) Pounds. Quick second case, the other shape: the length of a pencil: 7 inches, 7 feet, 7 miles, 7 kilometers. Miles and kilometers are walks between towns; 7 feet is taller than a door; 7 thumb joints along the pencil: 7 inches.

**Fast.** Try the number with each unit and picture it; keep the one that could be the thing.
**Sense.** The picture is the sense.
**Check.** Say it as a sentence: "a student has a mass of about 75 pounds." Believable.
**Rule out.** The units far too big or far too small dim together, with the picture as the reason.
**Line.** "Try the number with the unit, and picture it."
**Trap.** Choosing a unit that fits the attribute but not the size (grams: it is mass, but 75 of them is a handful). The lesson pictures the amount, never just the attribute.
**Kit.** `icon` set and the benchmark card.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model once the card exists.

### `object-for-measure` — The object closest to a stated measure.

Standard: **3.MD.A.2**, "estimate liquid volumes and masses of objects," and 4.MD.A.1 relative sizes. The seed "closest to 1 teaspoon" with "a teaspoon" as a choice is degenerate for the example (the unit is the object); pick 150 grams.

**Foundations first.** The benchmark card again, plus the metric facts said once: 1 kilogram is 1,000 grams, 1 liter is 1,000 milliliters. "Closest" means the other objects are many times too big or too small, so the first move is to put the objects in order.

**Read.** "Which object has a mass closest to 150 grams": the amount is fixed; the objects are compared. At a glance: 150 grams is less than a kilogram, so anything heavier than a liter bottle is gone.

**The example.** 150 grams: an apple, a bowling ball, a ten-year-old child, a bag of sugar. (1) A gram is a paper clip, so 150 grams is 150 paper clips, about a small handful. (2) Order the objects by benchmark: an apple; a bag of sugar is a kilogram, 1,000 grams; a bowling ball is several kilograms; a child is thirty. (3) 150 is below 1,000, so the apple is the only one on that side. (4) An apple. Quick second case, capacity: 150 liters. A liter is a big water bottle; a juice box is less than one; a bucket about ten; a bathtub about 150 bottles: a bathtub.

**Fast.** Order the objects, then place the amount among them.
**Sense.** 150 of a tiny unit is still small.
**Check.** The neighbors on either side are many times off: a bag of sugar is seven apples.
**Rule out.** Objects more than ten times the amount, or less than a tenth of it, dim together.
**Line.** "Order the objects, then place the amount."
**Trap.** "150 sounds big" and picking the bowling ball. The paper clip is shown 150 times, and 1 kg = 1,000 g is said before the choice.
**Kit.** `icon` set.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model.

### `rename-time-units` — Hours, minutes, days, and the mixed measure.

Standard: **4.MD.A.1**, "hr, min, sec ... express measurements in a larger unit in terms of a smaller unit"; the divide direction is **5.MD.A.1**. Months to years is not in the standard; keep as a stretch. Three cases: to the smaller unit, to the larger unit, and a two-part measure.

**Foundations first.** The clock unrolled is a ruler: the double ruler with hours on top (blue), minutes below (violet), 60 minute-spaces under every hour. The facts as group sizes, written: 1 hour = 60 minutes, 1 day = 24 hours. The direction said before the arithmetic: minutes are smaller, so more of them.

**Read.** "How many minutes long is the movie" (gold): a count of minutes, bigger than 145's neighbors on the hour scale: more than 120, less than 180. At a glance: the two parts are two names; the hours must be renamed before anything is added.

**The example.** 2 hours and 25 minutes, in minutes. (1) The double ruler; the gold line at 2 h reads 120 min. (2) 25 more minute-spaces light along the violet scale past 120: 145. (3) The board: 2 × 60 = 120; 120 + 25 = 145. (4) 145 minutes. (5) The trap named: 225 is where the gold line would read 3 hours 45 minutes; the two parts cannot be written side by side. Quick second case, the other direction: 480 minutes in hours: hours are bigger, fewer: 480 ÷ 60 = 8; the table row (8, 480). One beat: 5 days in hours, days to the smaller unit, 5 × 24 = 120, the same read-across with 24.

**Fast.** Hours times 60, then add the extra minutes; the other way, divide by 60.
**Sense.** Between 2 hours (120) and 3 hours (180).
**Check.** 145 − 120 = 25; or 145 ÷ 60 = 2 R 25.
**Line.** "Every part gets converted; then add."
**Trap.** 225, the concatenation; dividing 4 hours by 60. The hours are read across first and the minutes added on the same scale; the direction is said first.
**Kit.** `ruler` with two scales and `across`; `table` arrow. Nothing new.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model.

### `metric-length-reverse` — A smaller metric unit written in a larger one.

Badly conceived answer form: the checker wants 1469/500 km for 2,938 m. **5.MD.A.1**'s own example is "convert 5 cm to 0.05 m": the answer is 2.938, and the decimal must be the canonical form (accept the fraction too). Fix before the lesson ships. Foundation 11.

**Foundations first.** The place-value chart with unit headers: km | hm | dam | m | dm | cm | mm, the meter in the ones column, the point beside it. Said once: "kilo is the thousands column; centi is the hundredths column." A kilometer is a thousand meters exactly, so renaming meters as kilometers is dividing by 1,000, the shift from place value: digits move three places right, the point stays. The amount does not change.

**Read.** "Write 2,938 m in km" (km gold): a count of kilometers, smaller than 2,938 because a kilometer is bigger, and a bit under 3 because 3,000 m is 3 km. At a glance: kilo has three zeros, so three places.

**The example.** 2,938 m in km. (1) 2,938 slotted from the right under m, dam, hm, km: the 2 already sits under km; 2,938 meters is 2 kilometers and 938 meters. (2) Kilometers as the count: ÷ 1,000, three shifts right; the digits cross the point: 2.938. (3) The 938 meters are 938 thousandths of a kilometer, which is what the digits after the point say. (4) 2.938 km. Quick second case, a zero to fill: 47 cm in m: centi is two places, two shifts; the ones column is empty, so a zero holds it: 0.47.

**Fast.** Name the prefix's zeros; shift the digits that many places right; fill empty places with zeros.
**Sense.** Fewer kilometers than meters; just under 3.
**Check.** 2.938 × 1,000 = 2,938.
**Line.** "Kilo is the thousands column."
**Trap.** 29.38 (two shifts), and 4.7 for 47 cm (one). Every column in the chart is named, so the count of places is read, not remembered.
**Kit.** Unit headers on `digits`/`stack` with `"base"` (family spec 4.2); `shift` with fill; the checker's decimal form.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: the shift is place value's own motion, already proven.
