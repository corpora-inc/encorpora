# Measurement family, part 1: concepts for compare-attributes, measure-equal-units, ruler-measurement

Ten problem types. Each stands on the family design: the foundation numbers refer to section 1 of that design, the motions to section 2, the kit to section 4. Where a type is off grade or badly conceived, that is said first.

## Kindergarten: compare-attributes (K.MD.A.1, K.MD.A.2)

Every film in this skill opens with the same fifteen-second beat, drawn with the app's object figures: one bucket; three questions asked of it, each answered with a different tool. How long is it: a strip laid beside it. How heavy is it: it sits on a balance. How much does it hold: water pours in. "These are three things you can measure about one bucket. Color is not one of them: red is not more or less." That is K.MD.A.1's "several measurable attributes of a single object," and it is the only place it is practiced (section 6).

### `measurable-attribute-choice` — Which attribute?

A vocabulary type; keep it short.
**Standard:** K.MD.A.1, "Describe measurable attributes of objects, such as length or weight."
**Foundations first:** The opening beat, then the three words matched to their questions and tools, on one card that stays up: *length*: how long, how tall, how wide, how far across (a ruler or a strip); *weight*: how heavy, how light (a balance); *capacity*: how much it holds (pour into it). `term` card for *attribute*: "something a thing can have more or less of." Capacity is defined in passing as "how much it holds"; a child this age does not own the word, and the lesson says every time: "capacity means holds."
**Read:** Two shapes. Either a question is given and the attribute is asked, or an attribute is given and the matching question is asked. Both come down to one word in the question: heavy, long, tall, wide, hold. That word goes gold; it names the attribute. At a glance: color is never the answer here, because color is not a how-much.
**The example:** "Student A wants to know how heavy the pumpkin is. Which attribute should be measured?" Choices: length | color | capacity | weight. (1) Read it; *heavy* goes gold. (2) "Heavy is about weight." The pumpkin slides onto a balance pan (the weight tool); the balance tilts. (3) The strip and the pouring water (the other two tools) stay muted beside it: a strip tells how long, not how heavy; water tells how much it holds, and a pumpkin holds nothing. (4) Weight.
Quick second shape (the other direction, one beat each): "Which question asks about the length of the bucket?" The four questions appear; find the one with a length word: "How long is the bucket?" And one beat for the variant that trips children: "How wide is the table?" Wide is a length word too: you measure across with a ruler. Length.
**Fast:** Find the one word: heavy or light, weight; long, tall, wide, length; hold, capacity.
**Sense:** The tool you would pick says the attribute: balance for heavy, ruler for long, pour for hold.
**Check:** Say the question with the attribute's word in it: "how heavy is the pumpkin" is "what is the pumpkin's weight." If it still means the same thing, it is right.
**Rule out:** Color dims first with its one reason: "color is not more or less." Then the two tools that do not answer the question dim together.
**Line:** "Heavy is weight. Long, tall, wide is length. Holds is capacity."
**Trap:** Children read "length" as only "how long" and do not hear "tall" or "wide" as length. The card lists all three under length from the start and the table beat says it aloud.
**Kit:** The `icon` set (pumpkin, bucket, bag, table), the balance, a container for the pour. Nothing new beyond section 4.
**Writer:** A capable cheaper model, from this concept, once the icons exist: one card, one gold word, no motion that proves. Review the first sample for age-appropriate kindergarten language and pacing.

### `compare-lengths` — Which strip is longer?

**Standard:** K.MD.A.2, "Directly compare two objects with a measurable attribute in common, to see which object has 'more of'/'less of' the attribute." The describing words are longer, shorter, same length.
**Foundations first:** Two strips, teal and rust, and a vertical **start line**. "Longer means it reaches farther, but only if both start at the same place." Define by doing: two strips both on the start line, one sticks out past the other: that one is longer. Then the fair test in one line: "Line up the starts, then look at the ends."
**Read:** "Which strip is shorter?" or "longer?" The asked word goes gold; the answer is a strip's letter or "the same." At a glance: do both strips touch the start line? If not, nothing can be read yet.
**The example:** "Which strip is shorter?" Choices: Strip A | Strip B | They are the same length. Picture to scale: A starts at the start line and ends at 7; B starts at 3 and ends at 8. (1) Read. *Shorter* goes gold. (2) Look at the right ends first, on purpose: B's end sticks out past A's end. "It looks like B is longer. But B did not start at the line." (3) **Align**: B slides left until it touches the start line, its length unchanged (a thin bracket over B rides with it so the eye sees it is the same strip). B now ends at 5; A ends at 7. (4) A sticks out past B. A is longer, so B is shorter. (5) Strip B.
Quick second case (the third choice, one beat): A from the line to 6, B from 2 to 8. B slides back: both end at 6. "The ends match. They are the same length."
**Fast:** Starts on the line, then look at the ends.
**Sense:** After sliding, the strip that sticks out is the longer one, and the question asked for the other one.
**Check:** Line up the other ends instead: slide both so their right ends match; the one that sticks out on the left is longer. Same answer.
**Rule out:** "Same length" dims once the aligned ends do not match. (Light touch at this grade; one beat.)
**Line:** "Line up the starts."
**Trap:** Judging by where the ends are when the starts differ: the child says B is longer because B's end is farther right. The example is built so that answer is wrong, and the slide is what overturns it.
**Kit:** Strips as `poly`, the start line as a `seg`, `slide` for the align; a bracket that rides the strip (`dim` with `group`). Exists.
**Writer:** Strongest. The slide is the whole lesson and the K voice must hold with no strike-outs and no "wrong"; the wrong first look is staged, then overturned.

### `compare-weights` — Which is heavier?

**Standard:** K.MD.A.2 (same clause). Describing words: heavier, lighter, weigh the same.
**Foundations first:** The balance, empty and level. "When nothing is on it, the beam is flat. Put something heavy on one side and that side goes down, like a seesaw." One object lands; its pan drops. "The low side is the heavy side. If the beam stays flat, the two sides weigh the same." Then the line that matters most at this age, shown, not said: weight is not size. A big ball lands on one pan and a small bag on the other; the bag's pan goes down.
**Read:** "Which is heavier?" or "Which is lighter?" The asked word goes gold: *heavier* asks for the low pan, *lighter* asks for the high pan. At a glance: is the beam tilted or flat? Flat means "the same," and the question is already answered.
**The example:** "Which is heavier?" Choices: A (the bag) | B (the ball) | They weigh the same. Picture: the bag on the left pan, a ball visibly bigger than the bag on the right; the beam **tilts** so the bag's pan is lower. (1) Read; *heavier* goes gold. (2) "The ball is bigger. Bigger is not heavier. The balance tells us." (3) The low pan: the bag's. "The balance pushes the heavy side down." (4) A, the bag.
Quick second case (the other word, one beat): "Which is lighter? A (the bag) | B (the box)": the bag's pan is low, so the box is up high. Lighter is the high pan: B, the box. And one beat on a level beam: "flat means the same."
**Fast:** Heavier: the low pan. Lighter: the high pan. Flat: the same.
**Sense:** A heavy thing pulls its side down; say it in a sentence with the object's name.
**Check:** Say the answer as the balance shows it: "The bag pulls its side down, so the bag is heavier." If the sentence matches the picture, it is right.
**Rule out:** If the beam tilts, "the same" dims. The word in the question dims the pan it did not ask for.
**Line:** "The low side is the heavy side."
**Trap:** Judging weight by size: the big ball must be heavier. The example puts the bigger object on the high pan so the child sees size and weight come apart. (A second real one: "lighter" asks for the *other* pan and children answer the heavy one from habit; the gold word and the high-pan beat are for that.)
**Kit:** The app's balance with `tilt` and `level` (section 4.4); the `icon` set with a bag, ball, box drawn so the ball is visibly larger.
**Writer:** Strongest. The size-against-weight staging is the lesson; a weak writer says "size doesn't matter" over a picture that does not show it.

### `compare-capacity` — Which holds more?

**Standard:** K.MD.A.2 (same clause). Describing words: holds more, holds less, the same amount.
**Foundations first:** "Capacity means how much a container holds." Two containers in outline, water as a teal fill. The fair test: fill one, pour it into the other, and look. Three things can happen, shown once each on a small card that stays up: water spills over the rim (the first one holds more), the water stops below the rim with room left (the second one holds more), the water comes exactly to the rim and nothing spills (they hold the same).
**Read:** "A was full. All of it was poured into B, which was empty. Which holds more?" What it really asks: after the pour, what does B look like? At a glance: look at B's rim and at the table under it. Room, spill, or exactly full.
**The example:** "The glass (A) was full of water. All of it was poured into the mug (B), which was empty. Which holds more?" Choices: A, the glass | B, the mug | They hold the same amount. Picture: a tall thin glass, full; a short wide mug, empty. (1) Read. "The glass is taller. Taller is not the same as holds more. Pour, then look." (2) **Pour**: the water leaves the glass, rises in the mug, and stops with a clear gap under the rim. (3) "All of the glass's water fit, and there is room for more. The mug holds more." (4) B, the mug.
Quick second cases (the other two outcomes, one beat each, with the card): the vase poured into the pot: water runs over the pot's rim onto the table: "The pot could not hold it all. The vase holds more: A." The can into the pan: the water comes exactly to the rim: "Just full, nothing spilled: they hold the same."
**Fast:** Look at B: room left, B holds more; spilled, A holds more; exactly to the rim, the same.
**Sense:** Say what happened to the water in one sentence; the sentence names the answer.
**Check:** Picture pouring back: everything in the mug would fill the glass to the top with none left over? No; the mug's water would overflow a glass that small. The mug holds more.
**Rule out:** Water on the table dims "the same" and "B" together; a gap under the rim dims "the same" and "A" together.
**Line:** "Taller is not more. Pour and look."
**Trap:** Judging by height: the tall glass holds more. The example is the tall thin glass against the short wide mug, and the pour decides it.
**Kit:** `container` with `fill` and `pour`, spilling over the rim when capacity is passed (section 4.5); shapes glass, mug, vase, pot, can, pan.
**Writer:** Strongest. The pour must be staged so the three outcomes read without words, and the K voice carries it.

## Grade 1: measure-equal-units (1.MD.A.1, 1.MD.A.2)

Every film in this skill that lays units opens the same way, ten seconds, on a crayon: cubes arrive one at a time from the crayon's left end, each touching the last, the count ticking one, two, three; the last cube ends where the crayon ends. "The crayon is three cubes long. A cube is the unit. Measuring is counting the unit laid along the thing: start at the end, touching, no piling up, same unit every time." Family 1 in one line: the last number said is how many.

### `unit-size-and-count` — Smaller unit, more of them

Off grade: filed grade 1 but it is 2.MD.A.2 ("Measure the length of an object twice, using length units of different lengths... describe how the two measurements relate to the size of the unit chosen"), and the "how many long units" variant is division, 3.OA.A.3. Design it as grade 2's idea; keep the division case brief and second. It is in the pilot because it is the family's one idea.
**Standard:** 2.MD.A.2, the clause quoted; 3.OA.A.3 for the count variant.
**Foundations first:** The opening beat. Then one ribbon measured twice: cubes laid along it, count six; the same ribbon, **re-laid** with paper clips, each clip drawn exactly three cubes long, count two. The ribbon never moves. "The ribbon did not change. The clip is longer, so fewer clips fit." Said then, and once: **"Smaller unit, more of them."** This is foundation 5, and the family's line.
**Read:** Three of the four variants ask *which unit needs fewer* or *why does one row use more*; one asks *how many long units*. The first is a why; the second is a count. At a glance for the why: lay one copy of each unit on a start line; the longer unit is the one that needs fewer. At a glance for the count: the answer is less than the small-unit count.
**Example A (the why):** "The same ribbon is measured with unit A and with unit B. Which unit needs fewer copies to cover the ribbon, and why?" Choices: Unit A, because it is longer | Unit A, because it is shorter | Unit B, because it is shorter | Unit B, because it is longer. Picture: one copy of A and one of B on a start line; A is three times B. (1) Read; *fewer* goes gold. (2) Compare the units themselves: A sticks out past B. "A is the longer unit." (3) Lay A along the ribbon: two copies. Lay B along the same ribbon, below: six copies. (4) A needs fewer, because A is longer. (5) Unit A, because it is longer.
**Example B (the count):** "The ribbon is 6 small units long. One long unit is as long as 3 small units. How many long units cover the ribbon?" Choices: 9 | 1 | 3 | 2. A different act (counting groups), so fully worked, briefly. (1) Six cubes along the ribbon, counted. (2) One long unit drawn exactly three cubes long beside them. (3) **Bracket** three cubes: "one long unit"; bracket the next three: "two." The brackets reach the end. (4) Two long units. Family 7 in one line: "groups of three in six: two groups."
**Fast:** Longer unit, fewer of them; count by the group size.
**Sense:** The long-unit count is always smaller than the small-unit count. Two is less than six.
**Check:** Two long units are two threes, six small units: the ribbon's length. For the why: lay them and count.
**Rule out:** In A, two choices say something false about the picture (A shorter, B longer): dim together, "we can see A is longer." Then B-because-shorter: B needs more, not fewer. In B, 9 is more than 6 and a longer unit can never need more copies; 1 long unit reaches only halfway. Dim together.
**Line:** "Smaller unit, more of them."
**Trap:** "Bigger unit, bigger number": children pair big with more. The re-lay in the foundations and the two rows in the example make the longer unit visibly give the smaller count. The "gaps" decoy is handled in rule out: "both rows have no gaps; look."
**Kit:** `ruler` `lay` with two unit shapes drawn to scale against each other, re-lay on the same object, `bracket` with `per`, `dim` with a reason.
**Writer:** Strongest. A why with the misconception as decoys, and the first time the family's line is said; the picture has to make the reason visible, not stated.

### `measure-with-units` — How many paper clips long?

**Standard:** 1.MD.A.2, "Express the length of an object as a whole number of length units, by laying multiple copies of a shorter object (the length unit) end to end."
**Foundations first:** The opening beat on the crayon (cubes, three), which defines unit and shows the three conditions as the units land. Then: "Any same-size thing can be the unit: cubes, paper clips, your foot." The ribbon's clips are the example; the crayon's cubes were the definition, so the unit changes between them without a second example of the same case.
**Read:** "Paper clips are lined up along the ribbon, end to end with no gaps or overlaps. How many paper clips long is the ribbon?" What it asks: a count. At a glance: the first clip touches the start, the last touches the end, so the count is the length.
**The example:** The ribbon with nine clips (answer 9). (1) Read; *how many paper clips* goes gold: "the answer is a count of clips." (2) Check the ends: the first clip's end sits on the ribbon's start; the last clip's end sits on the ribbon's end (gold drop lines at both). (3) Count, touching each clip as it is said: each clip lights on its word, the count label ticking one to nine. (4) Nine paper clips long. The digit under the row.
**Fast:** Touch each unit once as you say its number; the last number is the length.
**Sense:** If the last unit ends where the object ends, the count is the whole object, no more, no less.
**Check:** Count again from the other end: nine again.
**Line:** "Touch each one once. The last number is how many."
**Trap:** Losing the count partway, or counting one clip twice, on a row of nine identical shapes. The count label lights each clip as it is said, one per word, so the eye and the voice agree.
**Kit:** `ruler` `lay` with clip and cube shapes and a per-word count; the ribbon, crayon, pencil, straw as objects.
**Writer:** A capable cheaper model, from this concept and the opening beat, after an independent review of a sample for grade-1 language and pacing.

### `correct-unit-measure` — Which picture measures it right?

**Standard:** 1.MD.A.2, "the number of same-size length units that span it with no gaps or overlaps."
**Foundations first:** The opening beat gives the right picture. Then the lesson breaks it three ways in front of the learner, each as its own `lay` done wrong, each with the count going wrong on screen: a **gap** ("the ribbon under the gap was never counted; the count is too small"), an **overlap** ("that bit of ribbon was counted twice; the count is too big"), and a start past the object's end or units that stop short ("part of the crayon has no units on it"). No rule is recited; each break is seen. Then the three questions to ask of any picture: Does the first unit start at the end? Do the units touch without piling? Does the last unit reach the other end?
**Read:** "Which picture shows how to measure the crayon with cubes? Picture A | B | C." What it asks: which picture passes all three questions. At a glance: white space between units or units sitting on one another rules a picture out before the ends are even checked.
**The example:** Three pictures stacked, the same crayon in each, to scale. A: cubes with a gap between the second and third. B: cubes touching, first at the start, last at the end. C: two cubes piled on one another. (1) Read; *how to measure* goes gold. (2) Picture A: the gap lights; "crayon nobody counted"; A dims with that reason. (3) Picture C: the overlap lights; "counted twice"; C dims. (4) Picture B: start, touching, end, each checked with a gold line as it is said. (5) Picture B.
**Fast:** Look for white space and for piles first; then check both ends.
**Sense:** A right picture covers the whole object once.
**Check:** Count B's cubes; the first touches the start, the last the end, so that count is the crayon's length and no other picture gives it.
**Rule out:** Each wrong picture dims the moment its break is found, with its reason. Nothing is struck out (K to 2).
**Line:** "No gaps, no piles, end to end."
**Trap:** Evenly spaced gaps look tidy, like a dotted line, and children accept them. The foundation shows the count under a gapped row come out too small, so a gap is seen as uncounted object, not as neatness.
**Kit:** `ruler` `lay` with `"wrong": "gap" | "overlap" | "offset"` and the wrong count or "?" (section 4.1); three rulers in one figure; `dim` on a picture.
**Writer:** Strongest. The counterexamples have to be staged so the error is visible in the count, and the lesson must not become a rule list.

### `order-lengths` — Which ribbon is shortest?

**Standard:** 1.MD.A.1, "Order three objects by length." (The clause "compare the lengths of two objects indirectly by using a third object" is not practiced by any type; see section 6.)
**Foundations first:** Longer and shorter from kindergarten, in one line: "longer sticks out past, from the same start." The new idea: you can know an order without seeing the ribbons, by drawing what the sentences say. Build the picture from two sentences before the problem: "Blue is longer than green" draws a blue strip on a start line, then a green strip stopping short of it. "You do not have to see the real ribbons. The sentence tells you where to stop the strip."
**Read:** Two sentences, each comparing two ribbons; one question word, longest or shortest, which goes gold. What it really asks: draw all three and read off an end. At a glance: a ribbon that is longer than one ribbon and shorter than another sits between them, so it is neither the longest nor the shortest.
**The example:** "The purple ribbon is longer than the blue ribbon. The red ribbon is shorter than the blue ribbon. Which ribbon is the shortest?" Choices: The red ribbon | The blue ribbon | The purple ribbon. (1) Read; *shortest* goes gold. (2) Blue is in both sentences, so draw blue first on the start line. (3) Sentence one: purple is longer than blue: a purple strip above blue, sticking out past it. (4) Sentence two: red is shorter than blue: a red strip below blue, stopping short of it. (5) Three strips on one start line: purple longest, red shortest. (6) The red ribbon.
Quick second case (the order of the sentences does not help you, one or two beats): "The blue ribbon is longer than the yellow ribbon. The red ribbon is longer than the blue ribbon. Which is the longest?" Draw blue; yellow stops short; red sticks out past blue. Longest: red, even though blue was the one called longer first.
**Fast:** Draw the ribbon named in both sentences first; each sentence adds one strip, sticking out or stopping short; read the asked end.
**Sense:** Three strips on one start line can always be read; the one that sticks out most is longest, the one that stops first is shortest.
**Check:** Read both sentences against the drawing: is purple past blue? Is red short of blue? Both true, so the picture is right.
**Rule out:** The ribbon named in both sentences is longer than one and shorter than the other, so it is in the middle: it dims with that reason. Two remain; the drawing decides.
**Line:** "Draw what the sentence says. Then look."
**Trap:** Grabbing the word from a sentence: "the blue ribbon is shorter than the yellow" and the child answers blue for shortest, before reading the second sentence. The drawing is only read after both strips are placed, and the quick case shows the first-named ribbon is not the answer.
**Kit:** Strips as `poly` with the family's start line, drawn on the word; the ribbon colors come from the problem (purple, blue, red, yellow, green) and are the one place this family's color scheme yields to the problem's own names. Exists.
**Writer:** Strongest. The picture is built from language, and the quick case must be chosen so the trap is live.

## Grade 2: ruler-measurement (2.MD.A.1, 2.MD.A.2, 2.MD.A.3, 2.MD.A.4)

### `shifted-ruler-length` — A strip that does not start at 0

**Standard:** 2.MD.A.1, "Measure the length of an object by selecting and using appropriate tools such as rulers." This type practices *using* a ruler; no type here practices *selecting* among rulers, yardsticks and tapes (section 6).
**Foundations first:** Foundation 6, built live. Centimeter squares are laid along a strip (the grade-1 act, one line), then they **join** into one bar: the squares fuse, a tick appears at every join, numbers at the ticks, 0 at the start, "cm" at the right end. "A ruler is the units already laid down. The number at a mark says how many spaces come before it." Count the spaces of a strip at 0 to 5: five spaces light, the end reads 5. "Count the spaces, not the lines." (The strip has five spaces and six lines; one beat shows that.)
**Read:** "The strip runs from A to B. What is its length in cm?" What it asks: how many centimeter spaces between A and B, not the number under B. At a glance: A is not at 0, so the answer is less than B's number.
**The example:** A at 3, B at 8, answer 5. (1) Read; *length* goes gold; "the answer is a count of spaces, in centimeters." (2) The **slide**, which is the proof: the strip sits at 0 to 5 with its five spaces lit; it slides right until its start is at 3; the lit spaces ride with it; its end now reads 8. "Same strip. Still five spaces. The end just reads a bigger number because it started later." (3) Read the picture as given: gold drop lines from A to the 3 and from B to the 8. The spaces between them light one at a time: one, two, three, four, five. (4) On the board, with the ruler's numbers: 8 − 3 = 5. "End minus start." (5) 5 cm.
**Fast:** End minus start. (Counting the lit spaces is the same number and the way to see why.)
**Sense:** The start is not 0, so the length is less than the end reading; 5 is less than 8.
**Check:** Count the spaces from A to B: five. Or count on from 3: four, five, six, seven, eight is five hops.
**Line:** "Length is end minus start. Count the spaces, not the lines."
**Trap:** Answering 8, the number under the strip's end: the most-missed ruler item in the grade. The slide shows the same five-space strip reading 5 and then 8, so the end number is seen to depend on where the strip started. A second real one, counting lines (3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 is six) gives 6; only spaces ever light.
**Kit:** `ruler` with `join`, `slide` (lit spaces ride with the object, drop lines and readings update), `light` (section 4.1).
**Writer:** Strongest, for this first one: the slide is the lesson and must be staged on the word. Once the kit action exists and this lesson is approved, `fractional-ruler-length` and the later ruler types can follow from it.

### `fractional-ruler-length` — Halves, fourths and eighths of an inch

Off grade and partly badly conceived. Halves and fourths of an inch are 3.MD.B.4, not grade 2; eighths are beyond that standard. The centimeter variant ("A to B, 3/4 cm") uses a centimeter ruler marked in fourths, a tool that does not exist (centimeter rulers are marked in tenths); replace it with inches or drop it. And the answers are improper fractions (11/4, 15/8) while a ruler is read as "two and three quarters"; the checker must accept 2¾ as canonical and 11/4 as also correct, or the lesson contradicts the checker. "To the nearest eighth" is reading, not rounding, when the end sits on a mark; the template should say so.
**Standard:** 3.MD.B.4, "measuring lengths using rulers marked with halves and fourths of an inch."
**Foundations first:** Foundation 6 again in one line ("a ruler is the units laid down; count the spaces") and the **cut**, live: one inch space on the ruler splits in half, a short tick appears, labeled ½; each half splits again, ticks labeled ¼ and ¾. "The tall marks with numbers are inches. The short marks cut one inch into four equal spaces: each small space is a fourth of an inch." Family 9 in one line: a fourth is one of four equal parts. The mark between 2 and 3 is read: "two and a half."
**Read:** "How long is the ribbon, in inches?" What it asks: whole inches, then how many small spaces past the last whole inch. At a glance: the end sits between 2 and 3, past the halfway mark, so the answer is between 2½ and 3.
**The example:** A ribbon from 0 to 2¾ (answer 11/4). (1) Read; *inches* goes gold. (2) Find the size of one small space first, before reading anything: between 0 and 1 are four small spaces; the step arc reads "¼ in." (3) Whole inches: the ribbon passes 1 and passes 2; two whole inches light. (4) Past the 2: the small spaces light one at a time, one fourth, two fourths, three fourths. (5) Two and three fourths inches; on the board: 2¾ in. (6) In fourths alone, since the app writes it that way: two inches is eight fourths, plus three is eleven: 2¾ = 11/4. Both forms stay on the board; the lesson says the ruler is read as two and three quarters.
Quick second cases (different cuts, same method, two or three beats each): a pencil on a ruler cut into eighths, from 0 to 1⅞: eight small spaces in one inch, each an eighth; one whole inch, then seven small spaces: 1⅞ = 15/8. And a strip not starting at 0 (A to B): count the small spaces between A and B, three quarters: ¾ in; or end minus start, as in `shifted-ruler-length`.
**Fast:** Count the small spaces in one whole inch; that is the small space's size. Whole inches, then leftover small spaces. (Or count every small space from the start and write it over the small space's number: eleven fourths.)
**Sense:** The end is between 2 and 3 and closer to 3; 2¾ is.
**Check:** One more small space would reach 3: 2¾ + ¼ = 3. Or count all eleven fourths from 0.
**Line:** "Find the size of one small space first."
**Trap:** Counting the small ticks as inches (reading 11 inches), or calling every small space a half no matter how many there are. The step beat, counting the small spaces inside one inch before any reading, makes both impossible, and the eighths case shows the small space's size changing with the cut.
**Kit:** `ruler` with `sub` and `cut`, `light` on sub-spaces, the step arc on a fraction (section 4.1); board acceptance of a mixed number and an improper fraction as the same answer.
**Writer:** A capable cheaper model, from this concept, once `shifted-ruler-length` has been approved and the `ruler` cut exists: one method on one picture, with the cases named here.
