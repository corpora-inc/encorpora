# Measurement, part 4: conversions (grade 5) and units and precision (Algebra 1): the concepts

Ten types. Two notes before the concepts, because they shape several of them.

- `measurement-conversions` is filed as a grade-5, within-system skill (5.MD.A.1). `approximate-unit-factor` is cross-system with decimal factors; it is correctly judged to serve 5.NBT.B.7 and 6.RP.A.3d, and the lesson should say so rather than pretend it is 5.MD.A.1.
- `units-and-precision` is named for HSN.Q.A.3 and no type practices it. `fl-metric-customary-conversion` is grade 6 (6.RP.A.3d) and `measurement-error-bound` is grade 7 (7.RP.A.3, percent error). Both are fine as applications inside Algebra 1; both concepts below are written at the grade their content is.

Every lesson in this part stands on the family's line: the amount never changes; the count changes with the unit; smaller unit, more of them. The direction is said aloud before any arithmetic, every time.

## Grade 5: measurement-conversions (5.MD.A.1)

### `mixed-length-total` — Two lengths in two units, total in the smaller unit

**Standard.** 5.MD.A.1: "Convert among different-sized standard measurement units within a given measurement system ... and use these conversions in solving multi-step, real world problems." Also 4.MD.A.1: "express measurements in a larger unit in terms of a smaller unit."

**Foundations first.** Three things, built in front of the learner. (1) A centimeter is a hundredth of a meter: the unit chart with headers m | dm | cm, the point beside the meters column; "centi is the hundredths column." (2) The double ruler: meters in blue on top, centimeters in violet below, the same zero, every blue tick exactly over a violet tick that reads 100, 200, 300. (3) The family's line, said once: nothing is added until the names match. You already know this from fractions: thirds and fifths are not added until the pieces are the same size.

**Read.** Two strips, two units, and the asked unit is centimeters (gold). The answer is a count of centimeters, bigger than the meter count by a lot. At a glance: the meters will gain two zeros; the centimeters stay as they are.

**The example.** 4 m and 8 cm, total in cm. Chosen so that "write them side by side" fails (the generated 5 m + 35 cm happens to give 535 both ways, and a lesson must never let that trick survive). (1) The 4-m strip (teal) lies along the double ruler from 0 to the blue 4; the 8-cm strip (rust) is laid touching its end. (2) The gold across-line at the blue 4 reads 400 on the violet scale: four meters is four hundred centimeters. In the chart, 4 shifts two columns left and the emptied columns fill with zeros: 4 m = 400 cm. (3) Now both are centimeters: 400 + 8, stacked with headers; the tens column is empty and holds its zero: 408. (4) The rust strip's end sits over the violet 408. 408 cm.

**Fast.** Meters gain two zeros; then add.

**Sense.** A little more than 4 m, and 4 m is 400 cm; 408 is a little more.

**Check.** 408 cm back to meters: 4.08 m, which is 4 m and 8 cm.

**Line.** "Make the names match, then add."

**Trap.** 4 + 8 = 12 (adding unlike units) and 48 cm (sticking the counts together). The double ruler makes the first impossible: the 4-m strip is visibly fifty times the other. The second is killed by the example: the zero in the tens column is written on screen, and the lesson says "4 m is four hundred centimeters, not forty-something."

**Kit.** Unit headers on the chart (4.2); `ruler` with `scales` and `across`. Nothing beyond the family's tools.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, one picture with named actions, and the unit-aware board check catches the arithmetic.

### `mixed-unit-border` — Perimeter of a rectangle with sides in two units

**Standard.** 5.MD.A.1 (as above) with 4.MD.A.3: "Apply the area and perimeter formulas for rectangles in real world and mathematical problems."

**Foundations first.** Perimeter belongs to family 24; name it in one line and show it: "the perimeter is the walk around the edge, all four sides." Trace the ribbon around the app's rectangle once, the length label counting as it goes, so "once around" means four sides before any number appears. Then the unit chart with m | dm | cm, and the family's line: nothing is added until the names match.

**Read.** A rectangle with one side in meters and one in centimeters; the asked unit is centimeters (gold). The answer is a count of centimeters, and it is four sides' worth, so about four times a side. At a glance: the meter side gains two zeros; the centimeter side stays.

**The example.** The app's rectangle, 2 m by 162 cm, drawn to scale (200 against 162, nearly square, which is why both sides must be read, not guessed). (1) The side labeled 2 m: in the chart, 2 shifts two columns left: 200 cm. The label on the figure rewrites itself to 200 cm; the shape does not change. (2) Now every side has a name in centimeters: 200, 162, 200, 162. (3) The ribbon traces the border side by side while the board writes 200 + 162 + 200 + 162, then the shorter form 2 × (200 + 162) under it: both are the same walk. (4) Stack 200 + 162 = 362; double it: 724. (5) 724 cm.

**Fast.** Convert the odd side, add the two sides, double.

**Sense.** Each side is about 2 m; four sides are about 8 m, which is 800 cm; 724 is a bit less because one pair is shorter than 2 m.

**Check.** 724 ÷ 2 = 362 = 200 + 162. Or 724 cm is 7.24 m, a sensible walk around a 2-m-by-1.62-m rectangle.

**Line.** "Make the names match, then walk all four sides."

**Trap.** Two real ones. 2 + 162 + 2 + 162 = 328, adding the counts as if they were the same unit: the rewritten label on the figure (200 cm) means 2 never reaches the board. And adding only the two labeled sides (362): the traced ribbon shows four sides lit, two of each.

**Kit.** The app's rectangle figure with a label that rewrites (`label` text change on the word), `draw` along the border with a running length. Exists or near it.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: the concept fixes the figure and the two traps.

### `customary-unit-reverse` — To the larger unit: whole units, or an exact count

**Standard.** 5.MD.A.1; the standard's own example ("convert 5 cm to 0.05 m") is this direction. Note: the goal mentions a two-step conversion (cups to quarts to gallons) that no variant generates; if it is added, it is the same picture twice.

**Foundations first.** The fact as a group size: "1 yard = 3 feet" says a yard is a group of three feet. The double ruler: feet in violet below, yards in blue above, a blue tick over every third violet tick. Then the direction, stated as the picture shows it: to the bigger unit there are fewer, because each one is a group. And the one new idea: a leftover smaller than a group is not a group. From family 7: division with a remainder.

**Read.** Two wordings under one name. "How many *whole* yards can be made from 31 feet" (whole goes gold): count complete groups; leftovers do not count. "40 pints is how many gallons": the count comes out exact. At a glance: the answer is smaller than the given number, about a third of it for yards, an eighth for gallons.

**The example.** 31 feet, whole yards. (1) 31 foot-spaces lit on the ruler, counted. (2) Brackets of three lay left to right, each labeled 1 yd, the yard count ticking 1, 2, ... 10. (3) One foot-space is left lit and unbracketed, labeled as the leftover; it cannot make a yard: a yard is three feet and this is one. (4) Board: 31 ÷ 3 = 10 R 1. The question asks whole yards: 10.

**Second case, brief** (a different wording and no leftover): 40 pints to gallons, 1 gallon = 8 pints. The same picture on a capacity line: pint ticks below, a gallon tick every 8. Brackets of 8: five, nothing left over. 40 ÷ 8 = 5 gallons. Three or four beats.

**Fast.** Divide by the group size. The whole-number part is the answer; the remainder is the leftover and never rounds up.

**Sense.** Fewer yards than feet; 31 is about 30, and 30 feet is 10 yards.

**Check.** Multiply back with the leftover: 10 × 3 + 2... no: 10 × 3 = 30, plus the 1 leftover is 31. And 11 yards would be 33 feet, more than we have.

**Line.** "Bigger unit, smaller number: divide. The leftover never makes another whole."

**Trap.** Rounding up to 11 because "there is a remainder": the lone unbracketed foot on screen is one foot, not a yard. And multiplying (93) because "converting means multiply": the direction is said before the division, and 93 yards from 31 feet is more yards than feet, which the ruler shows is impossible.

**Kit.** `ruler` `bracket` with `r.leftover`; the same bracket on a capacity line (the `bar` with two scales, or the ruler with unit names pt and gal).

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model once `bracket` exists; the two wordings are one picture and the concept settles the "whole" reading.

### `customary-mixed-units` — Mixed measures: to one unit, to mixed form, and adding with a trade (pilot #4)

**Standard.** 5.MD.A.1 and 4.MD.A.1 ("express measurements in a larger unit in terms of a smaller unit"). The goal also names subtraction with a trade; no variant generates it. Confirm, and if it exists, it is the third example's trade run backward (one cup split into eight ounces), one quick beat.

**Foundations first.** A mixed measure is two counts in two units: 5 ft 1 in is five of one thing and one of another. The fact is the group size: a foot is a group of twelve inches. The double ruler: feet in blue above, inches in violet below, each blue tick over a violet 12, 24, 36. Then the family's line in this type's form: every part gets converted before anything is combined. And one line from place value: a trade is ten ones becoming a ten; here the group is twelve, or eight, or three, and the trade happens at that number, not at ten.

**Read.** Three kinds, told apart by the words. "As inches only": one unit asked, so the feet must become inches first. "As yards and feet, with fewer than 3 feet left over": a count of groups and a leftover smaller than the group. "Add ... write the sum with fewer than 8 fluid ounces": small units first, trade at eight. At a glance in every kind: the small part of any mixed form must be less than the group size, so "2 yd 7 ft" is nonsense on sight.

**Example A, to one unit.** 5 ft 1 in as inches; choices 61 in, 51 in, 6 in, 60 in. Chosen because the 1 inch exposes the digit-sticking trap best. (1) The strip lies from 0 to just past the blue 5 on the double ruler. (2) The gold across-line at the blue 5 reads 60 on the violet scale: five feet is sixty inches; a table row writes itself: 5 ft | 60 in. (3) One more inch: the strip's end is one violet space past 60: 61. (4) Board: 5 × 12 = 60; 60 + 1 = 61. 61 in.
Rule out: 6 in adds the two counts without converting (a 5-foot strip is not six inches long: the ruler shows it); 60 in forgot the inch; 51 in sticks the digits together, and the lesson names it: "that is not how inches work; five feet is sixty inches, not fifty." Then 61 is proved.

**Example B, to mixed form.** 23 feet as yards and feet; choices 2 yd 3 ft, 7 yd 2 ft, 2 yd 7 ft, 6 yd 2 ft. (1) 23 foot-spaces lit. (2) Brackets of three, labeled 1 yd, counting to 7. (3) Two spaces left, lit, the leftover: 2 ft. (4) Board: 23 ÷ 3 = 7 R 2. 7 yd 2 ft.
Rule out: a leftover of 3 or 7 feet is not finished trading, another yard fits (both dim together with that one reason); 6 yd 2 ft is 20 feet. Prove: 7 × 3 + 2 = 23.

**Example C, adding with a trade.** 6 cups 5 fl oz + 3 cups 6 fl oz; choices 10 cups 1 fl oz, 10 cups 3 fl oz, 9 cups 3 fl oz, 11 cups 3 fl oz. Picture: two bars of cups, each cup cut into eight ounce-pieces (the re-cut bar), the loose ounces as pieces at the end. (1) Small units first: 5 + 6 = 11 ounce-pieces. (2) Eight of them fuse into one cup, which slides into the cups pile; three remain. (3) Cups: 6 + 3 + 1 = 10. (4) The stack with unit headers cups | fl oz, the carry written as the fuse happened: 10 cups 3 fl oz.
Rule out: 9 cups 3 fl oz forgot the traded cup; 11 cups 3 fl oz traded a cup that was not there. 10 cups 1 fl oz is the real one: trading at ten (write 1, carry 1) as if ounces were base ten; the lesson says "eight ounces make a cup, so the trade happens at eight, and three are left, not one."

**Fast.** To the small unit: big count × group size + small count. To mixed form: divide; the remainder stays in the small unit. To add: small units first, trade whenever they reach the group size, then the big units.

**Sense.** 5 ft 1 in is just over five feet, so just over 60 inches. 23 ft is a bit under 8 yards. Two cups' worth of ounces trades to one cup.

**Check.** Convert back: 61 ÷ 12 = 5 R 1. 7 × 3 + 2 = 23. 10 cups 3 oz is 83 oz; 6 c 5 oz is 53 and 3 c 6 oz is 30; 53 + 30 = 83.

**Line.** "Every part gets converted; the leftover stays in the small unit. Trade at the group, not at ten."

**Trap.** Named above, all real: digit-sticking (51), the unfinished trade (2 yd 7 ft), the base-ten carry (10 c 1 oz).

**Kit.** `ruler` with `across` and `bracket`; `table` row from `across`; the re-cut `bar` with piece names and a fuse of n pieces into one; `stack` with unit headers and a per-column group size (`"groups": {"fl oz": 8}`) so the carry is drawn at eight. The last is new.

**Writer.** The strongest writer: three kinds, decoys that are the misconceptions, and a trade at a non-ten group that the kit has never drawn.

### `meters-to-kilometers-blanks` — Add meters, write kilometers and meters

**Standard.** 5.MD.A.1: "use these conversions in solving multi-step, real world problems." The blanks ask for km and m, so the answer is a mixed measure.

**Foundations first.** Kilo is the thousands column: the chart with headers km | hm | dam | m, the meters column as the ones. One thousand meters is one kilometer, about ten minutes of walking (the benchmark, one line). Then the insight that makes the type trivial, built from place value's periods: the comma in a meter count is the split between kilometers and meters, because a kilometer is exactly a thousand meters. Show it: 2,248 m with a bracket over the thousands period labeled km and over the ones period labeled m.

**Read.** Three legs in meters; the sentence wants the total as kilometers and meters. At a glance: each leg is under a kilometer, so three legs are under three kilometers; the meters part of the answer must be less than 1,000.

**The example.** 542 m, 772 m, 934 m. (1) The three legs laid end to end on a line from 0 to 3 km, kilometer ticks in blue, meters in violet below; the total's end lands past the blue 2. (2) The stack with headers: 542 + 772 + 934 = 2,248, each column worked, the carries said as trades. (3) The km bracket sits over the 2; the m bracket over 248. The comma is the split. (4) The sentence fills: 2 kilometers 248 meters. On the line, the end is 248 past the 2-km tick.

**Fast.** Add. The digits left of the comma are kilometers; the three to its right are meters.

**Sense.** Under 3 km, more than 1.5 km (two legs alone pass 1,300).

**Check.** 2 × 1,000 + 248 = 2,248; and 248 is less than 1,000, so no kilometer is hiding in it.

**Line.** "Kilo is the thousands column. The comma is the split."

**Trap.** Splitting the total at the wrong place: 22 km 48 m, or 2 km 48 m. The period brackets labeled km and m sit over the digits, so the split is never guessed. Also a plain addition slip in the carries, which the stack makes visible.

**Kit.** `stack` with unit headers and period brackets carrying unit names (4.2); the line is `ruler` with two scales.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, the insight is written here.

### `approximate-unit-factor` — A decimal factor across systems; pick the closest

**Standard.** 5.NBT.B.7 (multiply decimals) and 6.RP.A.3d ("use ratio reasoning to convert measurement units"). Not 5.MD.A.1: the factors cross systems. Say so once in the alignment list; the lesson itself just teaches.

**Foundations first.** The factor as a sentence: "one meter is about 3.28 feet" means a foot is smaller than a meter, a little less than a third of one. The double ruler with meters in blue above and feet in violet below, and this time the ticks do not line up: 1 m sits a little past 3 ft, 2 m a little past 6. That is what "about" means, and it is why the question says "closest." Then the family's line: smaller unit, more of them; the direction decides whether the number grows or shrinks before any arithmetic.

**Read.** Decimal factor, two units, "closest" (gold). The choices are far apart, so the answer is found by direction and estimate; exact arithmetic only confirms. At a glance: which unit is smaller? The number should go that way.

**The example.** 29 meters in feet; one meter is about 3.28 feet; choices 8.8, 25.7, 95.1, 32.3 feet. (1) The factor read as a sentence; a foot is the smaller unit, so more feet than meters: the answer is bigger than 29. (2) Estimate with a friendly factor: a meter is about 3 feet, so 29 m is about 87 ft, a bit more. (3) Rule out: 8.8, 25.7 and 32.3 are all below or near 29; feet must be about three times the meters. They dim together with that one reason. (4) 95.1 is the only one near 87-and-a-bit. (5) Confirm exactly: 29 × 3.28 stacked as 29 × 328 = 9,512, two places: 95.12. 95.1 feet.

**Second case, brief** (the other direction, where the estimate must carry it alone): 17 km in miles; a mile is about 1.6 km, so a mile is bigger and there are fewer miles. About 17 ÷ 1.5, which is around 11. Only 10.6 is near 11; 27.2 (× 1.6), 18.6 (+ 1.6) and 15.4 (− 1.6) go the wrong way or are not a conversion at all. Check by multiplying back: 10.6 × 1.6 is about 17.

**Fast.** Direction first; round the factor to a friendly number; estimate; the one choice near it is the answer.

**Sense.** The estimate is the sense check; the direction is the first half of it.

**Check.** Convert back: 95.1 ÷ 3.28 is about 29; or 95 feet at about 3 per meter is about 32 meters, in the right range.

**Line.** "Say the direction, then estimate with a friendly number."

**Trap.** The decoys are the traps: adding or subtracting the factor, and multiplying when the unit is bigger. The direction beat kills three choices before any arithmetic, and the lesson says that "closest" means the estimate is the method here.

**Kit.** `ruler` with a non-integer `per` on the second scale (ticks that do not align).

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, and the decoys are structural.

## Algebra 1: units-and-precision (HSN.Q.A.1, HSN.Q.A.3)

### `unit-chain-conversion` — Multiply through a chain so the units come out right

**Standard.** HSN.Q.A.1: "Use units as a way to understand problems and to guide the solution of multi-step problems; choose and interpret units consistently in formulas." HSG.MG.A.2 for the density variants ("Apply concepts of density based on area and volume").

**Foundations first.** A conversion fact is a fraction equal to one: 1000 m over 1 km is a length over itself. Multiplying by one changes the name, never the amount (the double ruler, once, as the why: 18 km and 18,000 m are the same gold line). A rate is also a fraction with units, and any "per" fact may be written either way up: 4 g per cm³ is also 1 cm³ per 4 g. Units cancel exactly like factors, and the unit left standing is the answer's unit. Then the discipline this type rewards: every quantity is written with its unit in every line, and you write what you have and what you want before any fraction.

**Read.** Several facts, one quantity, a requested unit (gold). At a glance: name the unit you have and the unit you want; every unit in between must appear once on top and once on the bottom. Often the sense check is faster than the chain: half an hour at 18 km/h is 9 km.

**The example.** 18 km/h for 30 min, in meters. (1) Have: 18 km per 1 h, and 30 min. Want: m. (2) The chain on the board: 18 km / 1 h × 1000 m / 1 km × 1 h / 60 min × 30 min. Each fraction is placed with the unit to lose on the opposite side from where it stands; the lesson says why for each. (3) Cancel: km with km, h with h, min with min light gold in pairs and fade; m stands. (4) Numbers: 18 × 1000 × 30 ÷ 60 = 9,000. 9,000 m. (5) Sense said aloud: half an hour at 18 km an hour is 9 km, which is 9,000 m.

**Second example, full** (the fact that must be flipped): 28 kg at 4 g per cm³, in cm³. The density has grams on top, but grams must go; so it is written 1 cm³ / 4 g. 28 kg × 1000 g / 1 kg × 1 cm³ / 4 g = 7,000 cm³. Sense: every cm³ holds 4 g; 28,000 g fill 7,000 of them.

**Third, brief** (three "per"s in a row): 10 mg per kg per day, 20 kg, 4 doses, 50 mg tablets. 10 mg/(kg·day) × 20 kg = 200 mg/day; × 1 day / 4 doses = 50 mg/dose; × 1 tablet / 50 mg = 1 tablet per dose. The units lead; the numbers follow.

**Fast.** Write have and want; chain fractions so every unwanted unit is once up, once down; cancel; multiply the numbers. The surviving unit is the check.

**Sense.** The estimate from the story (half an hour, half the distance); for density, grams per little cube times how many cubes.

**Check.** The unit left is the one asked. 9,000 m is 9 km, half of 18.

**Line.** "The answer is the unit left standing. If it isn't, a fraction is upside down."

**Trap.** A fraction the wrong way up, so a unit appears twice on top and nothing cancels; and 18 × 30 = 540, ignoring that one is per hour and the other is minutes. The cancel motion makes both visible: the pair either lights or it does not.

**Kit.** Board `cancel` (4.7) and the unit-aware math check; `cancel` must handle a compound denominator (kg·day).

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model once `cancel` exists: one method, and the three examples are fixed here.

### `fl-metric-customary-conversion` — One given factor, either direction

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3d: "Use ratio reasoning to convert measurement units." Grade 6 content inside an Algebra 1 skill; fine as a warm-up, not HSN.Q.A.3.

**Foundations first.** The fact as a sentence with a group in it: "1 pound = 0.45 kilograms" says a pound is 0.45 of a kilogram. Which unit is bigger is read from the fact, not assumed: when the number beside the other unit is less than one, the unit with the 1 is the smaller one. The double ruler shows it: pounds in violet below, kilograms in blue above, a kg tick every 2.2 lb or so. Then the direction: to the unit that has the 1, divide (you are making groups); from it, multiply. And money is the same fact with no size to picture: 1 dollar = 18 pesos, a dollar is a group of 18 pesos.

**Read.** A quantity, a factor, a target unit (gold), "enter the number." At a glance: which unit has the 1, and is it the bigger one? Then: should the answer be bigger or smaller than the given number?

**The example.** 15.75 kg in pounds; 1 pound = 0.45 kilograms. Chosen because the factor is less than one, so the learner must read size from the fact. (1) A pound is 0.45 of a kilogram: less than half. So a pound is the smaller unit, and there are more pounds: more than double 15.75, around 31 to 35. (2) Pounds is the unit with the 1, so divide: 15.75 ÷ 0.45. (3) From family 2: move both points together, 1575 ÷ 45, long division: 35. (4) 35 pounds.

**Second case, brief** (multiply, factor greater than one): 30 gallons in liters, 1 gallon = 3.8 liters. Liters are smaller (a gallon is nearly four of them), so more liters, about 120. From the unit with the 1: multiply: 30 × 3.8 = 114.

**Fast.** To the unit with the 1, divide by k; from it, multiply by k. Say which way the number should go first.

**Sense.** A pound is less than half a kilogram, so pounds are more than double the kilograms.

**Check.** 35 × 0.45 = 15.75. 114 ÷ 3.8 = 30.

**Line.** "To the unit with the 1, divide; from it, multiply. Then ask: should it be bigger?"

**Trap.** Assuming the unit with the 1 is the bigger one, so 15.75 × 0.45 (about 7 pounds from nearly 16 kilograms, fewer pounds than kilograms); the direction beat and the ruler make it impossible. And "converting means multiply" when the gallon case is the one that happens to multiply.

**Kit.** `longdiv` for 1575 ÷ 45 (the family and place-value designs both want it; it is still rows of equations today); `ruler` with non-integer `per`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model.

### `measurement-error-bound` — Greatest or smallest possible value from a reading and its error

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3 ("percent error") for the percent form; the absolute form is decimal subtraction. It is the one type in this skill that touches HSN.Q.A.3's idea (a reading has limits), without practicing its clause. Also: the generated answers 689/25 and 4183/100 must be accepted as 27.56 and 41.83; a mass in grams is reported as a decimal.

**Foundations first.** A reading is not the truth; it is the middle of where the truth could be. Draw it: the number line, the reading as a dot, and "at most 0.3 g off" as a bracket of 0.3 on each side; the stretch between is shaded with both ends filled, because "at most" allows the error to equal the limit. The greatest possible value is the right end; the smallest is the left end. Then the one extra step for percent: "at most 10% of the reading" is an amount of grams you compute first, and 10% is a tenth (one shift right).

**Read.** A reading, an error limit (in grams, or as a percent), and one word: greatest or smallest (gold). At a glance: the answer is close to the reading; greatest is a bit above it, smallest a bit below. If the limit is a percent, it must become grams before anything else.

**The example.** 440 g, at most 10% of the reading; greatest possible mass. (1) 10% of 440: a tenth, 440 shifts one place: 44 g. The error is 44 grams, not 10 grams. (2) The line from 380 to 500: 440 drops; a 44 bracket each side; the interval shades from 396 to 484, both ends filled. (3) "Greatest" lights the right end: 440 + 44 = 484. (4) 484 g.

**Second case, brief** (the error given in grams, smallest, a trade across the point): 42.56 g, at most 0.73 g, smallest. The left end: 42.56 − 0.73, stacked with the trade: 41.83 g.

**Fast.** Percent to grams first (a percent of the reading). Greatest: reading plus the error. Smallest: reading minus the error.

**Sense.** The answer is near the reading; 484 is 44 over 440, and 44 is a tenth of 440.

**Check.** 484 − 440 = 44 = 10% of 440; 41.83 + 0.73 = 42.56.

**Line.** "The reading is the middle of where the truth could be."

**Trap.** Treating the percent as grams: 440 + 10 = 450. The interval is drawn 44 wide on each side before the word "greatest" is even read, so 10 g never appears as a distance. "At most" misread as excluding the ends is not something children actually do here; none worth naming beyond the percent.

**Kit.** `numberline` `catch` with `"closed": "both"` and `"center"`/`"radius"` (4.8); `stack` with the point line.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model once the interval piece exists: the why is written here and the method is one.

### `compound-unit-conversion` — Convert every unit inside a measurement (pilot #5)

**Standard.** HSN.Q.A.1: "choose and interpret units consistently in formulas"; 6.RP.A.3d: "manipulate and transform units appropriately when multiplying or dividing quantities." Note: the kilowatt-hour variant (power × time = energy) is a different idea, forming a quantity by multiplying two others; it earns one brief example here but is a candidate for its own type.

**Foundations first.** The square, built in front of the learner: a square one meter on a side; each side is 100 cm; cut it 10 by 10, each cell is 10 cm by 10 cm; zoom into one cell: it is 10 by 10 again, a hundred square centimeters. A hundred cells, each a hundred: 10,000 cm² in one m². Written as the fraction equal to one: (100 cm / 1 m) × (100 cm / 1 m) = 10,000 cm² / 1 m². Then the cube: a hundred layers of that square, a million cm³ (the app's cube solid, built in layers). The lesson says it once: convert the unit, then raise the factor with it: square the unit, square the factor. For a rate, every unit gets its own fraction, top and bottom.

**Read.** A quantity whose unit is squared, cubed, or a rate with a unit on top and a unit on the bottom; the asked unit (gold). At a glance: how many times does each length unit appear? That is how many times its fraction appears. Smaller unit, more of them: m² to cm² must get much bigger, and not by 100.

**Example A, area.** 18 m² in cm². (1) The square view: one m² is 10,000 cm². (2) Board: 18 m² × 100 cm / 1 m × 100 cm / 1 m. Cancel: m × m with m²; cm × cm is cm². (3) 18 × 10,000 = 180,000 cm². (4) Sense: eighteen squares, each ten thousand little ones.

**Quick case, volume.** 17 m³ in cm³: the cube, three fractions, 17 × 1,000,000 = 17,000,000 cm³. Three or four beats.

**Example B, a rate.** 4 g per cm³ in kg per m³. (1) Two units to convert, one on top, one on the bottom. (2) 4 g / 1 cm³ × 1 kg / 1000 g × 1,000,000 cm³ / 1 m³: the big cubic factor goes on top because cm³ is on the bottom. Cancel g with g, cm³ with cm³; kg over m³ stands. (3) 4 × 1,000,000 ÷ 1000 = 4,000 kg/m³. (4) Sense: a cubic meter is a million little cubes at 4 g each: 4 million grams, 4,000 kg.

**Quick case, a squared unit on the bottom.** 233,280 km/h² in m/s²: the hour fraction twice. × 1000 m / 1 km × (1 h / 3600 s)²: 233,280 × 1000 ÷ 12,960,000 = 18 m/s². (The reverse variant, 3 m/s² to km/h², is the same chain upside down: 38,880.)

**Quick case, power and time.** 750 W for 30 min in kWh: energy is power times time, so the units multiply too: 750 W × 30 min × 1 h / 60 min × 1 kW / 1000 W = 0.375 kWh. The unit "watt-hour" is literally the product.

**Fast.** Count how many times each unit appears; that many fractions for it, each the right way up; cancel; multiply. Square the unit, square the factor.

**Sense.** Smaller unit, more of them, and squared or cubed units change by the factor squared or cubed; 100 would leave 18 m² as the area of a bathroom floor in cm², obviously too few.

**Check.** The unit left is the one asked; 180,000 ÷ 10,000 = 18.

**Line.** "Square the unit, square the factor. Convert the bottom too."

**Trap.** All real and all adult: m² to cm² by 100 (the square view shows 100 by 100 cells, so 100 is never a candidate); converting only the top of a rate (the second example cancels both); the hour squared converted once (the quick case writes the fraction twice and squares it). The cancel motion fails visibly when a unit is left over.
