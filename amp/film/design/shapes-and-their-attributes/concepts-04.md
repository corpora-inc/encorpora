## Grade 4: lines-symmetry (4.G.A.3)

### `symmetry-lines-select` — Select the figures whose dashed line(s) are lines of symmetry. Select 2 or 3 of 5.

**Standard.** 4.G.A.3: "Recognize a line of symmetry for a two-dimensional figure as a line across the figure such that the figure can be folded along the line into matching parts. Identify line-symmetric figures." The "draw lines of symmetry" clause is never practiced (noted in the family design).

**Foundations first.** The fold, in the standard's own words, done on screen before any letter is read. One figure, an isosceles triangle pointing down, with a dashed line from its bottom corner to the middle of its top side. "A line of symmetry is a line you could fold the shape along so the two halves match exactly." The muted ghost stays; the copy lifts and flips over the dashed line; the whole outline glows gold: every corner landed on a corner, every side on a side. Then the same triangle with a dashed line that misses: the copy lands, the part that sticks out fills rust and holds. "The dashed line is only a guess. The fold decides." In this family a dashed line means a candidate fold; nothing else here is dashed.

**Read.** "Select the 2 figures whose dashed line is a line of symmetry": select exactly two, after folding each. At a glance: a dashed line with more corners on one side than the other cannot work (a corner needs a partner). Equal counts tell you nothing; fold.

**The example.** Five figures, lettered, one scale. A: a 6 by 4 rectangle, dashed diagonal. B: an isosceles triangle pointing down, dashed line from its point to the middle of the top side. C: a leaning parallelogram, dashed line through the middles of its two long sides. D: a right trapezoid, dashed line through the middles of its two parallel sides. E: a square turned 45°, dashed diagonal (horizontal). (1) A: fold; two corners overhang in rust, held. "The diagonal of a rectangle is not a line of symmetry." (2) B: fold; gold outline. Yes. (3) C: fold; the folded half leans the wrong way, overhang at both ends. No. (4) D: fold; the square corners land where the slant is. No. (5) E: fold over the diagonal; gold. Yes. A and E together: "a diagonal works for the square and fails for the rectangle. You cannot know by looking. Fold." (6) B and E selected; count: two, as asked.

**Second case (worked briefly; a different act).** Each figure has two dashed lines; "both" is gold. A: rectangle, both midlines: pass, pass. B: rectangle, one midline and one diagonal: pass, then the diagonal's rust overhang: out. C: rhombus, both diagonals: pass, pass. D: regular hexagon, corner-to-corner and middle-to-middle: pass, pass. E: isosceles triangle, the apex line and a line across the middle: pass, fail: out. A, C, D. "One miss and the figure is out, even if its other line was perfect."

**Fast.** Fold every dashed line in your head: does each corner have a partner straight across, the same distance from the line? A figure with a corner left over is out.

**Sense.** A shape with a square corner on one side of the line and a slant on the other cannot match.

**Check.** Fold the other half over instead; it must land the same way. Count the chosen figures against the number asked.

**Rule out.** Lines through a parallelogram's middle and a non-square rectangle's diagonals dim first, each for its stated reason, after their fold is shown once. In the two-line case, any figure with one failing line dims the moment it fails.

**Line.** "Fold it. If the halves match, that line is a line of symmetry."

**Trap.** The rectangle's diagonal, and the parallelogram's "it looks balanced." Both appear among the choices and both are folded on screen with the overhang held long enough to see. In the two-line case: selecting a figure because one of its lines worked; "both" is gold and the failing fold is shown.

**Kit.** The family's `fold` (4.4) and `dim` (4.7). Five lettered figures in one row at one scale is a layout the kit must manage on a phone (two rows of three and two).

**Writer.** Capable cheaper writer: one motion, five figures, the examples fully specified here; the K-to-4 voice is the only risk.

## Grade 5: shape-hierarchy (5.G.B.3, 5.G.B.4)

All four hierarchy types open with the same thirty-second build of the map, so the learner meets one picture four times: quadrilaterals the outer region (an example with no parallel sides in the ring); parallelograms inside; rectangles and rhombuses two overlapping regions inside parallelograms; squares their overlap; one to-scale example in every region. As each region is drawn its list is said: four sides; and two pairs of parallel sides; and four right angles; and four equal sides. "A longer list is a smaller group, and it sits inside the shorter one." Trapezoids are drawn inclusively (the family design's recommendation) and said once; none of these four types' choices depend on it.

### `quadrilateral-names` — Choose the list of every name that fits a marked quadrilateral.

**Flag.** The only wording the generator produced has the answer "quadrilateral" alone, and no choice list anywhere contains "parallelogram." Any marked shape with two pairs of parallel sides therefore has no complete list to choose; either the generator only ever draws the plain quadrilateral (then the type is nearly empty) or it offers wrong "complete" lists. Add parallelogram to the lists and vary the shape; the lesson below teaches the full list and assumes that fix.

**Standard.** 5.G.B.4: "Classify two-dimensional figures in a hierarchy based on properties." 5.G.B.3 in the drop into the map.

**Foundations first.** The map (above). The marks read aloud as they first appear: one tick on each of four sides means all four are the same length; one arrowhead on two sides means those two are parallel, two arrowheads the other pair; a small square in a corner means a right angle, and a corner with only an arc is not one. The checklist card in its fixed order: 4 sides; 2 pairs of parallel sides; 4 right angles; 4 equal sides; with the names that need each set of rows beside it.

**Read.** "Every name that fits," gold on "every": not the best name, all of them. At a glance: four sides, so "quadrilateral" is in the answer whatever else is; a choice without it is gone before any mark is read.

**The example.** A rhombus that is not a square, no side horizontal, every side one tick, two pairs of parallel arrows, arcs on the corners, no square-corner marks. (1) Walk: 4 sides; row 0 ✓; quadrilateral lights. (2) Arrows: two pairs; row 1 ✓; parallelogram lights. (3) Right angles: no squares in any corner; the ruler is laid in one corner and the side sticks out past the arm, obtuse; row 2 ✗; rectangle and square stay dark. (4) Ticks: all four match; row 3 ✓; rhombus lights. (5) The shape shrinks and drops into the rhombus region; the regions around it are read outward: rhombus, parallelogram, quadrilateral. That is the list. (6) Morph beat, said as the point: drag two corners until the arcs become squares; row 2 flips to ✓, no row loses its check, and the shape slides into the squares overlap. "A square passes every list. It is a rhombus, a rectangle, a parallelogram, a quadrilateral."

**Quick second case (the app's variant).** A plain quadrilateral: no arrows, no squares, no matching ticks. Row 0 ✓, rows 1 to 3 ✗. The shape drops into the outer ring. The list is one name: quadrilateral. "A longer list is not a better answer. You must pass every check on it."

**Fast.** Read the marks into the four rows; every lit name is in the list.

**Sense.** The names in a true list nest: each is a region around the last. A list with rectangle but not parallelogram cannot be complete; a list with square must contain every other name.

**Check.** For each name in the chosen list, say its checks and point at the marks that pass them.

**Rule out.** Any list with a name whose row shows ✗ (square or rectangle here: no right angles) dims; any list missing a lit name dims; both reasons said.

**Line.** "A name is a list of checks. Pass them all and the name fits."

**Trap.** Calling the rhombus a square "because the sides are equal" or a "diamond"; and choosing the shortest true list ("quadrilateral") because it is safe. The ruler in the corner kills the first; the lit names on the card kill the second.

**Kit.** Marks (4.1), ruler (4.2), checklist and map with `drop` (4.3), `move` for the morph, `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest: pilot lesson; the first staging of marks, checklist, map and morph together, which the cheaper writer will imitate.

### `hierarchy-statement` — Choose the statement about groups of quadrilaterals that is always true.

**Standard.** 5.G.B.3: "attributes belonging to a category of two-dimensional figures also belong to all subcategories of that category ... squares are rectangles." 5.G.B.4 for the map.

**Foundations first.** The map. Then the one idea: "Every A is a B" is true exactly when A's region sits inside B's. Shown twice on the map: "every rectangle is a parallelogram," the rectangle region lights and it is wholly inside the lit parallelogram region; then the reverse, "every parallelogram is a rectangle," and the part of the parallelogram region that sticks out of the rectangle region lights with its leaning example in it. "One shape that fits the first word and breaks the second, and 'always' is gone. That shape is called a counterexample." Define it in those words; it is the only term card.

**Read.** "Always true," gold on always. The four statements each name an A and a B. At a glance: find A on the map; if B's region is smaller than A's, the statement runs the wrong way and is dead.

**The example.** "Which statement is always true? Every quadrilateral is a rectangle. Every quadrilateral is a square. Every parallelogram is a rectangle. Every square is a quadrilateral." (1) Statement 1: A is the whole map, B is a region inside it; the example in the outer ring is a quadrilateral and not a rectangle. Counterexample drawn beside the statement: a quadrilateral with no parallel sides. Dead. (2) Statement 2: same ring, same shape. Dead, same reason, said in one breath. (3) Statement 3: the leaning parallelogram in the part of its region outside rectangles: four sides, parallel pairs, no right angle. Dead. (4) Statement 4: the square region sits inside quadrilaterals; everything with a name on this map has four sides. The square's example walks: four sides. True. Boxed.

**Fast.** Point at A and B on the map. Inside: true. Not inside: picture the most lopsided A you can.

**Sense.** The true statement is the one that goes from a small group to a bigger one; it sounds almost too easy, and that is what "always" buys.

**Check.** Try to draw a square that is not a quadrilateral: impossible, a square has four sides. Read the chosen statement aloud.

**Rule out.** Every statement whose B region is smaller than its A region dims together, in one beat, with its counterexample named: "a leaning parallelogram breaks all three."

**Line.** "One shape that breaks it, and 'always' is gone."

**Trap.** Reading "every quadrilateral is a rectangle" and picturing a rectangle, which is a quadrilateral, so it feels true. The lesson pictures the lopsided one instead, every time, on the map's own example.

**Kit.** Map (4.3) with region `highlight` and the outside-part light; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest: a judge-it type; the counterexample chosen is the quality.

### `inherited-property` — Choose the shape that must have a property of a bigger group.

**Flag.** The answer is "square" in three of four observed wordings. A child who learns "pick square" is right most of the time without the idea. Vary the answer (rhombus, rectangle, parallelogram all inherit from larger groups).

**Standard.** 5.G.B.3, exactly its example: "all rectangles have four right angles and squares are rectangles, so all squares have four right angles."

**Foundations first.** The map. Then the pin: a property written on a region's edge lights that region and every region inside it, and nothing outside. "Whatever a group promises, every member of its subgroups has. A shape outside the region may happen to have it, but it does not have to."

**Read.** "Every rhombus has 4 sides of equal length. Which shape must also have 4 sides of equal length?" Gold on must. At a glance: the answer is a region inside the named group's region; the biggest group on the card (quadrilateral) can never be the answer, and a group beside the named one (rectangle beside rhombus) can be the answer only if it is inside, which it is not.

**The example.** Choices: parallelogram, kite, rectangle, square. (1) "4 equal sides" pinned to the rhombus region; it lights, and the squares overlap inside it lights. Nothing else. (2) Parallelogram: its region is bigger; the leaning example in the lit-less part has two long sides and two short. Not must. (3) Kite: outside the parallelogram region; its example has two short sides and two long. A kite could have four equal sides, and then it would be a rhombus; could is not must. (4) Rectangle: beside, not inside; the 2 by 5 example. Not must. (5) Square: inside the rhombus region, lit. Every square passes the rhombus list, so every square has four equal sides. Boxed.

**Quick second case.** "Every parallelogram has 2 pairs of parallel sides. Which must also: rhombus, trapezoid, kite, quadrilateral." Pin on parallelograms; rhombus lights inside it; quadrilateral is the whole map (bigger: the ring's example fails); trapezoid and kite outside. Rhombus.

**Fast.** Find the named group; the answer is a region inside it.

**Sense.** The answer always has a longer list than the group named, never shorter.

**Check.** Say the answer's own list and find the property in it (a square's list contains "4 equal sides"). For each dimmed choice, name the one shape that lacks the property.

**Rule out.** The biggest group on the card dims at once ("must? the lopsided one says no"); groups outside or beside the named region dim with their example.

**Line.** "Whatever a group promises, every group inside it has."

**Trap.** Picking the biggest group because "it includes everything," reversing the direction; and picking rectangle for rhombus because "rectangles are like squares." The lit regions make direction visible; the 2 by 5 rectangle sits unlit.

**Kit.** Map with `pin` and nested `highlight` (4.3); `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest for the first; a cheaper writer could take the variants after, since the staging is one pin and one lit region.

### `quadrilateral-hierarchy-multiselect` — Choose all 3 statements about quadrilaterals that are always true.

**Standard.** 5.G.B.3 and 5.G.B.4; the statements are properties inherited down the hierarchy and claims that run against it.

**Foundations first.** The map, the pin, and the counterexample defined as in `hierarchy-statement`. One new line: two kinds of true statement live here. "Every A is a B" when A is inside B; and "A with the extra check X is a C" when A's list plus X is C's list (rhombus plus right angles is the square's list).

**Read.** "Choose the 3 statements that are always true ... A shape may belong to more than one category." Gold on 3 and always. Six statements; each is judged on its own and exactly three survive. At a glance: a statement that goes from a big region to a smaller one, or sideways between rectangle and rhombus, is dead; a statement that goes from a small region outward, or adds a check, is alive.

**The example.** The six statements. One rectangle, 2 by 5, drawn once in rust and reused; it is the counterexample for two statements. (1) "Any quadrilateral with 2 pairs of parallel sides must have 4 equal sides": that is "every parallelogram is a rhombus"; the 2 by 5 rectangle is a parallelogram with unequal sides. Dead. (2) "Rectangles have opposite sides of equal length": rectangles sit inside parallelograms; "opposite sides equal" is a parallelogram promise, pinned, lit down into rectangles. True. (3) "Every quadrilateral belongs to the parallelogram group": the ring's kite-like example. Dead. (4) "Being a rectangle guarantees a parallelogram": rectangle inside parallelogram on the map. True. (5) "Any rhombus with 4 right angles must also be a square": the rhombus checklist with row 2 flipped to ✓ is all four rows; that is the square's list; on the map the rhombus morphs into the overlap. True. (6) "Every rectangle has 4 sides of equal length": the same 2 by 5 rectangle. Dead. Three true: 2, 4, 5. Count matches.

**Fast.** For each: find the start shape on the map; outward or adding a check: true; inward or sideways: find the lopsided one.

**Sense.** The three true statements all point from a smaller group toward a bigger promise or complete a list; none of them makes a big group do something extra.

**Check.** Count the chosen: three. For each dimmed statement, point at the drawn shape that breaks it.

**Rule out.** The three inward or sideways statements dim together with the two counterexamples shown (the 2 by 5 rectangle; the no-parallel quadrilateral).

**Line.** "One shape that breaks it, and 'always' is gone."

**Trap.** Statement 1 reads like a definition and sounds true; statement 6 is "squares are rectangles" said backwards. Both die to the same drawn rectangle, held on screen for both.

**Kit.** Map, checklist, `dim`; the problem card with six sentence choices in smaller type (exists).

**Writer.** Strongest: six judgments, two kinds of truth, the counterexample reused on purpose.

### `classify-triangle-by-sides` — Name a triangle as scalene, isosceles or equilateral by its equal sides.

**Flag.** Judged to serve 2.G.A.1, which names no triangle kinds; it is 4.G.A.2/5.G.B.4 classification by properties and sits in the right skill, wrong link. Its text defines isosceles as "exactly two sides equal" inside the skill whose idea is that lists nest; under the inclusive convention the family recommends, equilateral is isosceles. Until the app chooses, the problem states its rule in parentheses and the lesson reads that parenthesis as part of the question. Also: the lengths variant offers "Right triangle (one square corner)," an angle name among side names; if the generator ever draws an isosceles right triangle, two choices fit. Check it.

**Standard.** 5.G.B.4 (classify by properties); relink.

**Foundations first.** Tick marks: sides with the same mark are the same length; a side with no mark claims nothing. "Equal" means same length, not same look. Then the three names, each with a to-scale triangle drawn in an unfamiliar position: scalene (no two equal, three different marks or none), isosceles (two equal, one tick on each), equilateral (all three, one tick on each). The names are new words at this grade; said and shown, no term card needed beyond the figures.

**Read.** The parenthesis after each name is the rule: "exactly two," "all three," "no two." Gold on the asked kind. At a glance: count the ticked sides on each figure, nothing else; a triangle with a tick on every side is not "exactly two."

**The example.** "Which triangle is isosceles (exactly two equal sides)?" Four triangles, lettered, to scale, none pointing up. A: three sides, three different marks (scalene). B: all three sides one tick (equilateral). C: two sides one tick, the third unmarked, tall and thin, lying on its long side. D: a right triangle, no equal sides. (1) Read the rule: exactly two. (2) A: ticks differ; zero equal. (3) B: three equal; "all three, not exactly two." (4) C: two sides share a tick; the third does not. Exactly two. (5) D: no matching marks. C.

**Quick second case (lengths, no figure given).** "Sides 6 cm, 6 cm, 6 cm": the kit draws it to scale; three equal; equilateral; "Right triangle" is an angle word and the equal sides give 60° corners, no square corner.

**Fast.** Count the sides that share a mark (or a length); 0, 2, 3 is the name.

**Sense.** A triangle with two equal sides has two equal corners opposite them; the drawn figure shows it.

**Check.** Count the ticks again starting from a different side; for lengths, pair them up.

**Rule out.** Triangles with the wrong count of matching marks dim together; the angle-name choice dims for being about corners, not sides.

**Line.** "A tick says equal. No tick, no claim."

**Trap.** Calling the equilateral triangle isosceles because "two of its sides are equal." Under the problem's stated rule it is not; the lesson reads the parenthesis aloud and says once that some books count it as isosceles, and the problem tells you which rule it uses.

**Kit.** Marks (4.1); `dim`. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper writer, with the convention note copied verbatim.

## Geometry: geometric-definitions (HSG.CO.A.1) and polygon-symmetries (HSG.CO.A.3)

### `spherical-geometry-compare` — How lines, triangles and shortest paths on a sphere differ from the plane.

**Flag.** Serves no Common Core standard; it is Texas TEKS G.4D. Keep it, mark it state-specific in the catalog, and build the sphere picture (4.8) for it alone. Four questions under one name; the lesson is a foundation plus four short examples.

**Foundations first.** What a line is, on the plane and on the sphere, built not asserted. On the plane: the straightest path, going on forever. On the sphere: pull a string tight between two points on a globe; it lies along the biggest circle the sphere allows, a circle whose center is the sphere's center: a great circle. The equator is one; a circle of latitude is not (its center is above the sphere's center, and a string along it goes slack when pulled; the tight string bows toward the pole). "On a sphere, a line is a great circle." Then the one fact everything hangs on: two great circles are cut by two planes through the center; two planes through one point meet in a line through that point, which pierces the sphere at two opposite points. So any two lines on the sphere cross, twice. Shown: the equator teal; a second great circle blue, tilted; it crosses the equator at two opposite points; the blue circle spins to every tilt and the two crossings never leave.

**Read.** Each question says "on a sphere, a line is a great circle" and asks one comparison. At a glance: on the sphere, anything that depends on two lines never meeting is gone.

**The example (parallels).** "Given a line and a point not on it, how many lines through the point are parallel, in the plane and on the sphere?" Plane: one, drawn. Sphere: a point off the equator; every great circle through it is tilted, and the spin showed every tilted one crosses the equator. None. "Plane: exactly one. Sphere: none."

**Three quick cases, same picture.** Shortest path: two points on the same latitude; the latitude arc (rust) and the great-circle arc (teal) drawn; a string pulled tight slides from the rust onto the teal; the shorter arc of the great circle, not the longer one, not the line through the inside (not on the surface). Right angles: north pole and two equator points a quarter turn apart; the triangle's three corners are each a right angle (a meridian meets the equator squarely; the two meridians meet at the pole at 90°). Plane: one at most (two would use up 180°). Sphere: three. Angle sum: that triangle's sum is 270, more than 180; a sphere's triangle always has more than 180 (and less than 540), and the bigger it is the more. So which could be a spherical triangle's angles: 60, 60, 60 sums to exactly 180: plane; 40, 40, 40 and 30, 40, 50 sum below 180: nothing; 120, 120, 120 sums to 360: yes.

**Fast.** Parallels: none. Shortest: the shorter great-circle arc. Right angles: three. Angle sum: more than 180.

**Sense.** Everything strange about the sphere comes from one fact: lines cross twice.

**Check.** For the angle-sum question, add the three angles; more than 180 and less than 540 or it is out. For parallels, tilt the second great circle in your head and watch it cross.

**Rule out.** Any choice built on a sphere line never meeting another dims; any angle triple summing to 180 or less dims together; any path leaving the surface dims.

**Line.** "On a sphere, lines are great circles, and any two of them cross twice."

**Trap.** Taking a circle of latitude for a line, because on a map it is straight; the slack string shows it. Answering "infinitely many" parallels through the pole because meridians look parallel at the equator; they meet at the pole on screen.

**Kit.** 4.8: a sphere with great circles that tilt and spin, a latitude circle, points, surface arcs in two inks, a shaded spherical triangle with right-angle marks, and a taut-string motion (an arc that slides from one path to the shorter one).

**Writer.** Strongest: the picture has to be invented and the reasoning staged, not described.

### `precise-geometric-definitions` — Choose the precise definition, or find the statement missing a condition.

**Standard.** HSG.CO.A.1: "Know precise definitions of angle, circle, perpendicular line, parallel line, and line segment, based on the undefined notions of point, line, distance along a line, and distance around a circular arc." Check that line segment and perpendicular line appear as asked objects; the observed wordings ask circle, angle, parallel lines, and a reversed question.

**Foundations first.** The opening line is the standard's: point, line and distance are not defined; everything else is built from them in a sentence that lets in exactly the right things. "A definition keeps out everything it should." The test, done once before the example: take a candidate sentence and ask what else it fits; draw the intruder. "A circle is a closed curve with no corners": an egg shape fits. Dead on sight.

**Read.** "Which is the precise definition of a circle?" Gold on precise. At a glance: a sentence using a word that is not itself defined ("space between," "distance between two lines") is not a definition at all; a sentence that correctly defines a different object is a decoy to read past.

**The example.** The circle, four choices. (1) "A closed curve with no corners": the egg. Out. (2) "The set of all points that are a given distance from a given point": no "in a plane"; in space, those points are a sphere, drawn. Out. (3) "The set of all points in a plane that are at most a given distance from a given point": "at most" lets in the inside; the filled disc, drawn. Out. (4) "The set of all points in a plane that are a given distance from a given point": every condition needed (in a plane, exactly that distance), nothing extra. Built live: a point, a distance, the plane, the points at that distance drawn in. Boxed.

**Quick second cases.** Parallel lines: "do not intersect" without "in a plane" fits two edges of a box that never meet and are not parallel (skew), drawn on a box. The reversed question ("exactly one is not a precise definition"): the ray sentence is precise, the perpendicular sentence is precise, the parallel sentence has its plane; "an angle is the space between two lines" uses an undefined word and two lines, not two rays sharing an endpoint. That is the odd one. And the angle question's decoy: a true definition of a ray; read what is asked.

**Fast.** For each sentence, name one shape it wrongly lets in. The one you cannot do that for is the definition.

**Sense.** The precise one is often the longest, because each condition keeps something out; "shorter and cleaner" is not a reason.

**Check.** Build the chosen definition from its words and get only the object named; remove any one condition and name what gets in.

**Rule out.** Sentences with undefined words ("space," "distance between lines") dim together as non-definitions; a correct definition of the wrong object dims when the asked word is reread.

**Line.** "A definition keeps out everything it should."

**Trap.** Preferring the shorter sentence; and choosing the familiar true sentence about a ray when an angle was asked. The intruder drawn for the short sentence, and the gold asked word, handle both.

**Kit.** Figures only: a sphere outline beside a circle, a filled disc, a box with skew edges. `dim`. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Strongest: the quality is the intruder chosen for each wrong sentence.

### `mcas-rotational-symmetry` — Which figure maps onto itself in a turn of less than a full circle.

**Flag.** The problem says "which figure" and shows only names; the lesson draws each named shape so the spin can be shown.

**Standard.** HSG.CO.A.3: "describe the rotations ... that carry it onto itself."

**Foundations first.** The spin, defined in motion: a muted ghost stays; a copy with one gold corner turns about the center; where it lands exactly on the ghost, the outline glows and a count ticks. "Rotational symmetry: some turn smaller than a full circle lands the shape on itself." Shown once on a square (lands at a quarter turn), once on a scalene triangle (never lands until 360). Then the one fact that makes the type fast: a quadrilateral that lands at all lands at the half turn, so test the half turn; a triangle lands only at a third turn, which needs all three sides equal, so only the equilateral triangle qualifies. Said over the spin, not as a rule.

**Read.** "Which figure has rotational symmetry?" or "does not have"; gold on has or not. At a glance: every triangle on the card except an equilateral one is out; every shape in the parallelogram family and every regular polygon is in.

**The example.** "Which has rotational symmetry: a scalene triangle, a rhombus that is not a square, an isosceles triangle that is not equilateral, a right triangle with unequal legs." Each drawn to scale. (1) Three triangles, none equilateral: spin the scalene one; the gold corner never meets a corner until it returns. The other two the same, in one beat. (2) The rhombus: center at the crossing of its diagonals; half turn; each corner lands on the opposite corner; gold outline. The rhombus.

**Second example (fully worked; the negated question and the look-alike).** "Which does not have rotational symmetry: a square, an isosceles trapezoid, a regular hexagon, a rectangle that is not a square." (1) Square and hexagon: regular, land every quarter and every sixth turn. (2) Rectangle: half turn lands, long side on long side. (3) Isosceles trapezoid: it has a fold line down its middle, so it looks symmetric; the half turn puts the long base where the short one was; overhang in rust, held. It never lands before a full turn. The trapezoid.

**Fast.** Parallelogram family or regular polygon: yes. Non-parallelogram trapezoid, kite, non-equilateral triangle: no.

**Sense.** A shape whose opposite sides are parallel and equal swaps them in a half turn; a trapezoid's two parallel sides differ, so no turn can swap them.

**Check.** Spin the chosen shape and watch it land; spin one of the dimmed ones and watch the overhang.

**Rule out.** All non-equilateral triangles dim in one beat with one reason; in the "does not" question, the regular polygons and the parallelogram family dim together.

**Line.** "Turn it until it lands. A fold line is not a turn."

**Trap.** The isosceles trapezoid "is symmetric, so it has rotational symmetry"; a leaning parallelogram (in other variants) "has none because it has no fold line." Both spins are shown; the parallelogram lands at the half turn, the trapezoid never does.

**Kit.** `spin` with ghost and overhang (4.4); `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest: non-regular shapes and two look-alike mistakes in opposite directions.

### `polygon-symmetry-counts` — The turns or flips that carry a polygon onto itself, counted.

**Standard.** HSG.CO.A.3: "Given a rectangle, parallelogram, trapezoid, or regular polygon, describe the rotations and reflections that carry it onto itself." Observed variants cover regular polygons, the rectangle's rotations and its reflections; the parallelogram and trapezoid are never asked and should be added (family design, section 6).

**Foundations first.** The spin from `mcas-rotational-symmetry`, now counted. A regular polygon with n sides, turned slowly through a full circle with one gold corner: it lands once for every corner, n times, the last landing being the start again. So the smallest turn is 360 ÷ n, the landings are its multiples, and 0° (the shape sitting still) is one of them when the question counts it. The fold from grade 4, in one line: a line of reflection is a fold line, and for a polygon it must run through the center and through a corner or the middle of a side.

**Read.** Three asks: list the turns greater than 0 and less than 360; count the turns at least 0 and less than 360 (0° counts, gold); the smallest positive turn. And one ask about lines. At a glance: the answer for a regular n-gon is built from 360 ÷ n; a rectangle is not regular, so 360 ÷ 4 does not apply to it.

**The example.** "Which rotations greater than 0° and less than 360° carry a square onto itself?" (1) Center at the diagonals' crossing; gold corner at the top. (2) Slow turn; landing at 90°, tick; 180°, tick; 270°, tick; 360° is back at the start. (3) 360 ÷ 4 = 90 written as what the spin showed; the list is 90, 180, 270. (4) Check: 4 × 90 = 360.

**Second case, worked (the non-regular shape, both halves of the standard).** A 2 by 5 rectangle. Spin: at 90° the long side lies where the short one was, overhang in rust; at 180° it lands; at 270° overhang again. Only 180°. Lines: fold through the middles of the long sides, lands; through the middles of the short sides, lands; a diagonal, overhang held. Two lines. "Not regular, so 360 ÷ 4 is not its number. Watch it turn."

**Quick case (the count).** "How many rotations at least 0° and less than 360° for a regular 9-gon; count the 0° rotation." One landing per corner, nine, the 0° landing first. 360 ÷ 9 = 40 is the step, not the count; 8 forgot 0°; 18 doubled.

**Fast.** Regular n-gon: smallest turn 360 ÷ n; turns are its multiples below 360; count n with 0°, n − 1 without; n fold lines. Anything else: spin and fold it.

**Sense.** More sides, smaller smallest turn, more landings. A rectangle lands fewer times than a square because it is less symmetric.

**Check.** Multiply back: n × (360 ÷ n) = 360. For a listed set, the turns are equally spaced and the next one would be 360.

**Rule out.** For a regular polygon, a list whose spacing is not 360 ÷ n dims; a count equal to the step angle (40) dims as the wrong kind of number; for the rectangle the 90°-step list dims when the quarter turn shows its overhang.

**Line.** "Turn it until it lands. A regular shape with n sides lands n times."

**Trap.** Leaving out the 0° turn when it is asked for; treating the rectangle as if regular (90, 180, 270) and counting its diagonals as fold lines. The spin ticks 0° first; the rectangle's quarter turn and diagonal fold both show rust.

**Kit.** `spin` with `landings` and count (4.4), `fold`; `dim`.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper writer once `mcas-rotational-symmetry` has set the staging; the examples and decoys are fully specified here.
