# Shapes and their attributes, part 1: the per-type concepts (K to 2)

Ten types across `name-shapes`, `compose-shapes`, `shape-attributes` and `polygon-properties`. Each stands on the family design: the marked figure, the walk from a start dot, the turn that keeps the count, the card corner as the square-corner ruler, and choices dimming together by count. Two things hold for all ten and are not repeated below: at K to 2 nothing is struck out, the voice says what to do instead; and "corner" is the word, with "vertex" given once as its other name where the problem uses it.

A flag that applies to the whole K group before any one type: `name-flat-shape` and `find-named-shape` practice K.G.A.2's "regardless of their orientations or overall size" only if the generator draws turned squares, tall rectangles and triangles pointing down. The lessons below correct that misconception on screen; the app should test it too (section 6 of the design).

---

## Name shapes (K)

### `name-flat-shape` — Name a flat shape.
**Standard**: K.G.A.2, "Correctly name shapes regardless of their orientations or overall size." Also K.G.B.4's "parts (e.g., number of sides and vertices/'corners')," since counting is how the name is found. The only wording seen offers four polygon names; the app's method also names the circle, so the lesson covers it in one beat.

**Foundations first** (about 30 seconds, drawn live): a flat shape lies on the page and a finger can trace all of it. A triangle is drawn corner by corner, pointing down. *Side*: one straight part of the outline. *Corner*: where two sides meet. *Closed*: the outline comes back to where it started. Then the walk: a gold dot on one corner, each side lights as it is counted, "one, two, three," and the walk stops when it reaches the dot. Corners get numbered dots: three. "Three sides, three corners." The names as counts: 3 triangle; 4 rectangle, or square when all four sides match; 6 hexagon. A circle drawn beside: no straight sides, no corners, nothing to walk.

**Read**: "What is the name of this shape?" At a glance: if it is round with no corners, circle; otherwise the count is the name, and only a count of 4 needs one more look.

**The example**: an obtuse triangle, long side on top, pointing down, tilted; nothing about it looks like the triangle a child draws. Choices: hexagon | rectangle | square | triangle. (1) Gold start dot on the top-left corner. (2) Walk: "one," the top side lights gold and settles teal with its 1; "two"; "three," and the walk arrives at the dot: "back at the start, stop." (3) Corners: dots 1, 2, 3. (4) "Three sides, three corners. Triangle." (5) The turn: the shape rotates until it points up, its numbers riding along. "Turn it. Still three sides. Still a triangle." A copy shrinks beside it: "Smaller. Still a triangle." (6) Triangle.

**Quick second case** (4 sides, where the count does not finish it): a tall rectangle, slightly tilted. Walk: 4. The corner of a card slides into each corner and fits: four square corners. A short side slides to lie along a long side and stops short: not all the same. "Rectangle. If all four sides matched, square." Three beats.

**Fast**: round, circle; otherwise walk; a 4 means check the sides.
**Sense**: sides and corners come out equal; if they don't, count again.
**Check**: count again from a different corner.
**Rule out**: after the walk, every name with another count dims together: "we counted three; hexagon, rectangle and square are other counts: gone."
**Line**: "Turn it. Still three sides. Still a triangle."
**Trap**: a triangle "must point up," a tall rectangle is "a door," a turned square is "a diamond." The worked shape points down and turns with its numbers riding; nothing about how it sits is ever said as a reason.
**Kit**: 4.1 (walk, addressable sides and corners), 4.7 (dim), the 4.2 ruler drawn and named as "the corner of a card," and a side that slides to lie along another (a `seg` copy on `slide`; exists).
**Writer**: strongest. This is the pilot; it sets the K voice and the walk's staging that the cheaper writer imitates.

### `find-named-shape` — Pick the shape with the given name.
**Standard**: K.G.A.2, same clause; K.G.B.4, "in different sizes and orientations." A pick-it type: the picture is a lettered row of shapes at one scale.

**Foundations first**: what each name needs, said as a short list over a small to-scale example: triangle, 3 sides; rectangle, 4 sides and 4 square corners; square, that and all 4 sides the same; hexagon, 6 sides; circle, round with no corners. Then the rule of the type: "Choosing means checking each one. Never pick by look."

**Read**: "Which shape is a square?" The name is gold. At a glance: any shape whose side count is wrong is gone before anything else; a circle is the one with no corners at all.

**The example**: "Which shape is a square?" A a triangle; B a small square turned so a corner points up; C a tall rectangle; D a rhombus, leaning, all sides equal, no square corners. (1) The square's list, written beside the row: 4 sides, 4 square corners, all sides the same. (2) Walk A: 3. A dims: "three sides is a triangle." (3) B, C and D each walk to 4. (4) The card corner slides into D's corners and does not fit: D's corners are not square corners. D dims. (5) C: the card fits all four corners; a short side slides along a long side and stops short: not all the same. C dims. (6) B: the card fits all four corners; each side slides onto the next and matches. Then B turns upright: "the same square. Turned does not change it, and small does not change it." B.

**Fast**: count first; then square corners; then equal sides. A shape leaves at its first failed check.
**Sense**: exactly one shape is left.
**Check**: say the name's list and point to each item on the chosen shape.
**Rule out**: by count, all at once, before any other check.
**Line**: "Count what it has, not how it sits."
**Trap**: the turned square is rejected as "a diamond"; the rhombus is accepted as a square because it is "a diamond" too. Both decisions come from look; the card corner and the side match are the only reasons the lesson gives.
**Kit**: 4.1, 4.2 (the ruler as a card corner), 4.7 with drawn targets, `slide`.
**Writer**: capable cheaper writer, from the pilot's staging; the design names this as the first K test of the cheaper writer, and an independent reviewer samples it first.

### `name-solid-shape` — Name a solid.
**Standard**: K.G.A.2; and K.G.A.3, "Identify shapes as two-dimensional (lying in a plane, 'flat') or three-dimensional ('solid')," which this type is the only one to touch, through the "circle" decoy. Keep that decoy: it is the standard.

**Foundations first**: flat and solid, side by side: a circle drawn on paper, traced by a finger, flat; a can standing on a table line, with a front and sides you can see, solid. *Face*: a flat part of a solid. *Curved surface*: the part that rolls. *Tip*: a point. Then the four solids stand in a row, named one at a time with what each has: sphere, round all over, no flat face; cone, one circle face and a tip; cylinder, two circle faces with a curved surface between; cube, six square faces, all the same.

**Read**: "What is the name of this solid shape?" "Solid" is gold: the answer is a solid's name, so a flat name cannot be right. At a glance: round all over, sphere; a tip, cone; otherwise count the flat faces and see their shape.

**The example**: a cylinder standing on the table. Choices: cone | cube | cylinder | circle. (1) "A circle is flat. This stands up. Circle is gone." It dims first, alone, for that reason. (2) Lift: the cylinder rises and leaves a teal circle on the table where it stood. "That is where a circle is: it is the bottom face." The top face lights: two circle faces. (3) The curved surface between them is traced around. (4) No tip, so not a cone; no square faces, so not a cube; both dim with their reasons. (5) Cylinder. Quick beat: the cylinder rolls onto its side. "Still two circle faces. Still a cylinder."

**Fast**: round all over, sphere; one circle and a tip, cone; two circles, cylinder; squares, cube.
**Sense**: a solid gets a solid's name.
**Check**: count the flat faces: two, both circles.
**Rule out**: the flat word first; then tip; then face shape.
**Line**: "Lift it. The shape it stood on is one of its faces."
**Trap**: the cube is "a square," the sphere "a circle," the cylinder "a circle." The lift shows the circle as one face of the solid, not the solid.
**Kit**: 4.5 (cylinder, cone, sphere, cube; stand, lift, face highlight), a roll for the orientation beat (`turn`).
**Writer**: capable cheaper writer, once the `solid-parts` pilot has shown that solids render well.

## Compose shapes (K)

### `compose-shape-name` — Name the shape the pieces make.
**Standard**: K.G.B.6, "Compose simple shapes to form larger shapes. For example, 'Can you join these two triangles with full sides touching to make a rectangle?'" The standard's own example is the worked example. K.G.A.2 for the naming. (1.G.A.2's trapezoids, half-circles and solids are never composed anywhere; section 6.)

**Foundations first**: "put together with full sides touching, no gaps or overlaps" shown as a motion: two pieces slide until one whole side lies along one whole side; a version with a gap and one with an overlap show in rust what the words forbid. *Seam*: the line where the pieces touch; it is inside. *Outline*: the outside; the name belongs to the outline. "Skip the seams. Walk the outside."

**Read**: "These 2 triangles are put together... What shape do they make?" "shape do they make" is gold: the answer names the new outline, not the pieces. At a glance: the pieces' name is not the answer by itself.

**The example**: two right triangles, teal and rust, joined long side to long side into a tall rectangle. Choices: square | triangle | hexagon | rectangle. (1) Apart: each walked, three corners each. "Six corners in all?" said as the question a child asks. (2) Join: the rust triangle turns a half turn and slides until the long sides touch; the seam fades. (3) Walk the outline from a dot: one, two, three, four, back at the dot. Four corners dotted. At each end of the seam two piece-corners have become one corner. (4) The card corner fits all four. A short side slides along a long side and stops short. (5) Rectangle.

**Quick second case**: four small squares make one big square. The walk goes straight past the point where two pieces meet on a side: "no bend, no corner." Four sides that all match: square. Three beats.

**Fast**: join, let the seam go, walk the outside.
**Sense**: the outline has fewer corners than the pieces had together.
**Check**: trace the outline with a finger without crossing a seam; recount from another corner.
**Rule out**: by the walk's count: triangle and hexagon dim together ("six is the pieces' corners, not the shape's"). Rectangle against square by the side match.
**Line**: "Skip the seams. Walk the outside."
**Trap**: adding the pieces' corners (3 + 3 = 6, so "hexagon"); counting a point where two pieces meet on a straight side as a corner. The seam fades before the walk, and the walk does not bend there.
**Kit**: 4.6 `join`, 4.1 walk; `slide`, `turn` exist.
**Writer**: strongest. Staging a fit so the seam's corners visibly merge is the lesson; a weaker author describes it.

### `complete-the-shape` — Pick the piece that fills the gap.
**Standard**: K.G.B.6, composing; the gap is the missing piece.

**Foundations first**: the gap has a shape of its own. Its outline is traced dashed, its sides counted, long and short named. "A piece may be turned": a piece turned around is the same piece. A piece that fits touches every side of the gap and sticks out nowhere.

**Read**: "Which piece fills the empty space to complete the hexagon? A piece may be turned." At a glance: a piece with a different number of sides than the gap is gone; the rest are tried.

**The example**: a regular hexagon with its lower half missing; the upper half, a trapezoid, in place. Choices: A a trapezoid drawn upside down relative to the gap, the right size; B a rectangle as wide as the gap; C a trapezoid the right shape but too small. (1) Trace the gap: four sides, the long seam on top, a short side below, two slanted. (2) B slides in: its two bottom corners stick out past the slanted sides; the overhang fills rust and holds. "It sticks out. Not this one." B dims. (3) C slides in: it touches the seam but leaves rust gaps at the slanted sides. "Too small. There is still empty space." C dims. (4) A: four sides, long and short in the right places. It turns a half turn, slides in, and every edge glows gold. The outline is walked: six. A.

**Fast**: count the gap's sides; match long to long and short to short; turn and drop it in.
**Sense**: the finished shape is the one the question named, with nothing left empty and nothing sticking out.
**Check**: walk the completed outline.
**Rule out**: wrong side count dims at once; the rest are tried, and each failure is seen.
**Line**: "The gap is a shape too. Count its sides."
**Trap**: rejecting the right piece because it points the wrong way; accepting a piece that is the right shape but the wrong size. The turn and the rust gap.
**Kit**: 4.6 (`join` noting gap and overlap, drawn in rust and held), 4.1, `slide`, `turn`.
**Writer**: strongest, for the same reason as `compose-shape-name`.

### `decompose-flat-shape` — Name the pieces a cut makes.
**Standard**: K.G.B.4 ("parts") and K.G.A.2; it is K.G.B.6 run backwards. Flags: "equal parts" is 1.G.A.3's wording; here it only means the pieces are all the same, which the lesson checks by sliding one onto another. And if the generator never cuts corner to corner, the "2 triangles" choice can never be right; either cut that way sometimes or drop the choice.

**Foundations first**: a *cut* is a straight line across a shape; it makes *pieces*, and each piece is a shape of its own, walked like any shape. The question asks two things: how many, and what.

**Read**: "A rectangle is cut into equal parts. What are the new shapes?" At a glance: count the pieces; every choice with another number is gone before the shape is named.

**The example**: a wide rectangle cut once, straight down the middle. Choices: 4 squares | 3 squares | 2 triangles | 2 rectangles. (1) The cut draws across; the pieces part by a small gap. (2) Count the pieces: 1, 2 dropped into them. (3) Walk one piece: four sides; the card corner fits each corner. The piece looks almost square, so a short side slides along a long side and stops short: not the same. "Rectangle." (4) The other piece slides onto the first and lands exactly: both the same. (5) 2 rectangles.

**Quick second case**: the same rectangle cut corner to corner. Two pieces; walk one: three sides. "2 triangles. Where the cut goes decides what the pieces are." Three beats.

**Fast**: count the pieces, walk one piece.
**Sense**: the pieces slid back together make the original.
**Check**: slide them back; the rectangle returns.
**Rule out**: by the piece count, all at once.
**Line**: "Count the pieces. Then walk one."
**Trap**: half a rectangle looks square, so it is called a square; a square's pieces are assumed to be squares. The side match and the corner-to-corner cut.
**Kit**: 4.6 `cut`, 4.1, `slide`.
**Writer**: capable cheaper writer.

## Shape attributes (grade 1)

### `count-sides-corners` — Count sides or corners.
**Standard**: K.G.B.4, "parts (e.g., number of sides and vertices/'corners')." Filed in a grade-1 skill; fine as review, and the prompt's "vertices" means the word is defined here.

**Foundations first**: *side*, *corner*, and "another word for corner is vertex; more than one are vertices," once. *Closed*: the outline comes back to the start. The walk from a start dot, stopping at the dot. Then the fact the whole type turns on, shown on the figure: every corner sits between two sides and every side between two corners, so the two counts are always the same.

**Read**: "How many sides does this shape have?" or "How many corners, or vertices?" The asked word is gold. At a glance: either answer is the other one too.

**The example**: an irregular hexagon, stretched, one side much longer than the rest, tilted; not the honeycomb a child knows by sight. (1) Gold start dot. (2) Walk: one, two, three; the long side lights as one: "a long side is still one side"; four, five, six, back at the dot. 6. (3) "How many corners?" The dots drop, 1 to 6, on the same shape: "as many corners as sides." Both wordings are covered on one figure.

**Fast**: dot the start, walk, stop at the dot.
**Sense**: sides and corners agree.
**Check**: count from a different corner; or count corners when sides were asked.
**Rule out**: open answer; none.
**Line**: "Put a dot on every corner as you count."
**Trap**: counting the first side again at the end because there was no start mark; losing the place; counting a long side as two. The dot and the number written on each counted side.
**Kit**: 4.1 only.
**Writer**: capable cheaper writer.

### `defining-attribute` — Which sentence is true for every one?
**Standard**: 1.G.A.1, "Distinguish between defining attributes (e.g., triangles are closed and three-sided) versus non-defining attributes (e.g., color, orientation, overall size)." The clause "build and draw shapes to possess defining attributes" is never practiced (section 6).

**Foundations first**: "every" means A, B and C, and every triangle anyone could ever draw. A *defining* thing is what makes it the shape: take it away and it is not a triangle any more. Then the family's central motion: a triangle's corners are dragged through tall, flat, long, pointing down, while a short card beside it keeps two lists, "stays" (3 straight sides, 3 corners, closed) and "changes" (how tall, how wide, which way it points, how big). "What stays is what makes it a triangle."

**Read**: "Shapes A, B and C are all triangles. Which sentence is true for every triangle?" "every" gold. At a glance: a sentence about how big, how tall or how it sits cannot be true of every one, because turning the shape changes it and turning never changes what a shape is.

**The example**: A a tall thin triangle; B a wide one standing on a corner, pointing down; C a small even one. Choices: All of its sides are the same length | It is taller than it is wide | It has a side that lies flat along the bottom | It has exactly 3 straight sides and 3 corners. The method, said once: try each sentence on every shape; one shape that breaks it is enough. (1) "Taller than wide": B is wider than tall. Gone. (2) "A side that lies flat along the bottom": B stands on a corner. Gone; and A turns so its flat side leaves the bottom: "turning does not change the shape, so this was never about the shape." (3) "All of its sides are the same length": C's sides slide onto each other and match: true of C; A's short side along its long side stops short. "True of C, not of every triangle." Gone. (4) "Exactly 3 straight sides and 3 corners": walk A, B, C: three, three, three. Then C morphs through every triangle while the count holds. That is the answer.

**Fast**: a sentence about size, direction or how it sits is never the one; the sentence about sides and corners is checked on all three.
**Sense**: the sentence matches the shape's name: three for a triangle.
**Check**: picture the oddest triangle you can and read the sentence about it.
**Rule out**: one shape that breaks each decoy, named; the "how it sits" decoys die together to the turn.
**Line**: "One shape that breaks it, and 'every' is gone."
**Trap**: choosing the sentence that is true of the nice even triangle, C, because that is the triangle in the child's head. The lesson checks C first and lets A break it.
**Kit**: `move` of points (exists) for the morph; a stays/changes card (a two-column `text` block or a small 4.3 card); 4.7 dim. The rectangle and hexagon variants use the same method with their own counts; if a rectangle variant includes a square-looking one, "all sides the same" is true of it and false of a long one, which is the same kill.
**Writer**: strongest. The morph with its two lists is the family's idea in one motion, and the decoys must die in the order that teaches.

## Polygon properties (grade 2)

### `name-polygon` — Name a polygon by its side count.
**Standard**: 2.G.A.1, "Identify triangles, quadrilaterals, pentagons, hexagons, and cubes." Octagon goes beyond the list; fine.

**Foundations first**: *polygon*: a closed flat shape made only of straight sides (the one `term` card). *Angle*: the opening at a corner, drawn as a small arc; one angle at every corner, so sides, corners and angles all count the same. The names as counts over a row of irregular, tilted examples: 3 triangle, 4 quadrilateral, 5 pentagon, 6 hexagon, 8 octagon. The new word gets one more beat: "quad means four; a square, a rectangle and any other four-sided shape are all quadrilaterals."

**Read**: "What kind of polygon is this?" At a glance: straight sides, so the count is the name.

**The example**: an irregular pentagon, no house shape, one corner wide and shallow, tilted. Choices: octagon | quadrilateral | triangle | pentagon. (1) Start dot; walk one to five. At the wide corner the outline bends, so it is a corner; the card corner laid in it shows the opening wider than square, and still a corner. (2) Five sides, five angles. (3) Pentagon. Quick beat: a kite-shaped four-sided figure with no square corners: "four sides. Quadrilateral. Any four sides."

**Fast**: walk, match the count.
**Sense**: sides and angles agree.
**Check**: count the angles.
**Rule out**: every name with another count dims together.
**Line**: "The name is the count."
**Trap**: a pentagon has to look like a house, so a lopsided one is miscounted; a wide, shallow corner is skipped. The walk marks every corner.
**Kit**: 4.1 with `arc` marks, 4.7, `term`.
**Writer**: capable cheaper writer.

### `polygon-with-angles` — Pick the shape with exactly n angles.
**Standard**: 2.G.A.1, "Recognize ... shapes having specified attributes, such as a given number of angles."

**Foundations first**: *angle*: the opening at a corner, an arc drawn in it; one angle per corner, no more. "Exactly 4" means four, not three, not five. Counting angles is dotting the corners.

**Read**: "Which shape has exactly 4 angles?" "exactly 4 angles" gold. At a glance: every shape is counted; look decides nothing.

**The example**: A a triangle; B a rectangle with one corner cut off, so it has five corners, two of them wide; C a slanted quadrilateral with no square corners; D a hexagon. (1) A: arcs drop at each corner, one, two, three. Dims. (2) B: one, two, three, four, five; the cut corner made two wide angles, each with its arc. "Five, not four." Dims. (3) D: six. Dims. (4) C: four arcs. "No square corners, and it needs none: four angles, exactly." C.

**Fast**: count the corners of each shape; the one whose count matches is it.
**Sense**: one shape left.
**Check**: count C's sides: four.
**Rule out**: by count, after the walks, together.
**Line**: "One angle at every corner. Count the corners."
**Trap**: the cut-corner rectangle read as four because it "looks like a rectangle"; believing only square corners are angles, so the slanted shape seems to have none. The arcs on every corner.
**Kit**: 4.1 (walk of corners with arcs), 4.7 with drawn targets.
**Writer**: capable cheaper writer.
