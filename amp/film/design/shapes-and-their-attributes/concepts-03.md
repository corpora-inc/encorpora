# Lines, angles, classifying and symmetry (`lines-symmetry`, grade 4): the concepts

Ten types below, plus `angle-classify`, which the pilot already owns. Three things first.

**The skill should be three skills** (design §6): 4.G.A.1 (`line-ray-segment`, `line-relationship`, `line-pair-select`, `angle-kind-select`, `angle-classify`), 4.G.A.2 (`classify-triangle-angles`, `triangle-sides-angles-blanks`, `quadrilateral-attributes`, `shape-sides-select`) and 4.G.A.3 (`symmetry-line-count`, `symmetry-line-check`). The concepts are written so the ruler lesson comes first and everything else names it in one line.

**Two shared openings, each about twenty seconds, drawn live.** The lines-and-angles films open with the square corner: the corner of a sheet of paper is a right angle; a gold L slides into any corner and lines one arm up with one side; the other side hides inside (acute), lies along the arm (right) or sticks out past it (obtuse). "Lay the corner of your paper in it." The symmetry films open with one fold: a shape, a dashed line, a copy lifts and flips over the line; where it lands on the ghost the outline glows, where it misses the overhang fills rust. "If the halves match, that line is a line of symmetry." Every film in this skill reads the figure's marks aloud before any work.

**Flags, collected.** `line-relationship`'s method text ("swap the two numbers and flip up and down") is the negative-reciprocal slope test, grade 8; the lesson teaches the grade-4 check (lay the square corner where the lines meet) and shows the turned step as the picture behind the swap. `triangle-sides-angles-blanks` says "exactly two sides equal" inside a skill whose idea is nesting; the blanks force one word, so the lesson says "the most exact name" and writes equilateral for three ticks. `symmetry-line-count` is open answer: no circle, ever. `line-pair-select` has no grid and says "appear"; the lesson gives a sure test anyway (slide a copy). `shape-sides-select`'s "perpendicular if made longer" is handled by extending both sides to their crossing before the ruler is laid.

---

### `line-ray-segment` — Line, ray or segment

**Standard.** 4.G.A.1: "Draw points, lines, line segments, rays ... Identify these in two-dimensional figures." The identify clause. The app never asks the child to draw; the lesson draws all three so the standard's first verb happens on screen at least once.

**Foundations first.** A point: a dot that marks one spot, named with a capital letter; W and X appear. A straight path through both. Now the ends, because the ends are the whole lesson. Two dots and nothing beyond them: the path stops at both ends: a **line segment**, segment WX, and it has a length you could measure. Replace the dot at X with an arrowhead: the path keeps going past X and never stops: a **ray**, named from its stopping end first, ray WX (ray XW would stop at X; the lesson says this once). Replace the dot at W too: it never stops either way: a **line**, line WX or line XW. Three figures built from the same two points; the only thing that changed is the ends. The small bar and arrows written over the letters are shown once beside each name since tests use them; the app's choices are words.

**Read.** "Which name matches this figure?" The letters say nothing about the kind; only the ends do. At a glance: count arrowheads. None, one, two.

**The example.** A ray slanting down to the left: dot at W upper right, arrowhead past X lower left, so the arrow is not where a child expects an "end" to be. (1) Left end: an arrowhead: it keeps going. (2) Right end: a dot: it stops. (3) One stop, one arrow: a ray. (4) Name from the stop: ray WX. The figure then stretches longer on screen: the ends have not changed, still a ray.

**Fast.** Count the arrowheads: 0 segment, 1 ray, 2 line. Always works.
**Sense.** A segment is the only one you could measure with a ruler; a line is the only one that runs off both sides of the page.
**Check.** Say the chosen name's meaning while looking at each end: "ray: stops at W, keeps going past X."
**Rule out.** Two of the three choices die on the arrow count at once; the lesson says so and dims them together.
**Line.** "Look at the ends. A dot stops. An arrow keeps going."
**Trap.** Naming by length: a long stroke "is a line," a short one "a segment." The stretch beat kills it. A smaller one: reading the arrowhead as the figure's end and the dot as decoration; the opening builds the arrow as "keeps going."
**Kit.** `seg` with `ends` (4.2); `move` to stretch. Nothing more.
**Writer.** Cheaper: one check, one tool, the concept names the example and the stretch.

### `line-relationship` — Parallel, perpendicular or neither, on a dot grid

**Standard.** 4.G.A.1: "perpendicular and parallel lines. Identify these in two-dimensional figures." Flag: the app's method is grade-8 slope algebra; see above.

**Foundations first.** On the dot grid two lines are drawn. The **step** of a line: from one dot it passes through, the smallest "over, up" that reaches the next dot it passes through, drawn as a little staircase and repeated along the line ("over two, up one; over two, up one"; the lesson says the step is the smallest stair that repeats, so over four up two is the same step as over two up one). Same step on both lines: they lean the same way, stay the same number of dots apart everywhere, and never meet: **parallel**. Different steps: they lean differently, so somewhere they meet; both are extended to the crossing. Lay the square corner in the crossing: a fit means **perpendicular**; the lesson adds that perpendicular lines make four square corners at the crossing. Then the picture behind the app's swap: the first line's step staircase turns a quarter turn about its corner and lands exactly on the second line's step. A quarter turn is a square corner; that is why the numbers swap. Said once, as what the turn showed.

**Read.** "How are these two lines related?" Three choices. At a glance: do both lines go up to the right with the same steepness? If not, they meet, and the only question left is whether the corner is square.

**The example.** The "neither" case, because it is the one children get wrong: line 1 with step over 2, up 1; line 2 with step over 1, down 1, drawn short and far apart so they do not cross on the card. (1) Steps read on both lines, staircases drawn. (2) Different steps: not parallel; parallel dims. (3) Both lines extend until they cross. (4) The ruler lays at the crossing along line 1; line 2 hides inside the arms, not along them. Not a square corner. (5) The step of line 1 turns a quarter turn: over 1, down 2; line 2's step is over 1, down 1; they do not match: the same verdict. (6) Neither. Quick second case, perpendicular: over 2 up 1 against over 1 down 2; the turned step lands; the ruler fits. The parallel case was built in the opening (two lines of different lengths on purpose).

**Fast.** Same step: parallel. Otherwise lay the square corner where they meet (or turn the step and see if it lands).
**Sense.** Parallel lines never change their gap: count the dots between them at two places. Perpendicular lines: one goes up-right, the other down-right (or one flat, one upright).
**Check.** Read the step along the other line from a different dot; test the other corner at the crossing.
**Rule out.** Different steps dim "parallel" at once. Two lines that both go up to the right cannot be perpendicular: "perpendicular" dims at once.
**Line.** "Same step: parallel. A square corner where they meet: perpendicular. Otherwise neither."
**Trap.** "They cross, so they're perpendicular." The ruler shows the gap. "Parallel means the same length." The parallel lines in the opening have different lengths and the lesson says so.
**Kit.** 4.2 dot grid, `steps`, `lay` at a crossing of two lines; `extend` exists. Add: `steps` as a part that `turn` can rotate about its corner, and the two lines colored teal and blue.
**Writer.** Cheaper, after `angle-classify` shows the ruler; three cases are fully specified here.

### `classify-triangle-angles` — Acute, right or obtuse triangle

**Standard.** 4.G.A.2: "the presence or absence of angles of a specified size. Recognize right triangles as a category, and identify right triangles."

**Foundations first.** The ruler opening. Then the one fact this type stands on: a triangle can hold at most one square-or-wider corner. Shown, not said: two square corners drawn at the ends of a base send the two sides straight up, parallel, never meeting; the third corner never comes. So every triangle has at least two acute corners, and the name comes from the one remaining corner, the biggest. If the biggest is square: **right triangle**. Wider: **obtuse triangle**. Narrower, so all three are acute: **acute triangle**. Where is the biggest corner? Across from the longest side; a side of the figure stretches and the corner facing it opens as it goes.

**Read.** "Classify this triangle by its angles." At a glance: a square mark anywhere and it is a right triangle, done. No mark: find the longest side; the corner across from it is the one to test; the other two do not matter.

**The example.** A right triangle with its right angle at the top, apex up, one leg much longer than the other, the square mark drawn (the app usually marks it; the lesson also lays the ruler so the child knows what to do when it does not). (1) Marks read: a small square at the top corner. (2) Longest side: the bottom; the corner across from it is the top; that is the one to test. (3) The ruler lays in: the second side lies along the arm; the square mark was telling the truth. (4) Right triangle. Quick second case: a wide, flat triangle pointing down, two sharp corners at the top, the wide corner at the bottom, no mark. A child who says "acute, it has sharp corners" is answered here: the ruler goes in the biggest corner only; the side sticks out: obtuse.

**Fast.** Longest side, corner across from it, lay the square corner there. One check.
**Sense.** Two square corners never fit in a triangle; a right triangle's other two corners are always acute.
**Check.** Lay the ruler in the other two corners: both narrower.
**Rule out.** A square mark anywhere dims acute and obtuse together. With no mark, the two small corners are no evidence either way; only the biggest decides.
**Line.** "Only the biggest corner names it."
**Trap.** "It has an acute angle, so it's an acute triangle." The quick second case is built for it. Judging by arm length (a tall skinny triangle "looks acute"): the ruler decides.
**Kit.** 4.1 marks (`right`), 4.2 `lay` with verdict, `move` for the stretch. Nothing new.
**Writer.** Cheaper.

### `symmetry-line-count` — How many lines of symmetry

**Standard.** 4.G.A.3: "Recognize a line of symmetry ... such that the figure can be folded along the line into matching parts. Identify line-symmetric figures and draw lines of symmetry." The app counts; the lesson draws each line it finds, which is the standard's last verb.

**Foundations first.** The fold opening. Then where folds can be: a fold line splits the shape into two halves of the same size, so it passes through the middle of the shape, and each end lands on a corner or on the exact middle of a side. The candidates for any shape: corner to corner, middle to middle, corner to the middle of the side across. Try each; count the ones that match.

**Read.** "How many lines of symmetry does this shape have?" Open answer. At a glance: the count can never be more than the number of sides; for a shape with all sides and corners alike, it equals the number of sides.

**The example.** A rectangle taller than wide (3 by 5), because its wrong answer is the most common wrong answer in the whole skill. (1) Candidates dashed: the up-down middle line, the across middle line, the two diagonals. (2) Fold the up-down middle: match; the line is drawn solid in teal: one. (3) Fold the across middle: match: two. (4) Fold a diagonal: the copy lands with a corner sticking out past the ghost, rust overhang held on screen while the voice says why: the corners of a rectangle are not the same distance from the middle of a diagonal. (5) The other diagonal: same overhang. (6) Two. Quick second case, a shape with none: a leaning parallelogram; both middle lines are folded and both leave overhang; zero; one beat looks ahead: "it does land on itself after a half turn, but a turn is not a fold." One-beat sense picture: a regular pentagon folded once, corner to opposite middle, then the other four drawn: five, as many as sides.

**Fast.** Try the folds through the middle: corner to corner, middle to middle, corner to middle. Count the matches. A regular shape: as many as sides.
**Sense.** Never more than the side count.
**Check.** Every fold line you drew should cross at one point in the middle of the shape; a line that misses the crossing is wrong. Refold each counted line the other way.
**Line.** "Fold it. If the halves match, that line counts."
**Trap.** The rectangle's diagonals (four). The diagonal fold with its overhang held is the center of the lesson. The parallelogram "looks balanced": both folds fail on screen.
**Kit.** 4.4 `fold`; a pass should leave the fold line drawn solid; a fail should hold its overhang until the next fold begins. Nothing else.
**Writer.** Cheaper: one tool, the cases are named.

### `symmetry-line-check` — Which dashed line is a line of symmetry

**Standard.** 4.G.A.3, the same clause: "folded along the line into matching parts."

**Foundations first.** The fold opening, and the halves rule: a fold line leaves the same amount of shape on each side.

**Read.** "Which dashed line is a line of symmetry?" Four lettered dashed lines on one shape; exactly one works. At a glance: a line that leaves more shape on one side than the other is gone before any folding. A line through a corner must come out at a corner or at a side's exact middle.

**The example.** An isosceles triangle pointing left (apex at the left, base vertical on the right), because children expect fold lines to run up and down. Lines: A, from the apex to the middle of the base (true); B, an up-down line through the middle of the triangle; C, from the top base corner to the middle of the side across from it (the right recipe on the wrong corner); D, through the apex but tilted. (1) Marks read: two ticks on the slanting sides: they match. (2) Halves at a glance: B leaves a small tip on the left and a big piece on the right; D leaves more below than above: both dim together, with the reason. (3) C is folded: the copy's corner lands off the ghost; overhang in rust. (4) A is folded: every edge glows: match. (5) A. Then the whole figure turns so the triangle points up and A stands vertical: the same line, the same match; the fold did not care which way the shape faced.

**Fast.** Equal halves first; fold what is left.
**Sense.** Each end of the true line sits on a corner or a side's middle, and the ticks say which sides are twins: the fold line runs between the twins.
**Check.** Fold the chosen line in the other direction; the other candidates all showed overhang.
**Rule out.** Unequal halves dims most choices at once; then one fold each for what remains.
**Line.** "A fold line cuts the shape into two halves that match."
**Trap.** "Fold lines go up and down." The sideways triangle and the turn at the end. Choosing by look: the C fold.
**Kit.** 4.4 `fold`, 4.7 `dim` with figure targets, 4.1 ticks. Nothing new.
**Writer.** Cheaper.

### `triangle-sides-angles-blanks` — Name a triangle two ways

**Standard.** 4.G.A.2 for the angle name ("angles of a specified size ... identify right triangles"). The side names (scalene, isosceles, equilateral) are not in any grade-4 Common Core clause; 2.G.A.1 ("a given number of angles or ... equal faces") is a stretch. State tests ask it; keep it, filed as a state-specific type. Convention flag: see the opening.

**Foundations first.** Two questions about one triangle, answered separately. By sides: tick marks; sides with the same number of ticks are the same length. No two alike: **scalene**. Two alike: **isosceles**. All three alike: **equilateral** (it also passes the isosceles check; the blank wants the most exact word, so write equilateral). By angles: the biggest corner, the ruler: right, obtuse, acute (the ruler opening). The two names are independent: a right triangle with two ticks and a right triangle with none are drawn side by side.

**Read.** Two blanks; the problem says which marks mean what. At a glance: the side with no tick is the odd one out and the longest; the corner across from it is the biggest and the one to test. Three ticks and the angle name is acute, always.

**The example.** A right triangle with the right angle at the top, one tick on each leg, no tick on the base, a square mark at the top. (1) Sides blank: count ticks. One here, one here: twins. None on the bottom: not a twin. Two equal sides: isosceles. (2) Angles blank: the unmarked side is the longest; the corner across from it is the top; the square mark says right; the ruler lays in and fits. Right. (3) Both blanks filled: isosceles, right. (4) Sense picture: a square folds along its diagonal and this triangle is one half; that is why two equal legs and a square corner go together. Quick second case: an equilateral triangle, three sides one tick each, no mark; the ruler goes in one corner, the side hides inside; all corners alike, so all acute. Equilateral, acute; and the lesson says once that three equal sides can never hold a square corner.

**Fast.** Ticks count: sides blank. Unmarked longest side, corner across, ruler: angles blank.
**Sense.** Three ticks: acute. A square mark: the other two corners are acute.
**Check.** Recount ticks starting from another corner; lay the ruler in the other two corners.
**Line.** "Two questions, two blanks. Ticks for the sides, the square corner for the angles."
**Trap.** Answering both blanks from one thought ("a square mark, so right ... and scalene"). The two beats are separate on screen. Reading a side with two ticks as "two equal sides": ticks are counted as matching sets, said aloud.
**Kit.** 4.1 ticks and right mark, 4.2 `lay`; an `answer` with two slots. Nothing new.
**Writer.** Cheaper.

### `quadrilateral-attributes` — The most exact name from the marks

**Standard.** 4.G.A.2: "based on the presence or absence of parallel or perpendicular lines, or ... angles of a specified size"; "most exact" is 3.G.A.1's shared-attributes idea and 5.G.B.4's hierarchy at grade-4 depth. The choice sets include trapezoid in some variants; under the inclusive convention the trapezoid-answer figure has exactly one pair of arrows, so "most exact" and the convention never fight. The lesson says that once.

**Foundations first.** Each mark defined live on a figure: arrowheads, one per pair of parallel sides (same step, never meet); tick marks for equal sides; the small square for a right angle (the ruler). Then the checklist card in its fixed order: 4 sides; 2 pairs of parallel sides; 4 right angles; 4 equal sides; with the names beside their rows: quadrilateral, parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus, square; trapezoid as its own row, 1 pair. The map, drawn once: quadrilaterals, parallelograms inside, rectangles and rhombuses overlapping inside those, squares in the overlap, trapezoids in the ring.

**Read.** "Most exact name" goes gold; "the marks are the evidence" means nothing is judged by look. At a glance: count arrow pairs. One: trapezoid, and every parallelogram name dies. Two: at least a parallelogram; then look for square marks and for ticks on all four sides.

**The example.** A rhombus tilted so a diagonal stands vertical (the "diamond"), two pairs of arrows, one tick on every side, no square marks; choices parallelogram, square, rectangle, rhombus. (1) Marks read aloud. (2) Checklist: 4 sides ✓. (3) Two arrow pairs: 2 pairs parallel ✓; parallelogram lights. (4) No square marks; the ruler lays in a corner and the side hides inside: narrower than square: 4 right angles ✗; rectangle and square dim together. (5) Four single ticks: 4 equal sides ✓; rhombus lights. (6) Longest list passed: rhombus, tagged most exact; parallelogram dims last, for a different reason: true, but shorter. (7) The shape drops into the rhombus region, outside the square overlap; the square example sitting in the overlap is the same tilt with four square marks: the tilt was never the difference. Quick second case: a rectangle drawn tilted, two arrow pairs, two tick groups, four square marks: passes rows one to three, fails four: rectangle. One-beat trapezoid: a single arrow pair, so parallelogram and everything under it die at row two.

**Fast.** Arrows, squares, ticks, in that order; the longest set of rows all passed.
**Sense.** A square passes everything, so one failed check ends it. No square marks means no rectangle and no square.
**Check.** Read the chosen name's rows aloud and point to the mark that passed each; lay the ruler in a second corner.
**Rule out.** The arrow count kills a group at once; missing square marks kill rectangle and square together; a true-but-shorter name dies last, and the lesson says why it is different.
**Line.** "Most exact means the longest list it passes."
**Trap.** The tilted rhombus called a square, or the tilted square called a rhombus: the right-angle row and the ruler. Choosing parallelogram because it is true: "most exact" gold.
**Kit.** 4.1 marks, 4.3 checklist and map with `drop`, 4.2 `lay`, 4.7 `dim`. `--dry`'s check of marks against the figure's geometry matters most here.
**Writer.** Cheaper, after the grade-5 pilot `quadrilateral-names` shows the checklist staging.

### `shape-sides-select` — Select the shapes with the property

**Standard.** 4.G.A.2, word for word: "Classify two-dimensional figures based on the presence or absence of parallel or perpendicular lines, or the presence or absence of angles of a specified size."

**Foundations first.** The ruler opening. Then the two words that change the job: **at least one** means one is enough, and for an obtuse angle the only corner worth testing is a shape's widest one; **no** means every corner must fail, and finding one square corner ends that shape. Parallel sides are checked by the step (one line, from `line-relationship`); perpendicular sides by the ruler at the corner they share, or where they would meet if drawn longer.

**Read.** The problem defines its property and says how many to choose; both go gold. At a glance: the number asked is the number you must end with; a shape with a square mark at every corner has no obtuse angle; any shape with a square mark fails "no right angle."

**The example.** "Choose the 3 shapes that show at least one obtuse angle." Six to-scale shapes in a lettered row: A a square turned 45°, B a rhombus, C a right triangle, D a tall acute triangle, E an obtuse triangle pointing down, F a regular pentagon. (1) A: square marks everywhere: no obtuse: dims. (2) B: widest corner tested, the side sticks out: obtuse: lit. (3) C: a square corner, and the other two in a triangle are acute: dims. (4) D: widest corner tested, hides inside: acute: dims. (5) E: the bottom corner sticks out: lit. (6) F: it looks neat, so it is tested, not guessed: the ruler lays in one corner and the side sticks out: obtuse: lit. (7) Count: three lit, three asked. B, E, F. Quick second case, the quantifier flipped: "Find the 4 shapes with no right angle" on another row; the ruler hunts for one square corner per shape, and a shape with one is out the moment it is found. One-beat third: "2 shapes with a pair of parallel sides," steps drawn along two sides.

**Fast.** For "at least one," test each shape's widest corner only. For "no," hunt for one square corner. Count your picks against the number asked.
**Sense.** A triangle holds at most one obtuse or right corner; the pentagon's corners are all alike, so one test speaks for all five.
**Check.** Recount the chosen shapes; re-lay the ruler in each chosen one.
**Rule out.** Shapes with square marks at every corner dim at once for obtuse; any shape with a square mark dims at once for "no right angle."
**Line.** "At least one: find one and stop. No: find one and the shape is out."
**Trap.** Choosing by look (the regular pentagon "isn't obtuse"; a leaning parallelogram "is"); reading "no right angle" as "has a right angle"; the wrong number of picks. The quantifier is gold, the count is said at the end.
**Kit.** 4.1, 4.2 `lay` repeated across a row, 4.7 `dim` with figure targets; a lettered row of six to-scale shapes needs no new part but the layout must keep letters clear of the shapes.
**Writer.** Cheaper; the two quantifier cases are both written out here.

### `line-pair-select` — The two lines that are parallel, or perpendicular

**Standard.** 4.G.A.1: "perpendicular and parallel lines. Identify these."

**Foundations first.** No grid here, so the step is replaced by its plain-paper cousin: trace a line on a scrap and **slide** it over without turning; if it lies along another line, the two lean exactly the same way and never meet: parallel. For perpendicular: extend both lines to where they cross and lay the square corner. The lesson says both as real moves a child can do on paper.

**Read.** "Select the 2 lines that appear parallel" (or perpendicular). Five lettered lines of different lengths and leans. At a glance: parallel lines lean the same way, both up to the right or both down; a line leaning the other way is out. Perpendicular lines lean opposite ways (or one flat, one upright); two that lean the same way are out.

**The example.** Parallel: A and B lean the same way, different lengths, far apart; D leans almost like them, about fifteen degrees off; C crosses A at a square corner; E is flat. (1) Leans sorted: C and E lean the wrong way for A: dim together. (2) A copy of A slides onto B without turning: it lies along B: parallel. (3) The copy slides onto D: a thin sliver opens between them, filled rust and held: not parallel. (4) A and B; two chosen, two asked. Quick second case, perpendicular, on the same five: A and C extended to their crossing, the ruler laid: it fits; the other crossings tried in one beat: not square.

**Fast.** Sort by lean first; slide a copy to confirm parallel; ruler at the crossing to confirm perpendicular. Count to two.
**Sense.** Parallel lines keep the same gap along their whole length; perpendicular lines make four square corners where they meet.
**Check.** Slide the copy the other way; lay the ruler in the other corner of the crossing; exactly two selected.
**Rule out.** Opposite leans kill "parallel" candidates at once; same leans kill "perpendicular" candidates at once.
**Line.** "Slide it, don't turn it. If it lands along the other line, they're parallel."
**Trap.** "Parallel means the same length": A and B differ visibly. The almost-parallel D chosen by look: the sliver. "They cross, so perpendicular": the ruler at the other crossings.
**Kit.** `slide` on a copy part, `extend`, 4.2 `lay` at a crossing of two lines, 4.7 `dim` with figure targets. Add to `--dry`: a note when two lines the author marks parallel differ in direction.
**Writer.** Cheaper.

### `angle-kind-select` — Select the angles of one kind

**Standard.** 4.G.A.1: "angles (right, acute, obtuse) ... Identify these."

**Foundations first.** The ruler opening, in full here since the problem is nothing else; then the one beat from the pilot: an angle's arms stretch and shrink and the ruler's verdict does not move. Turning the page does not change an angle either: a right angle is turned on screen until neither arm is level, the ruler lays in, still fits.

**Read.** The problem defines the kind and says how many; both gold. At a glance: a drawn square mark settles that angle instantly, in or out. Nothing else is decided by eye.

**The example.** "Select the 2 angles that appear obtuse." Six angles in a lettered row, built to deceive the eye and nothing else: A, 95 degrees with short arms; B, 150 degrees with long arms; C, 85 degrees with very long arms; D, 90 degrees turned so neither arm is level; E, 40 degrees; F, 60 degrees with long arms. (1) The ruler visits each in order; a verdict appears beside each as it settles: A sticks out, obtuse; B sticks out, obtuse; C hides inside by a hair, acute; D lies along the arm, right; E and F hide inside, acute. (2) C's arms shrink to A's length and A's stretch to C's: the verdicts stay. (3) Two obtuse, two asked: A and B. The right and acute wordings use the same six and the same ruler; one beat says so.

**Fast.** Lay the square corner in every angle; six rulers is six seconds. Count to the number asked.
**Sense.** Obtuse is wider than the corner of your paper and narrower than a flat line; right is exactly the corner.
**Check.** Lay the ruler the other way round in each chosen angle; exactly the asked number selected.
**Rule out.** A square mark rules an angle in or out at once. Nothing else is ruled out without the ruler.
**Line.** "Lay the square corner in it. The arms don't matter; the opening does."
**Trap.** Arm length (C chosen, A missed): the swap beat. A turned right angle not recognized: D.
**Kit.** 4.2 `lay` with verdict, repeated; 4.7 `dim`. Nothing new.
**Writer.** Cheaper: the pilot `angle-classify` is its reference and the six angles are specified.
