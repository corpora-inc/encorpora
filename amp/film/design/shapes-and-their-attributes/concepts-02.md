# Shapes and their attributes, part 2: per-type concepts for `polygon-properties`, `quadrilateral-categories` and `angle-classify`

Ten types. Each stands on the family design: the marked figure, the walk, the build, the ruler, the checklist, the dim. Where a type is off grade or badly conceived, that comes first.

Two things that cut across the grade-2 types, said here once:

- **The vocabulary is ahead of the grade.** 2.G.A.1 names triangles, quadrilaterals, pentagons, hexagons and cubes. The app's grade-2 cards say "pentagonal prism", "hexagonal pyramid", "congruent", "trapezoid", "vertices". The lessons keep every one of these, because the test will, but each is defined on the spot in grade-2 words: a face is a flat side, an edge is a stick, a vertex is a corner (a clay ball), congruent is "the same size and the same shape", a trapezoid is "four sides, and one pair that run the same way".
- **Solids are built, not shown.** Every solid lesson opens with the same forty-second build (K.G.B.5's sticks and clay balls): a base, then for a prism a copy straight above and a post from every base corner, for a pyramid one ball above and a stick to it from every base corner. Once a child has seen that twice, "pentagonal prism" is not a name to memorize but a thing to build in the head: pentagon, copy, posts.

## Grade 2: polygon-properties (2.G.A.1)

### `solid-parts` — Count the faces, edges or vertices of a prism or a pyramid

**Standard.** 2.G.A.1, "shapes having specified attributes, such as ... a given number of equal faces", and K.G.B.4, "parts (e.g., number of sides and vertices/'corners')" for three-dimensional shapes. Counting edges and vertices of a pentagonal prism is beyond anything Common Core asks at grade 2 (it is Texas 2.8B territory); keep the type, keep the words plain, and do not let the variants reach hexagonal solids at this grade.

**Foundations first.** A **face** is a flat side of the solid: a place you could lay a sheet of paper. An **edge** is where two faces meet: a straight stick. A **vertex** is where sticks meet: a corner, a clay ball; "corners" is said beside it every time. Dashed lines are edges at the back that the front is hiding; they are real and they are counted. Then the build, live: a triangle base of three balls and three sticks; a copy of it straight above; a post from each base corner. That is a **prism**: a base, its copy, and posts. Then a square base and one ball above, a stick from each base corner to it: a **pyramid**: a base and a point.

**Read.** Which part is asked, gold: faces, edges or vertices; they are different counts of the same solid. "Dashed lines show edges hidden at the back" is read as an instruction: count those too. At a glance: the base decides everything; find the base and count its sides first.

**The example.** "How many edges does this solid have?" on a triangular prism, standing on a triangle, two back edges dashed. (1) The base: walk its three sticks, 1, 2, 3, each lighting gold and settling teal with its number. (2) The copy on top: 4, 5, 6. (3) The posts: 7, 8, 9; the dashed post is lit in its own beat ("this one is behind; it is still a stick"). (4) 9. Then one more beat on the same solid: "every base corner got a post: three corners, three posts; and the base was copied: three sticks became six. Every count here is some copies of three." That line is the whole method at grade 2, without a formula.

**Second example (different build).** "How many faces does this solid have?" on a square pyramid: faces are counted by lighting, not walking, and the pyramid has no copy and no posts. Front two triangles light, 1, 2; the two at the back (their edges dashed) light, 3, 4; the solid lifts off the table and the square it stood on stays flat in teal: 5. "A pyramid: a base, and one triangle for every side of the base." (The vertices variant, 8 on a box, is the prism method: four balls down, four up; one beat if time allows, no own screen needed.)

**Fast.** Count the base's sides. Prism: that many once more for the top and once more for the posts (edges), or base plus two (faces), or base twice (vertices). Pyramid: base twice (edges), base plus one (faces and vertices). Said as the build, never as a rule.

**Sense.** The counts come in groups the size of the base. A prism's vertices are an even number. A pyramid has as many faces as vertices.

**Check.** Count the dashed parts by themselves, then add them to the solid ones. Or start the walk from a different corner of the base.

**Line.** "A prism is a base, its copy, and posts. A pyramid is a base and a point."

**Trap.** Counting only what shows: a cube "has three faces", a prism "has six edges". Real and universal. The dashed parts get their own counting beat, and the lift shows the bottom face. Second, smaller: hearing "vertices" and not knowing it means corners; "corners" is said every time beside it.

**Kit.** Family tool 4.5 in full: `solid` with `build` (base, top or apex, posts), lit faces with a count, `stand` and `lift`, dashed hidden edges. Numbered dots on balls and numbers beside sticks, as the walk does on polygons.

**Writer.** Pilot lesson 3; the strongest writer. It sets the build staging every later solid lesson copies, and the beauty of the solids is decided here.

### `solid-bottom-face-shape` — The shape of the face on the table

**Standard.** K.G.B.4 (parts of solids) and K.G.A.2 (name the flat shape). Filed at grade 2, which is fine as review. One wording only; the solid varies, so the lesson must cover a straight-sided footprint and a round one. A note on the choices: when both "square" and "rectangle" are offered, the app means "rectangle" as the one with two long sides and two short; the lesson says once that a square is a kind of rectangle, and that here we give the most exact name.

**Foundations first.** A solid standing on a table has one face pressed flat against it: the bottom face. We cannot see it, so we lift the solid off the table and the face stays behind, flat, in teal, then turns to face us so we see its true shape. The checks for a flat shape, in the order a child does them: round all the way with no corners, a circle; otherwise walk the sides and count; four sides with four square corners is a rectangle, and if all four sides are also the same length, a square.

**Read.** "The flat face that touches the table", gold on *touches the table*. It is not the face at the front and not the face on top. At a glance: a prism's bottom face is the same shape as its top face; a cylinder's is round.

**The example.** A triangular prism lying on a rectangular face, a triangle showing at the front. Choices: triangle, rectangle, square, circle. (1) Read: the question wants the face on the table, not the one facing us. (2) Lift: the solid rises; the face it stood on stays on the table in teal, then turns to face us. (3) Walk it: 4 sides. Lay the square corner in one corner: a match, right; the marks show all four square. (4) Two sides long, two short: not all equal. Rectangle. (5) The triangle facing front is an end of the solid; it was never touching the table.

**Second example (the round footprint).** A cylinder standing up; choices hexagon, square, rectangle, circle. In the picture its bottom looks like a squashed oval; children say "oval". Lift, and the face turns to face us: round all the way, no corners, the same width across every way: a circle. "Tilted, a circle looks like an oval. Face on, it is a circle." Quick, three or four beats.

**Fast.** Lift it in your head and look at its footprint face on. Round: circle. Straight sides: count them; four with square corners: compare the lengths.

**Sense.** For a prism the footprint matches the top face. The footprint has exactly as many sides as the solid has posts.

**Check.** Count the posts (edges rising from the table): that many sides on the footprint. Or lay the ruler at a second corner.

**Rule out.** Hexagon dims the moment the footprint has four corners; circle dims the moment it has any corner; between square and rectangle, the side lengths decide, read from the tick marks.

**Line.** "Lift it. The face it stood on is the answer."

**Trap.** Naming the face that faces you (the triangle on the end), because that is the shape you see. The example is built so the front face and the bottom face differ. Second: "oval" for the tilted circle, "slanted" or "diamond" for a tilted square; the face-on turn ends both.

**Kit.** 4.5's `lift` gains `"faceOn": true`: after the lift, the footprint rotates to face the viewer at true shape, with ticks and square-corner marks drawable on it; the footprint is then a polygon that takes `walk` and `lay`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper writer, from this concept, once `lift` exists and the `solid-parts` pilot shows the staging. One method, two cases, no reasoning beyond the concept.

### `polygon-sides-select` — Select the names and descriptions with as many sides as the drawn shape

**Standard.** 2.G.A.1: "Identify triangles, quadrilaterals, pentagons, hexagons" and "a given number of angles." The choices mix names ("a hexagon") with descriptions ("a shape with 6 corners", "a shape with 6 angles"), so the lesson's job is the fact that makes them one thing: sides, corners and angles of a polygon are all the same count.

**Foundations first.** A **side** is one straight part of the outline. A **corner** is where two sides meet. An **angle** is the opening at a corner; one corner, one angle. Walk the outline of a hexagon from a start dot: six sides, and the walk passes six corners, because every side ends at a corner and the last one is the start. Then the names by count: 3 triangle, 4 quadrilateral (square and rectangle are quadrilaterals), 5 pentagon, 6 hexagon, 8 octagon. A **polygon** is any closed shape made only of straight sides.

**Read.** "Have as many sides as the shape below", gold on *as many sides*; "select the 3" means exactly three. At a glance: once the drawn shape's count is known, every choice is a number, and we are matching numbers.

**The example.** An irregular hexagon, turned, nothing like a honeycomb cell; choices: a pentagon, a shape with 6 corners, a hexagon, a square, a shape with 6 angles; select 3. (1) Walk the sides from the start dot: 1 to 6; the walk stops where it began. 6 sides. (2) Numbered dots drop on the corners: also 6; a small arc appears in each corner: 6 angles. One count, three words. (3) Each choice becomes a number: pentagon 5, 6 corners 6, hexagon 6, square 4, 6 angles 6. (4) Three choices say 6; the other two dim together: "a pentagon is five and a square is four; gone." (5) The three light.

**Fast.** Count the sides once. Every name and every description on the card is a number; keep the ones that say the same number.

**Sense.** Sides, corners and angles of one shape are always the same number; a card where two of the three correct choices are "6 corners" and "6 angles" is the generator saying so.

**Check.** Exactly three chosen. Count the corners by dots from a different start; same number.

**Rule out.** Any name whose count is different dims at once, in one beat, with the counts said.

**Line.** "Sides, corners, angles: one shape, one number."

**Trap.** A long, lopsided hexagon "isn't a hexagon" because it does not look like one. The example is deliberately irregular and the count, not the look, names it. Second: "6 angles" treated as a trick when the shape "has 6 sides"; the dots and arcs on the same corners end it.

**Kit.** Family 4.1 (`walk` on sides and corners, `marks.arc`) and 4.7 (`dim`). Nothing new.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper writer. One walk, one fact, a match.

### `solid-figure-select` — Select the drawn solids with a stated property

**Standard.** 2.G.A.1, "shapes having specified attributes, such as ... a given number of equal faces." "Congruent" in a grade-2 card is off grade; the lesson says "matching: the same size and the same shape" and names the word once. The curriculum plan's note that this type and `solid-property-select` generate the same prompt is not borne out: here the six solids are drawn and lettered; there they are named. Keep both; they practice opposite directions (picture to name, name to build).

**Foundations first.** Flat face versus curved surface (a ball has one surface and no flat face; a can has two flat faces and one curved surface; a cone one of each). The build, twice: a prism is a base, its copy and posts, so it always has two matching faces, one above the other, running the same way (parallel); a pyramid is a base and a point, so every face that is not the base is a triangle. Name the six drawn solids once from their parts, in one beat each, so the choices are known shapes before the property is read.

**Read.** The property, gold, said in child words before any solid is looked at: "two matching faces that run the same way" means "is it a prism, or a can?" "At least one triangular face" means "a pyramid, or a prism built on a triangle." "Select the 3" means exactly three. At a glance: a property about curves needs no counting; a property about a count needs the build.

**The example.** "Select the 3 solids that have two congruent parallel faces." Solids A cube, B triangular prism, C square pyramid, D cylinder, E cone, F sphere. (1) Say the property plainly: two faces, same size and shape, one straight above the other. (2) The prisms: A, the cube, a square and its copy; B, the triangular prism, a triangle and its copy; each pair highlights gold. (3) D, the cylinder: a circle and its matching circle above it. Round, but still two matching flat faces; it passes. (4) C has one base and a point; E has one circle and a point; F has no flat face at all: they dim together. (5) A, B, D.

**Second example (the pyramid side of the build).** "Select the 2 solids that have at least one triangular face": the square pyramid's sides are triangles; the triangular prism's two ends are triangles; the cube, cylinder, cone and sphere have none. Four beats. The counting properties ("6 or more flat faces", "only flat surfaces") ride on `solid-parts`' count (base plus two, base plus one) and the flat-or-curved glance; the recap says so, no extra screen.

**Fast.** Turn the property into a build question first: prism or pyramid, flat or round, how many faces from the base. Then look at each letter once.

**Sense.** Every prism passes "two matching parallel faces", and no pyramid ever does. Every pyramid passes "triangular faces". The count asked for tells you how many should light.

**Check.** Exactly the number asked. For each chosen solid, point to the two faces (or the triangle, or the curve) on the picture.

**Rule out.** In one beat, with the reason: "C and E come to a point: one base only. F has no flat face." For a curved-surface property, every solid made only of sticks dims at once.

**Line.** "A prism is a base, its copy and posts, so it always has two matching faces."

**Trap.** "A cylinder has no faces, faces have corners." Real; children reserve "face" for polygons. The cylinder passes in the worked example and the lesson says why. Second: the cube not recognized as a prism; the build shows a square, its copy and four posts.

**Kit.** 4.5 solids drawn small in a lettered row of six at one scale; `highlight` on `s.base` and `s.top` together; `dim` with figure targets (4.7).

**Writer.** A capable cheaper writer, after the solids pilot. The reasoning is in the concept; the writing is checks on a row of pictures.

### `congruent-figure-pair` — Which two figures are exactly the same size and shape

**Misfiled.** Filed under 8.G.A.2 (a sequence of rigid motions). At grade 2, "same size and shape, turn or flip it to see" is K.G.B.4, "compare two-dimensional shapes, in different sizes and orientations." Relink it there and keep it; the 8.G.A.2 half ("describe a sequence") is family 26's.

**Foundations first.** Two shapes are **congruent** when you could cut one out and lay it on the other with nothing sticking out and nothing uncovered, turning it or flipping it over if you need to. That needs two things: the same shape (same number of sides, corners the same) and the same size (every side the same length as its partner). The figures sit on a dot grid, so "same length" is a count of dots along a side: grade 2 can measure. A bigger copy is the same shape but not the same size. A shape with one corner pulled has some sides longer, so it is not the same shape.

**Read.** "Exactly the same size and shape", both words gold; "drawn at the same scale" means the grid is honest. At a glance: a figure visibly bigger or smaller than the others cannot be in the pair; a figure with a side of a different length cannot either.

**The example.** Four figures on one dot grid: A a right trapezoid; B the same trapezoid turned a quarter turn and flipped; C a copy of A half again as large; D A with its top corner pulled one dot to the right. Choices: A and B, A and C, B and C, B and D. (1) Size first: C is larger; its bottom side counts six dots where A's counts four. Any pair with C is out: "A and C", "B and C" dim together. (2) D against A: three sides match by count, one does not (five dots against four) and a corner has moved; "B and D" dims. (3) Prove A and B: a ghost of A stays; B lifts, turns a quarter turn, flips, slides onto A, and its outline glows gold all the way round: nothing sticking out. (4) A and B.

**Fast.** Throw out the odd size by one side's count. Then count the sides of the rest; a mismatch throws out that figure. Two remain; turn one onto the other in your head.

**Sense.** A pair that lands has sides that match count for count; if any count differs, nothing you do with turning will fix it.

**Check.** Lay it on. Every corner lands on a corner, every side on a side. Or count every side of both; all match.

**Rule out.** Any pair naming the bigger copy dims at once ("same shape, wrong size"); any pair naming the pulled figure dims ("one side is longer").

**Line.** "Turned or flipped, it is the same shape if it lands."

**Trap.** "B is turned, so it is different." Real and exactly K.G.A.2's point; B lands on A on screen. The other way round, "C is the same shape, so they match"; the dot count ends it.

**Kit.** `grid` with `dots: true` (4.2); the fold and spin's landing test (4.4) applied to a `turn`, `flip` and `slide` of a copy onto another figure: a muted ghost, a gold glow where the outline coincides, rust overhang where it does not. Call it `{"do":"land","target":"B","on":"A"}` after the author's motions; `--dry` notes a landing that does not match.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper writer. Both motions exist; the concept names the four figures and the order the decoys die.

### `name-cut-shape-pieces` — Name the pieces a flat shape is cut into

**Standard.** 2.G.A.1, "Identify triangles, quadrilaterals"; K.G.B.4 for the counting. The cut itself is 1.G.A.3's picture. A convention note: the app's method says a trapezoid has "exactly one pair of parallel sides"; the family recommends the inclusive definition. Here it never bites (a piece cut from a rectangle by a slanted line has exactly one pair), so the lesson defines a trapezoid as "four sides, with a pair that run the same way" and says nothing about "exactly."

**Foundations first.** A straight cut through a shape makes pieces; each piece is a new shape with its own outline, and the cut line is now a side of each piece it touches. The pieces part by a small gap so each outline can be walked alone. Names by sides: 3 a triangle; 4 a quadrilateral; four sides and four square corners a rectangle; a rectangle with all four sides equal a square; four sides with a pair that run the same way (parallel, drawn with arrowheads) a trapezoid.

**Read.** "What are the pieces", gold on *pieces*: not the shape we started with. The colors count the pieces for us. At a glance: the answer must have as many names as there are colors.

**The example.** A wide rectangle cut from its bottom-left corner to the middle of the top side; pieces in two colors. Choices: a triangle and a trapezoid, a triangle and a rectangle, 2 triangles, a square and a rectangle. (1) Two colors, two pieces: "2 triangles and a rectangle" would be three pieces; "a square and a rectangle" are both four-sided, but one piece here has a slanted side; we will see. (2) The pieces part. Walk the left piece: 1, 2, 3. A triangle; its square corner is the rectangle's old corner. (3) Walk the right piece: 1, 2, 3, 4. Four sides. Lay the square corner at its two right-hand corners: right. At the two corners on the cut: not square. Not a rectangle. Its top and bottom run the same way: arrowheads. A trapezoid. (4) A triangle and a trapezoid.

**Second example (the cut everyone meets).** The same rectangle cut corner to corner: both pieces walked, 3 and 3: 2 triangles. Three beats. (A rectangle cut on two slanted lines into two triangles and a rectangle is the same method on three pieces; no own screen.)

**Fast.** Count the colors. Walk each piece, counting the cut as a side. Name by count; for four sides, lay the ruler at every corner before saying rectangle.

**Sense.** As many names as colors. A cut edge is slanted, so a piece touching it on two sides cannot have square corners there.

**Check.** The pieces slide back together and make the original shape; nothing missing, nothing extra.

**Rule out.** Any choice with the wrong number of pieces dims at once. Then any four-sided name whose checks the piece fails.

**Line.** "The cut is a new side. Walk each piece on its own."

**Trap.** Calling the four-sided piece a rectangle because it has two square corners. Real; the ruler at all four corners is the beat that stops it. Also walking the original rectangle instead of a piece; the gap between pieces makes each outline its own.

**Kit.** 4.6 `cut` with the gap and addressable pieces that take `walk`; 4.2 `lay` on a piece's corner; `marks.parallel` on the pieces. Nothing beyond the family's list.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper writer; the concept chooses the cut and the order of checks.

### `solid-property-select` — Select the named prisms and pyramids with a stated property

**Standard.** 2.G.A.1 as for `solid-figure-select`; the names "pentagonal pyramid", "hexagonal prism" are far ahead of grade-2 Common Core and must be taught, not assumed. The idea that makes this type doable at all: **a solid's name is build instructions.** The first word is the base; the second word says prism (copy and posts) or pyramid (a point).

**Foundations first.** Read a name backwards. "Pentagonal prism": pentagon, then prism: draw a pentagon of five balls and sticks, copy it above, five posts. "Triangular pyramid": triangle, then a point: three balls and sticks, one ball above, three sticks up. A cube is a square prism: a square, its copy, four posts. Then the counts the build gives, said as groups, not formulas: edges of a prism are three groups of the base's sides (base, copy, posts); edges of a pyramid are two groups (base, sticks to the point); faces are the base's sides plus two ends, or plus one base.

**Read.** The property, gold; "12 or more" includes 12; "fewer than 6" does not include 6. At a glance: "one base and all other faces triangles" is every pyramid; "two matching parallel faces" is every prism, cube included; a count property needs the build for each name.

**The example.** "Select the 3 solids that have 12 or more edges: Rectangular prism, Triangular pyramid, Triangular prism, Pentagonal prism, Pentagonal pyramid, Hexagonal pyramid." A small table beside the row of six tiny builds: name, base, prism or pyramid, edges. (1) Rectangular prism: rectangle, 4 sides; prism: 4, 4, 4: 12. Yes. (2) Triangular pyramid: 3; pyramid: 3 and 3: 6. (3) Triangular prism: 3, 3, 3: 9. (4) Pentagonal prism: 5, 5, 5: 15. Yes. (5) Pentagonal pyramid: 5 and 5: 10. (6) Hexagonal pyramid: 6 and 6: 12. "Twelve or more" includes twelve: yes. (7) The three light; the other three dim with their counts said.

**Second example (the word decides).** "Select the 3 solids that have one base and all its other faces triangles": the three names ending in *pyramid*; one build shown (square pyramid: four triangles rise to the point); the three *prism* names dim together: "a prism has two bases." Three beats.

**Fast.** Second word: prism or pyramid. First word: count the base. Edges: three bases for a prism, two for a pyramid. Faces: base plus two, or base plus one.

**Sense.** More sides on the base, more of everything. A prism always has more edges than the pyramid on the same base. "Or more" means the number itself counts.

**Check.** Build the borderline one (the hexagonal pyramid at exactly 12) with balls and sticks and count the sticks with numbered dots.

**Rule out.** For a prism property every name ending in *pyramid* dims at once, and the reverse; for a count, the smallest bases go first ("triangular anything cannot reach twelve").

**Line.** "The name is the build: the base first, then copy and posts or a point."

**Trap.** "Twelve or more" read as "more than twelve", dropping the hexagonal pyramid. Real at every grade; the example is chosen to sit exactly on 12. Second: a triangular prism thought to be "all triangles" (its two ends are; its three sides are rectangles); the build shows it.

**Kit.** The `table` part (exists) beside a row of six small `solid` builds; `dim` across table rows and figures together.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper writer, from this concept; the table and the borderline case are specified, and `--dry` checks the counts.

## Grade 3: quadrilateral-categories (3.G.A.1)

### `classify-quadrilateral` — The most exact name for a marked quadrilateral

**Standard.** 3.G.A.1: "shapes in different categories (e.g., rhombuses, rectangles, and others) may share attributes ... shared attributes can define a larger category (e.g., quadrilaterals). Recognize rhombuses, rectangles, and squares as examples of quadrilaterals." Choice set is exactly the standard's four names; no parallelogram, no trapezoid, so no convention is needed here. The first nesting a child meets.

**Foundations first.** The marks, each defined as it is read: a small square in a corner means a right angle, a square corner; a tick on a side means "same length as every other side with one tick." What is not marked is not promised. Then the idea: a name is a list of checks. Quadrilateral: 4 sides. Rectangle: 4 sides and 4 square corners. Rhombus: 4 sides and 4 equal sides. Square: all three. The checklist card appears with those three rows and the four names beside the rows they need. Then the morph, once: a square's corners are pushed so it leans; the right-angle marks vanish, the ticks stay: a rhombus. Pull it back and stretch two sides: the marks swap: a rectangle. A square is the shape that keeps both.

**Read.** "Most exact name", gold on *most exact*: more than one name may fit; we want the longest list the shape passes. At a glance: if every corner has a square mark, it is at least a rectangle; if every side has the same tick, at least a rhombus; if neither, the name is quadrilateral and that is a real answer.

**The example.** A rhombus turned so one corner points up (the "diamond"), four single ticks, no corner marks. Choices: rhombus, square, rectangle, quadrilateral. (1) Row 1: walk the sides, 4: ✓; quadrilateral lights. (2) Row 2, square corners: lay the ruler in one corner: the second side sticks out past the arm; not square; and no corner has the mark: ✗. Rectangle fails; so does square. (3) Row 3, equal sides: one tick on each of the four: ✓; rhombus lights. (4) Two names lit, quadrilateral and rhombus; rhombus is the longer list: most exact. (5) The shape drops into the rhombus region of the map; "it is a quadrilateral too, but we can say more."

**Second example (every check passes).** The same shape with square corners added, turned the same way: every row ✓, every name lights, square tagged most exact. "A square passes the rectangle list and the rhombus list. Every square is a rectangle; every square is a rhombus." Four beats. **Third, one screen, brief:** a four-sided shape with two ticks on two sides only and one square-corner mark: row 1 ✓, rows 2 and 3 ✗; quadrilateral is the only name lit, and it is the answer. The generator's seen answer is this case; children think it must have a fancier name.

**Fast.** Two questions, in order: square marks on all four corners? the same tick on all four sides? Both: square. First only: rectangle. Second only: rhombus. Neither: quadrilateral.

**Sense.** A rhombus is a square that lost its square corners; a rectangle is a square that lost its equal sides. If the shape looks like a pushed-over square, expect rhombus.

**Check.** Say the chosen name's list aloud and touch the mark for each item on the figure. For the dimmed names, say the item they fail.

**Rule out.** Square and rectangle dim together in one beat ("no square corners"); quadrilateral dims for a different reason, said ("true, but shorter"). The two reasons are the lesson.

**Line.** "Most exact means the longest list it passes."

**Trap.** "It's a diamond, so it's a rhombus" and "it's a tilted square": naming by how it sits. The ruler, not the tilt, is what fails the square; and the second example is the identical tilt with square corners added, which *is* a square. Second: "a square can't be a rectangle": the checklist shows it passing every rectangle row.

**Kit.** 4.1 marks and `walk`; 4.2 `lay`; 4.3 checklist (three rows, four names) and the quadrilateral map with no parallelogram ring at this grade; 4.7 `dim`.

**Writer.** The strongest writer: the first nesting, the diamond trap, the order the decoys die, and the morph's timing are the quality.

### `find-quadrilateral` — The quadrilateral in a named group, or the one in none

**Standard.** 3.G.A.1, both halves: "Recognize rhombuses, rectangles, and squares as examples of quadrilaterals" (which shape is a rhombus/rectangle/square) and, as the identify-version of "draw examples of quadrilaterals that do not belong to any of these subcategories", "which quadrilateral is not a square, a rectangle or a rhombus." **A generator flag:** a square is a rectangle and a rhombus. If the card asks "which shape is a rectangle" and offers both a square and a non-square rectangle, two answers are right. The generator must never offer a square as a decoy for rectangle or rhombus; the lesson will say plainly that a square would also be right.

**Foundations first.** The same marks and the same three lists as `classify-quadrilateral`, defined again briefly (this lesson may be seen first). "Which shape is a rhombus" means: run the rhombus list on every lettered shape. "Not a square, a rectangle or a rhombus" means: find the shape that fails both the square-corner check and the equal-side check.

**Read.** The group name, gold; or *not*, gold. At a glance: a shape with four square-corner marks is at least a rectangle; one with four matching ticks at least a rhombus; the "none" answer has neither.

**The example.** "Which quadrilateral is not a square, a rectangle or a rhombus?" A: a square turned 45°, marks on all corners and sides; B: a four-sided shape with two square corners and no ticks (a right trapezoid, unnamed at this grade); C: a tall rectangle, four corner marks, two kinds of tick; D: a rhombus, four ticks, no corner marks. (1) A: every mark; it passes every list, so it is a square (and a rectangle, and a rhombus); it belongs to all three: out, and "turned does not matter." (2) C: four square corners: a rectangle: out. (3) D: four equal sides: a rhombus: out. (4) B: two square corners only; lay the ruler at the other two: not square. No ticks: no promise of equal sides. It fails both lists. B. (5) B drops into the outer ring of the map, outside every named region.

**Second example (a named group).** "Which shape is a rhombus?" A: a rhombus, four ticks, no corner marks; B: a rectangle; C: a kite-shaped quadrilateral with one tick on two sides and two ticks on the other two; D: an unmarked quadrilateral. Say the rhombus list; look only for four matching ticks: A. C's ticks are two kinds: two pairs of equal sides, not four equal. Four beats.

**Fast.** Say the list for the group asked. Look at each letter only for that list's marks. For "none", look for the shape with neither all square corners nor all matching ticks.

**Sense.** The square passes everything, so it is never the "none" answer, and it is a true answer to "which is a rectangle" or "which is a rhombus" if it is offered. A shape with exactly two square corners is never a rectangle.

**Check.** For the chosen shape, touch each mark the list needs. For "none", name the check each other letter passed.

**Rule out.** For "which is a rectangle", every shape missing a corner mark dims in one beat. For "none", every shape that passed a list dims as it passes, with the list named.

**Line.** "Say the list, then look only for its marks."

**Trap.** The turned square taken as "not a square", so chosen as the "none" answer: the worked example's first step kills it. Two square corners taken as a rectangle: the ruler at the other corners. The kite's "equal sides" taken as a rhombus: two kinds of ticks.

**Kit.** 4.1 marks, 4.2 `lay`, 4.3 the map, 4.7 `dim` with figure targets. A compact per-letter mini-checklist (two rows) beside each shape would help the phone layout; otherwise the board card reused per letter.

**Writer.** The strongest writer, with `classify-quadrilateral`: the choice of decoys and the honesty about the square being two right answers are the quality.

## Grade 4: lines-symmetry (4.G.A.1)

### `angle-classify` — Acute, right, obtuse or straight

**Standard.** 4.G.A.1: "angles (right, acute, obtuse) ... Identify these in two-dimensional figures." Straight is in the choices and is taught (two square corners). "Reflex" is in the type's description and in no choice; drop it from the description. The story variants ("props a window wide", "opens a gate out from the fence") rest on an assumed picture (wide means past a square corner; straight out means a square corner); the lesson draws each story so the assumption is seen, not guessed.

**Foundations first.** An **angle** is the opening between two arms that meet at a point, the **vertex**; its size is how far open, not how long the arms are. The ruler: a **square corner**, the corner of a sheet of paper, is a **right angle**. Lay the corner of the card in the angle with one arm along one side; where the other side falls is the verdict. Hides behind the card's arm: **acute**, narrower than a square corner. Sticks out past it, but the arms are not yet one line: **obtuse**. The two arms make one straight line, two square corners side by side: **straight**. The ruler slides into a right angle first so the match is seen once.

**Read.** "What kind of angle", gold on *kind*: not a measure, a sort. In a story, which two things are the arms (ladder and ground, not ladder and wall) and where they meet. At a glance: arms that make one line are straight; a corner of anything built square (a frame, a book, a door) is right.

**The example.** A drawn angle opening up and to the left, short arms, a small arc; obtuse. Choices: acute, right, obtuse, straight. (1) Read: the arc marks the opening we are sorting. (2) Lay the ruler: the gold L slides in, one arm along the lower side. The other side sticks out past the card's arm. Wider than a square corner. (3) Not straight: the two arms do not make one line; a second ruler would be needed to reach it and this angle does not. Obtuse. (4) The arms stretch to three times their length; the ruler stays; the verdict does not change. "Longer arms, same opening." (5) The arc shrinks and grows; still obtuse.

**Second example (the story, drawn).** "Student B leans a ladder against a wall. What kind of angle does the ladder make with the ground?" Draw the ground as a line, the wall standing up from it, the ladder leaning; the angle asked is marked at the ladder's foot, between ladder and ground, in gold; the ladder-wall angle is not the one asked. Lay the ruler at the foot: the ladder hides behind the card's arm. Acute. Four beats. **Third, brief:** "Student A swings a garden gate from its closed place around to the fence": the gate's closed position and the gate now lie along one line; two square corners fill the opening: straight. "Flat is still an angle." Three beats.

**Fast.** Lay the square corner with one arm on one side. Behind: acute. Match: right. Past: obtuse. One line: straight.

**Sense.** Acute is less than a quarter turn; obtuse is between a quarter turn and a half; straight is exactly half. An open book lying flat is straight; a slice of pizza is acute.

**Check.** Lay the ruler along the other arm instead; the same verdict. In a story, name the two arms aloud and point to where they meet.

**Rule out.** "Straight" dims unless the arms make one line; "right" dims unless the ruler matches exactly; then one of acute and obtuse remains, by which side of the card's arm the second side fell.

**Line.** "Lay the square corner in it. Narrower is acute, wider is obtuse, a match is right."

**Trap.** Judging by arm length ("the long arms make it big") or by arc size; the stretch and the arc change on screen with the verdict holding. Swapping the names acute and obtuse; the lesson pairs each name with its ruler picture and the recap shows both side by side. In stories, picking the wrong two arms (ladder and wall); the gold arc marks the asked pair. A straight angle "isn't an angle, it's a line"; the two rulers fill it.

**Kit.** 4.2 `ruler` and `lay` with `verdict`; arms stretched by `move` of their endpoints while the ruler holds; a second ruler laid beside the first for straight. Small story drawings (ground line, wall, ladder; fence and gate) from existing `seg` and `poly`.

**Writer.** Pilot lesson 4: the strongest writer, because this lesson sets the ruler's staging for seven grade-4 types. The later ruler types (`angle-kind-select`, `classify-triangle-angles`) go to a capable cheaper writer with this lesson as their reference.
