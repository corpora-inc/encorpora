# Family 8, concepts for the multiplicative and multistep types

Ten problem types from three skills. Each stands on the family design: the strip, the five habits, the same-thing test, the ? that chooses the operation. Where a type is misfiled or carries an off-grade variant, that is said first. Arithmetic belongs to the family that owns it and is named, not retaught.

**A shared opening for the equal-groups types** (`operation-sentence-choice`, `staar-story-model-choice`, `delivery-then-use`, `purchase-change`, `fl-two-step-story-equation`): about twenty seconds, drawn live. "Equal groups" means the same number in every one. A story about equal groups gives two numbers that count *different things*: how many groups (the count) and how many in each (the size). On the strip, the size is written in each piece, the count is a bracket above ("9 cups"), and the whole is a bracket below. The three positions for the ?: whole missing is count × size; size missing is whole ÷ count; count missing is whole ÷ size. The test that always works: do the two numbers count the same thing? Shells and shells: add or take away. Cups and pencils: equal pieces.

### Grade 3: multiplicative-stories (3.OA.A.3, 3.OA.A.1)

**`operation-sentence-choice` — The number sentence for equal groups.** Foundation 6. Multiple choice; a mixed type with one idea: four ways of showing equal groups (a story, a picture of groups, jumps on a number line, a sentence given and the story asked) and one "which can NOT".
Standard: 3.OA.A.3, "solve word problems in situations involving equal groups ... by using drawings and equations with a symbol for the unknown number"; 3.OA.A.1, "interpret 5 × 7 as the total number of objects in 5 groups of 7 objects each."
Foundations first: the shared opening. Then two facts on the strip: a sentence is a way of writing the picture, and 9 × 3 is the short way of writing 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3, nine pieces of three; 3 × 9 covers the same strip (the pieces do not care which number is said first). A division cuts a whole into equal pieces, so the whole comes first.
Read: "in all" goes gold; n is the total number of pencils, and it counts pencils, not cups. At a glance: the total is bigger than either number, and cups and pencils are different things, so this is equal pieces.
Example: 9 cups, 3 pencils in each; choices 9 + 3, 9 × 3, 9 ÷ 3, 9 − 3. (1) The app's picture of nine cups is outlined; the groups slide into a row and become nine equal pieces, 3 written in each, count bracket "9 cups" above, a gold ? bracket below. (2) Nine pieces of three: 9 × 3 = n, written under the picture in the pieces' colors, read back as the story. (3) Check by counting the pieces by threes: 3, 6, 9 ... 27; n = 27; 27 pencils, and 27 is bigger than 9 and than 3, as the picture promised.
Quick second case (the "can NOT"): a frog makes 8 jumps of 2. A strip of jumps is a number line with thickness: eight pieces of 2 from 0. Eight twos added, 8 × 2 and 2 × 8 all cover that strip. 8 ÷ 2 cuts 8 into twos: a different picture, four pieces. It cannot find the distance.
Quick third case (the other direction): "Which story can be solved with 7 × 4?" The story must have seven equal groups of four (or four of seven). "Shared equally among 7" cuts a whole: division. "4 more are put in" and "5 are taken out" are marbles joining or leaving marbles: same thing, add or take away. The stickers story has 7 pages of 4.
Fast: name the count and the size; the sentence with those two numbers multiplied is the total. For "can NOT", the sentence whose picture is different.
Sense: a total of groups is bigger than the count and bigger than the size.
Check: count the pieces aloud by the size.
Rule out: 9 + 3 adds cups to pencils, different things (dim). 9 − 3: nothing left (dim). 9 ÷ 3 cuts 9 cups into threes, and sharing makes smaller; the total is the biggest number here (dim).
Line: "Same thing: add or take away. Groups the same size: multiply."
Trap: 9 + 3 = 12, the most common grade-3 answer when two numbers appear in a story. The count bracket and the pieces make the 9 a count, not an amount of pencils, so there is nothing to add it to.
Kit: `equal` pieces and the count bracket; the app's group picture and number-line jumps through `scene`, with the "become a bar" motion generalized to groups (groups outlined, slid into a row, fused into equal pieces). `dim`.
Writer: a capable cheaper model, from this concept: one picture, three named decoys, the kit's `equal` carries the proof.

### Grade 3: multi-operation-stories (3.OA.D.8)

**`fl-two-step-story-equation` — The equation for a two-step story with a letter.** Foundations 5, 6, 7. Multiple choice; three shapes of story, which is why it needs more than one example.
Standard: 3.OA.D.8, "Represent these problems using equations with a letter standing for the unknown quantity."
Foundations first: the shared opening, then: a letter is a ? with a name, and the story says what it counts ("b is the number of markers in one box"; "x is all the stickers"). The one question that decides every equation here: where does the extra go? Beside the pieces, as its own piece (4 loose markers are not in any box), or inside every piece (each friend gets 5 more)? Parentheses mean "this part of the picture first", and they appear exactly when the extra is inside each piece.
Read: the letter's definition goes gold. At a glance: if the letter is "one box" or "each friend", the equal pieces are the letter; if it is the stickers being shared, the whole is the letter. The total is on the right of the equation; the asked total counts the same thing as the pieces.
Example A: 3 boxes of b markers, 4 loose, 28 in all; choices 3b − 4, 3 + b + 4, 3b + 4, 3(b − 4). (1) Three equal pieces labeled b (gold, dashed), then a short teal piece 4 beside them; whole bracket 28. (2) Read the picture left to right: b + b + b + 4 = 28; three b's is 3b: 3b + 4 = 28, each term in its piece's color. (3) Read it back as the story: three boxes and four loose make twenty-eight. (4) Check by finding b (3b = 24, b = 8, three eights are 24, and 24 + 4 = 28), then say the sense: b must be less than 28 ÷ 3, and 8 is.
Rule out: 3 + b + 4 adds boxes to markers: different things. 3b − 4: nothing left. 3(b − 4): the loose markers are not in a box. All three dim together.
Example B (the parentheses case, worked fully because its decoy is the one tests aim at): 8 friends each have b cards; each gets 5 more; together 96. (1) Eight equal pieces b. (2) A 5 is added inside every piece at once: eight pieces, each now b and 5 together; bracket 96. (3) Eight of (b + 5): 8(b + 5) = 96. Contrast with A aloud: there, one 4 beside the boxes; here, a 5 in every piece. (4) Check: 96 ÷ 8 = 12 in each piece now; 12 − 5 = 7 at first; eight twelves are 96.
Rule out: 8b − 5 and 8(b − 5): nobody lost cards. b ÷ 8 + 5: nothing was shared.
Quick third case: x stickers shared among 3, each gets 4 more, each has 9. Here the letter is the whole: a strip x cut into three equal pieces, one piece is x ÷ 3; a 4 joins that piece; the piece is 9. x ÷ 3 + 4 = 9 (x = 15). 3x + 4 = 9 would make three piles of stickers smaller than one pile.
Fast: draw the pieces, then ask "beside, or inside each?" Beside: count × letter, plus the extra. Inside each: count × (letter + extra). Shared: letter ÷ count.
Sense: one box holds fewer than the whole; one share is less than the total.
Check: find the letter from the chosen equation and put it back in the story.
Line: "The extra: beside the boxes, or inside each one? Draw it, and the parentheses draw themselves."
Trap: 3 + b + 4 (every number grabbed; misconception 4) and 3(b − 4) or 8b − 5 (the extra attached to the wrong piece; misconception 9). The strip puts the 4 outside the boxes and the 5 inside every piece, so neither can be written another way.
Kit: `equal` with a letter label; a new `grow` action that adds a labeled sub-piece inside every equal piece at once, with the piece's label becoming "b + 5"; the equation written in the pieces' colors.
Writer: strongest. The quality is in the "beside or inside" words and in staging B against A.

**`staar-story-model-choice` — The equation or expression for a sharing story, or the one that does not fit.** Foundations 5 and 6. Multiple choice.
Off grade, say first: the two money variants (2.71 + 4.08 + f = 10.27) are decimals, 4.MD.A.2, grade 4; move them to `money-decimal-story` or a grade-4 type. The "can NOT" variant offers 48 × 1/8, a fraction of a whole number (4.NF.B.4), which a grade-3 learner has not met; replace it with 48 ÷ 8 = m written as a sentence, or move that variant up. The core here is one grade-3 idea and that is what the lesson teaches.
Standard: 3.OA.A.3 ("equal groups ... equations with a symbol for the unknown"), 3.OA.A.4 ("determine the unknown whole number in a multiplication or division equation"), 3.OA.B.6 ("understand division as an unknown-factor problem").
Foundations first: the shared opening. Then the fact this type lives on: one sharing picture can be written as two sentences. A whole of 81 cut into 9 equal pieces of n: "nine pieces of n make 81" is 9 × n = 81; "81 cut into 9 is n" is 81 ÷ 9 = n. Same picture, both true. And in a division the whole comes first: 9 ÷ 81 would cut nine cookies into eighty-one pieces.
Read: "shared equally" says equal pieces; "each" (gold) says the letter is the size of one piece. At a glance: the letter is smaller than the whole, and a right sentence must have the whole, 81, as the biggest amount in it.
Example: 81 cookies shared among 9 guests; n is each guest's share; choices 9 × n = 81, n − 9 = 81, 9 ÷ n = 81, 9 + n = 81. (1) Strip 81, bracket above; cut into 9 equal pieces, n (gold) in each, count bracket "9 guests". (2) Nine pieces of n make 81: 9 × n = 81, in the pieces' colors; say once that 81 ÷ 9 = n reads the same picture. (3) Check: nine nines are 81, so n = 9; nine pieces of 9 fit the strip.
Rule out: 9 + n adds guests to cookies; n − 9 = 81 makes one share bigger than all the cookies; 9 ÷ n = 81 shares nine cookies and gets more than nine. Dim together.
Quick second case (the "can NOT", a different ask): 36 books on 4 equal shelves, m in each. Three sentences read the picture: 4 × m = 36 (four pieces of m), 36 ÷ 4 = m (cut into four), 36 ÷ m = 4 (cut into pieces of m, four of them: count = whole ÷ size). 36 − 4 = m takes shelves away from books. That is the one that cannot be used.
Fast: draw the cut strip; any sentence that reads it is right. For "can NOT", find the ones that read it; the leftover choice is the answer.
Sense: a share is less than the whole; whole first in a division.
Check: find the letter and multiply back: 9 × 9 = 81.
Line: "Count times each makes the whole. Whole divided by count is each. Same picture, two sentences."
Trap: 9 ÷ n = 81 (numbers in story order, misconception 2). The strip is 81 long and the pieces are inside it; nine cannot be cut into eighty-one.
Kit: nothing beyond `equal`, brackets and `dim`.
Writer: strongest, because it is mixed (two asks, three readings of one picture, and the "can NOT" turns the rule-out into the method); a weak writer makes it "sharing means divide".

**`delivery-then-use` — Equal boxes arrive, some are used, how many remain.** Foundations 6 and 7. Open answer.
Weak seed: 3 boxes of 3, 1 used. The example below is the other variant.
Standard: 3.OA.D.8, "Solve two-step word problems using the four operations."
Foundations first: the shared opening, then foundation 7 in one line: in a two-step story the first picture's answer becomes a number in the second picture, and that middle number is written down before anything else happens.
Read: "remain" goes gold; the ? is pencils left; it counts pencils. At a glance: boxes and pencils are different things, so the delivery is equal pieces; the ? is less than the delivery, and 38 is almost all of 7 sixes, so few remain.
Example: 7 boxes with 6 pencils each; 38 used. (1) Seven equal pieces of 6, count bracket "7 boxes", whole bracket ?. (2) Count by sixes along the pieces: 6, 12, ... 42; the bracket reads 42; on the board, 7 × 6 = 42, and the film says: write the middle number down. (3) Second act: a cut line, 38 turns rust and slides out, a ghost stays; the rest is ?: 42 − 38. (4) Count up from 38: two to 40, two more to 42: 4 (family 6's way). (5) Put it back: 38 used and 4 left make 42; "4 pencils remain."
Fast: multiply, write it, subtract from it.
Sense: 38 is close to 42, so the answer is small; it is less than one box.
Check: 38 + 4 = 42.
Line: "Two steps. Write the middle number down."
Trap: answering 42, or losing it by the time 38 is read (misconception 5). The 42 is written on the bracket and on the board before the cut, and the question is reread before the answer is boxed.
Kit: `equal`, `cut` with ghost, `chain` from the first bracket to the second strip's whole (or the same strip reused, which is simpler and truer).
Writer: a capable cheaper model.

**`purchase-change` — Items at a price, a payment, the change.** Foundations 3 and 6. Open answer; one variant.
Standard: 3.OA.D.8 (two steps); 4.MD.A.2 names money, but these are whole dollars.
Foundations first: the shared opening; then change in a child's words: you hand over more than it costs, and what comes back is the change. On the strip: the payment is a whole; the cost is a part of it; the change is the rest. Cost + change = payment.
Read: "change" goes gold; the ? is dollars back, less than the payment. At a glance: 4 notebooks at 9 is four pieces of 9, 36, and 38 is just above it, so the change is small.
Example: 4 notebooks at 9 dollars each, pay 38. (1) Four equal pieces of 9, count bracket "4 notebooks"; count by nines: 36; written down. (2) A second strip, 38 (the payment), slides in above and lines up at the left; the cost strip sits under it; the guide rises from 36; the gap is ?. (3) Count up from the cost to the payment the way a cashier does: 36, 37, 38: 2 dollars. (4) Put it back: 36 for the notebooks and 2 back make 38.
Fast: multiply for the cost, then count up to the payment.
Sense: change is less than the payment, and here less than one notebook.
Check: 36 + 2 = 38.
Line: "Cost plus change is what you paid."
Trap: 38 − 9 = 29, subtracting the price once and skipping the count. Realistic, and the strip prevents it: there is no single 9 to take away, only a cost strip of four nines, which is drawn before anything is compared.
Kit: `equal`, `gap` between two aligned strips.
Writer: a capable cheaper model.

**`delivery-operations-slots` — Choose + or − for each change in a story.** Foundation 4. Slot answer.
Note: judged 4.OA.A.3 inside a grade-3 skill; three changes within 100 is a fair grade-3 stretch and the lesson should treat it as 3.NBT.A.2 arithmetic on a strip.
Standard: 4.OA.A.3 / 3.OA.D.8 in substance: "using the four operations" on a multistep story read in order.
Foundations first: a story in order is a strip that changes. Start at the left. Something arrives: a piece joins at the right end and the bracket grows. Something is sold, wilts, is given away: a piece is cut from the right and the bracket shrinks. Each change is one step, and the total is written after each one. The verb that tells what happened is what decides the sign: did tulips come in, or go out?
Read: the three changes, in order, each gold in turn. At a glance: the ? is tulips left; count the ins and the outs: 26 in, 44 out, so the shop ends below 42.
Example: 42 tulips; a delivery of 26; 22 sold in the morning, 22 more in the afternoon. (1) Strip 42. (2) "A delivery adds 26": a piece 26 slides in; bracket 68; slot 1 fills with +; board 42 + 26 = 68. (3) "22 sold": cut, rust out, ghost; 68 − 22 = 46; slot 2: −. (4) "22 more in the afternoon": the film stops on "more": more tulips were sold, so more leave; cut: 46 − 22 = 24; slot 3: −. (5) Read the finished sentence as the story.
Quick second case (a different first motion): 89 tulips; 15 wilt and are thrown away; then 22 and 7 arrive. The first change is a leave: cut 15 (74), join 22 (96), join 7 (103). −, +, +.
Fast: for each change ask "in or out?" and write the sign before the next change is read. Work left to right.
Sense: more in than out means the shop ends above where it started; more out means below.
Check: run the story backwards from the answer: 24 + 22 + 22 − 26 = 42.
Line: "In: add. Out: take away. One change at a time, in the story's order."
Trap: "22 more" read as add (keyword hunting, misconception 1), and "and 22 more" read as one event. The second 22 is cut away on screen as its own piece because the story says it was sold, and the film says that "more" here means more were sold.
Kit: the slot card: the problem's sentence with three operation slots that fill on the word (`{"do":"slot","n":2,"choice":"b"}`), beside the strip. `add`, `cut`, a bracket that updates.
Writer: a capable cheaper model.

**`story-expression-select` — Select every expression or sentence that finds the unknown.** Foundations 2 and 8. Select n of 5; two shapes.
Standard: 4.OA.A.3, "using the four operations ... equations with a letter standing for the unknown quantity"; the grade-3 variants are 3.OA.D.8.
Foundations first: parts make a whole, and the ? can be anywhere; several sentences can read one picture. Then foundation 8, drawn: two pieces that leave one at a time, in either order, or together as one grouped piece, leave the same length. A piece coming back is a different motion the story never had. And a "select" question is a count: as many right choices as it says, no more.
Read: the number to select goes gold. At a glance: in the first shape the ? is what is left, smaller than the whole; in the second the ? is a part, and the box must be a part in every right sentence.
Example A: 523 books; 133 shelved in the morning, 92 in the afternoon; select 3. (1) Strip 523. (2) Cut 133 (rust out, ghost), then 92; the rest is ?: 523 − 133 − 92, the pieces in order. (3) Cut the other way: 92 first, then 133: the same rest, 523 − 92 − 133. (4) The two rust pieces fuse into one, 225, and leave together: 523 − (133 + 92). (5) (523 − 133) − 92 is the first one with the first cut marked. Three found; the count matches. (6) Compute each: 298, 298, 298.
Rule out: 523 − 133 + 92 puts 92 books back on the cart; nothing came back. 133 + 92 − 523 takes the whole from the parts; you can't take away more than you have. Dim together.
Example B (the box as a part, a genuinely different shape): a book of 205 pages, 169 left; □ is pages already read; select 2. (1) Strip 205, two pieces: □ (gold) and 169. (2) The part and the rest make the whole: □ + 169 = 205; the whole minus the read pages leaves the rest: 205 − □ = 169. Both read the picture. (3) □ = 36; 169 + 36 = 205.
Rule out: □ − 169 = 205 and □ − 205 = 169 make the read pages bigger than the book; 205 + 169 = □ adds the two parts, so the box would be bigger than the book too. Dim together.
Fast: draw once; every sentence that reads the picture is right; stop when the count is reached. If two choices look alike, compute both; it is cheap.
Sense: what is left is less than the whole; a part is less than the whole.
Check: every selected choice gives the same number; the wrong ones give 482 or a number smaller than zero.
Line: "Pieces that leave one at a time or together leave the same amount. Something coming back is a different story."
Trap: 523 − 133 + 92 ("and" means plus, misconception 7) and missing 523 − 92 − 133 because its order differs from the story. Both pieces leave on screen in both orders, and the rest is visibly the same length; the "+ 92" is shown as a piece sliding back in, which the story never says.
Kit: `group` / `split`; `dim` for select-many with the selected choices marked; the cut run in two orders on one drawing.
Writer: strongest. The lesson is equivalence reasoning with several right answers; a weak writer picks one and recites the rest.

**`strip-diagram-match` — Read the app's strip diagram and choose its equation or story.** Foundations 2, 5, 6. Multiple choice; two directions, two kinds of diagram.
Misfiled, say first: linked to 1.OA.A.1 and 1.OA.D.8, but half its diagrams have equal parts and equations like 9 + 4 × n = 21, which is 3.OA.D.8 only; the two-part diagrams (9 = 4 + ?) are grade 1 and serve here as the warm-up. File it under 3.OA.D.8.
Standard: 3.OA.D.8, "Represent these problems using equations with a letter standing for the unknown quantity"; the two-part diagrams practice 1.OA.D.8.
Foundations first: how to read a strip diagram, because here the picture is given and the story is asked. The bracket is the whole. Each piece is a part. Pieces of the same length with one letter are equal parts: the same number in each. The ? or the letter is the unknown. Reading a diagram is saying what the pieces do: parts make the whole; equal pieces are count × letter. Then one line about the strip's scale: the pieces are drawn the size they are, so a piece shorter than the 9 is less than 9.
Read: nothing gold in a story, so the gold goes on the ?. At a glance: how many unknown pieces, and is the known part inside them or beside them?
Example A (diagram to equation): whole 21, one piece 9, four equal pieces n. (1) The app's own diagram, parts lit as they are named: bracket 21; piece 9; four pieces n. (2) Read left to right: 9 + n + n + n + n = 21; four n's is 4 × n: 9 + 4 × n = 21, in the pieces' colors. (3) Check: 4 × n = 12, n = 3; four pieces of 3 beside the 9 make 21, and the n pieces are shorter than the 9 as drawn.
Rule out: (9 + n) × 4 would put a 9 inside each of four pieces: the diagram has one 9. 9 + n = 21 has one unknown piece. 4 × n = 21 forgets the 9. Dim together.
Example B (diagram to story, a different direction): whole 12, one piece 4, two equal pieces ?. The story must tell the picture in order: 12 at first; 4 goes (loses 4); the rest is cut into 2 equal pieces (shared among 2 cousins); each is the ?. (1) Lit piece by piece as the right story is read. (2) Check: 12 − 4 = 8, 8 ÷ 2 = 4; two fours and a four make 12.
Rule out: "shares among 3": three pieces, not two. "Shares all 12 among 2": no 4 piece. "Gives 4 to each of 2 cousins; how many are left": then the two equal pieces would be 4s and the ? would be the rest. Dim together.
Quick case (the two-part diagrams): whole 9, part 4, part ?. The ? is a part, so the story is "has 4, gets some more, now has 9". "Gets 9 more" and "9 times as many" read the whole as a part. The equation form: 5 + n = 7, not 5 + 7 = n.
Fast: say the picture aloud as a sentence; the choice that says the same thing is it.
Sense: the letter's piece is shorter than the whole, and as drawn, shorter or longer than the known part.
Check: find the letter and see that the pieces add to the bracket.
Line: "Read the picture: parts make the whole. Equal pieces: count times the letter."
Trap: (9 + n) × 4 (the known part attached inside each piece, misconception 9), and "gets 9 more" (the whole read as a part). The lit diagram has one 9 and a bracket, so neither reading can be drawn.
Kit: the app's strip scene through `scene` (the lesson's picture must be the problem's picture), with pieces lit one at a time; `dim`. `strip-diagram-equation` follows from this concept with no new kit.
Writer: strongest. The lesson is reading a picture into words in both directions; the decoys are near-stories that differ in one piece.

### Grade 4: multistep-whole-stories (4.OA.A.3)

**`whole-pack-ribbon-choice` — Which way to buy costs less when packs come whole.** Foundations 3, 6, 9, 10. Multiple choice.
Variants, say first: in both generated variants Offer A wins by a wide margin, so the choices "B" and "Same cost" are never answers and the extra pack never decides. The type should generate cases where rounding up flips the answer (the quick second case below is one), or it teaches that the leftover does not matter.
Standard: 4.OA.A.3, "multistep word problems ... including problems in which remainders must be interpreted"; 4.MD.A.2 (money).
Foundations first: the deal and the leftover, in a child's words: lay 6-meter pieces along the ribbon you need; the stretch left at the end is shorter than a pack, and you cannot buy part of a pack, so the leftover means one more pack. Then: a cost is a strip too, and two costs lined up at the left show which is shorter.
Read: "costs less" goes gold; the ? is a choice, so both costs must be found. At a glance: the two numbers 19 and 6 count the same thing (meters), so 19 cut into sixes is a division with a leftover; Offer B is 19 pieces of 4 dollars.
Example: need 19 m; A: whole 6 m packs at 8 dollars; B: any length at 4 dollars a meter. (1) Strip 19 m. Deal 6-meter pieces: 6, 12, 18; 1 meter left, rust, shorter than a pack. Three packs are not enough; the fourth pack covers the last meter (with 5 m spare, said once): 4 packs. (2) Cost of A: four pieces of 8, counted 8, 16, 24, 32. (3) Cost of B: 19 pieces of 4: 19 × 4 = 76 (the stack, or 20 fours minus one four). (4) Two cost strips lined up at the left, A 32 above B 76; the gap is 44. A costs less. (5) Reread: with the leftover meter paid for, A is still cheaper.
Quick second case (where the leftover decides; beyond the generated variants on purpose): need 19 m; A: 6 m packs at 12 dollars; B: 2 dollars a meter. Three packs would be 36 and would look cheaper than B's 38, but three packs are 18 m; four packs are 48. B costs less. The extra pack flipped the answer.
Fast: divide the length by the pack; any leftover means one more pack; multiply each offer; line them up.
Sense: the number of packs is never less than meters ÷ pack length; a cost for 19 m at 4 a meter is near 80.
Check: 4 × 6 = 24 m is enough and 3 × 6 = 18 is not; 32 against 76.
Rule out: "Same cost" dims as soon as one cost is clearly bigger; the rest needs the computation.
Line: "Part of a pack is a whole pack. Then price each way and line them up."
Trap: buying three packs because 19 ÷ 6 is "3 remainder 1" and the remainder is dropped (misconception 6); in the flipped case this gives the wrong offer. The rust meter sticking out past the third pack is a meter of ribbon with no pack to cover it.
Kit: `deal` with the leftover; two cost strips with a `gap`, in a second figure with its own unit (meters and dollars never share a figure); `dim`.
Writer: strongest. A remainder that decides a purchase, two quantities, and a flipped case chosen on purpose.

**`estimate-reasonableness` — Round first, then do the story; or judge an answer by an estimate.** Foundation 10. Multiple choice; two asks.
Off grade, say first: the variant rounding 478.7 and 143.7 grams to whole numbers is 5.NBT.A.4 (rounding decimals), grade 5; move it. The decoy 448 for 7 × 63 + 8 × 22 is neither the exact total (617) nor a rounding of it; decoys should be the real errors (617 itself; 420 from stopping after one group; 80 from adding the rounded sizes and forgetting the piles).
Standard: 4.OA.A.3, "Assess the reasonableness of answers using mental computation and estimation strategies including rounding"; 3.NBT.A.1 for the rounding itself.
Foundations first: an estimate is a quick near answer, used to check, never written as the answer. Rounding is family 2's number line: which neighbor is closer; "nearest ten" ends in 0. The order is the whole lesson: round each number first, then do the story with the round numbers. The strip shows why: pieces of 63 and pieces of 60 are nearly the same length, so the whole is nearly the same.
Read: "estimate" or "reasonable" goes gold, and "round ... to the nearest ten". At a glance: an estimate from tens ends in 0, so any choice that does not is gone; the story is equal pieces (piles and cards), so the estimate is a product, and it must be bigger than the biggest group alone.
Example A: 7 piles of 63 cards and 8 piles of 22; nearest ten; choices 580, 80, 448, 420. (1) The number line: 63 drops between 60 and 70, nearer 60; 22 drops nearer 20. (2) The story with round numbers: seven pieces of 60 is 420; eight pieces of 20 is 160; end to end, 580. (3) Say what it means: the real total is near 580, and both numbers rounded down, so the real total is a bit more. (4) Check, once: 7 × 63 = 441, 8 × 22 = 176, 617; near 580, and above it, as promised.
Rule out: 448 does not end in 0: not an estimate from tens. 80 is smaller than seven piles of 60 alone. 420 is only the first group: the second was never added. Dim together.
Example B (the other ask, worked fully): Student H says 22 × 81 = 1,782; is it reasonable? (1) Round: 20 and 80; twenty pieces of 80 is 1,600. (2) Both numbers rounded down, so the real product is a little more than 1,600. 1,782 is near 1,600 and above it: reasonable. (3) The exact check is not needed; that is the point of an estimate.
Rule out: "because it is a whole number" is not a reason. "Cannot tell without the exact product": the estimate is how you tell. "Not reasonable, should be near 1,600": 1,782 is near 1,600; the estimate does not have to match.
