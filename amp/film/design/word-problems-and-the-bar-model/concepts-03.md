# Family 8 concepts, part 3: multistep whole stories (grade 4)

Six types, four of them one idea (the remainder trio plus the "between" kind of estimation). Every concept here stands on the family design's foundations 6 (equal pieces), 7 (the chain), 9 (the leftover) and 10 (estimate first), and uses the strip. Arithmetic is named, not retaught: a division fact, the stack, long division (family 7).

---

### `staar-multistep-stories` — Two-step stories with whole numbers or units

**First, what is wrong.** The type is a grab bag with four unrelated kinds under one name. One kind is off grade and should leave the type: 0.45 liter × 13 glasses, then liters to milliliters, is decimal multiplication (5.NBT.B.7) and a decimal conversion (5.MD.A.1); the lesson does not work it. One variant, 2 miles = 10,560 feet so 15 miles, is 5,280 × 15, a four-digit by two-digit product, past 4.NBT.B.5 (four digits by one digit, or two by two); re-seed the mile variant or split it as 5,280 × 10 + 5,280 × 5. The three remaining kinds (a conversion table; share then regroup; join then compare) are each worth a type of their own. Until the app splits them, this is a mixed lesson: one example per kind, each from its story, a recap that names the shapes and never says "the same steps".

**Standard.** 4.OA.A.3: "Solve multistep word problems posed with whole numbers and having whole-number answers using the four operations." 4.MD.A.1 for the table kind: "express measurements in a larger unit in terms of a smaller unit. Record measurement equivalents in a two-column table... (1, 12), (2, 24), (3, 36)." 4.NBT.B.4 for the stack.

**Foundations first.** Two steps means two pictures, and the first picture's answer is a number in the second (foundation 7): "write the middle number down" is the habit this whole type exists to build. Then the tell for every story here: do the two numbers count the same thing? Tickets and tickets: parts and a whole, or two strips lined up. Stickers and friends, yards and feet: equal pieces. A conversion table is defined on the strip: "1 yard is 3 feet" is one piece of 3; the row 6 yards = 18 feet is six of those pieces end to end; every row of the table is the same strip with more pieces. That is how the standard's table is "generated", and the lesson draws two rows from one strip before any example.

**Read.** Each story is read to the end, aloud; the question names the ? and what it counts (feet, not yards; piles of one friend, not of everyone; "how many more", a gap). At a glance: a table that gives a big row and asks a different row is "find one, then multiply"; "shared... then puts their stickers into" is two divisions, the second on one friend's share; "together... than" is a join and then a gap.

**Example A (conversion table, worked in full).** "A conversion table shows that 6 yards = 18 feet. How many feet are in 17 yards?" (1) The table beside the figure, two rows: 6 yards | 18 feet, and 1 yard | ?. (2) Strip of 6 equal pieces, whole 18 bracketed below: 18 ÷ 6 = 3 (a fact), 3 drops into every piece: one yard is 3 feet; the table's unit row fills. (3) Middle number written on the board: 3 feet in each yard. (4) A second strip at the same unit: 17 pieces of 3 with the break and a count bracket "17 yards", whole ?: 17 × 3 = 51. (5) Back to the story: 17 yards is 51 feet; the second strip is visibly almost three of the first.

**Example B (share, then regroup; quick).** 108 stickers among 9 friends, piles of 4, piles for one friend. Strip of 108 cut into 9 equal pieces: 12 each (108 ÷ 9). The 12 flies (chain) out of one piece and becomes the whole of a new short strip; dealt in 4s: three pieces, nothing left: 3. The question is reread: one friend's piles, not everyone's.

**Example C (join, then compare; quick).** Fall 380 and winter 377 join: stack, 757, the middle number written. Spring 416 lined up beneath, left edges together; the gap bracketed ?: stack, 757 − 416 = 341.

**Fast.** Different things: find the size of one piece first (divide), then build the strip the question asks for (multiply). Same thing: join, then line up. Always write the middle number before reading on.

**Sense.** A: 17 yards is nearly three sixes, so nearly 3 × 18 = 54; 51 is right beside it. B: 108 stickers over 9 friends is about 12 each, piles of 4 means about 3; fewer than 9 × 4 = 36. C: two plays together are about 2 × 380 = 760, around 340 more than 416.

**Check.** A: 51 ÷ 3 = 17 yards; the table's rows all fit the strip. B: 3 piles × 4 = 12; 9 × 12 = 108. C: 341 + 416 = 757 = 380 + 377.

**Line.** "Two steps. Write the middle number down."

**Trap.** Converting by adding: 6 yards is 18 feet, so 17 yards is 18 + 11 = 29 feet (the design's misconception 11; the exact error the 5 ft = 60 in variant invites: 60 + 6 = 66). Impossible once each yard is a piece of 3 and there are 17 of them. In B, dividing the whole by 4 (108 ÷ 4 = 27, every friend's piles) and answering the wrong ?; the chain draws the second strip from one friend's piece. In C, "how many more" read as add everything (1,173).

**Kit.** `table` (exists) filled from the strip; `strip` with `equal`, the break, `chain`; `stack`. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Strongest. Three kinds under one name is exactly where a weaker writer invents a recipe.

### `compatible-number-estimate` — Compatible numbers and front-end estimation

**First, what is wrong.** The "between which two whole numbers" kind is not an estimate; it is exact placement of a quotient between its two neighbors, and both its variants have two-digit divisors (238 ÷ 26, 3,631 ÷ 93), which is 5.NBT.B.6, not 4.NBT.B.6. Keep it as a stretch, but say so. Its decoy "between 9 and 11" is also true of 9.15; the question should ask for the two whole numbers next to each other. The "multiple of 4" instruction is right but unexplained; the lesson explains it.

**Standard.** 4.OA.A.3: "Assess the reasonableness of answers using mental computation and estimation strategies including rounding." 4.NBT.B.4, B.5 for the arithmetic.

**Foundations first.** Define an estimate in a child's words: a quick near answer, used to check, never written as the answer (foundation 10). Then three different ways to get one, each defined before its example: a *compatible number* is a nearby number that makes the arithmetic clean (25s like 4s, because four 25s make a hundred, the way four quarters make a dollar; 8s like 80 and 800); *front-end* keeps only the first digit of each number and makes the rest zeros, places kept; and "between which two whole numbers" is a deal: how many whole pieces fit, with something left over. The number line from family 2 shows where 61 sits between the multiples of 4 (60 and 64); the place-value chart from family 2 shows front-end as the lower places going muted.

**Read.** The question names the estimate it wants and the number to use ("closest multiple of 4", "front-end", "between which two"). At a glance: an estimate is a round-looking number; the exact-looking choice is never it; for front-end the answer has as many digits as the numbers do.

**Example A (compatible product, worked in full).** "The coach orders 61 packs with 25 wristbands in each pack. Estimate 61 × 25 with compatible numbers. Change 61 to the closest multiple of 4, then multiply by 25." (1) Why 4: four 25s make 100, four quarters a dollar; so a multiple of 4 packs is whole hundreds. (2) Number line 56 to 64, ticks at the multiples of 4; 61 drops: one from 60, three from 64: 60. (3) 60 packs is 15 groups of 4 packs; each group is 100: 1,500. (4) Rule out, then prove: 1,500.
Rule out: 150 and 15,000 have the wrong number of zeros (60 × 25 is more than 60 × 20 = 1,200, far less than ten thousand); 1,600 is 64 × 25, the farther multiple. Dim together.

**Example B (compatible quotient, quick).** 811 ÷ 8: 811 is 11 from 800 and 69 from 880; 800 ÷ 8 = 100. Rule out 10 and 1,000 by size (8 × 100 = 800 is already right beside 811); 110 is 880 ÷ 8, the farther multiple.

**Example C (front-end, quick).** 4,241 + 3,995 + 4,518: chart with the thousands column bright and the rest muted: 4,000 + 3,000 + 4,000 = 11,000. Said once: front-end drops parts of every number, so it always comes out a little low; the real total is above it. Rule out: 11 dropped the places (eleven what?); 12,754 is the exact total, not an estimate; 13,000 rounded 3,995 up to 4,000 and 4,518 up to 5,000, which is rounding, not front-end.

**Example D (between two whole numbers, quick).** 238 ÷ 26: a strip of 238 dealt in 26s: nine pieces (234) and a rust stub of 4, shorter than a piece: nine whole 26s and a bit: between 9 and 10. Rule out: "9 and 11" are not next-door neighbors; "10 and 11" would need ten 26s (260) to fit; "8 and 9" would need 238 to be under 234. One more beat for the big case: 3,631 ÷ 93: the estimate points to the neighborhood (3,600 ÷ 90 = 40), then test the fences: 93 × 40 = 3,720 is too much, 93 × 39 = 3,627 fits with 4 left: between 39 and 40.

**Fast.** Compatible: the nearest number in the family the question names, then a fact. Front-end: first digits, zeros, add. Between: estimate the neighborhood, multiply to find the two fences.

**Sense.** The estimate must sit near the exact (1,525; 101 R3; 12,754), and for front-end, below it.

**Check.** Count zeros; for the "between" kind, 26 × 9 ≤ 238 < 26 × 10.

**Line.** "An estimate is for checking. It is never the answer."

**Trap.** Rounding when the question said front-end (3,995 → 4,000 gives 13,000), and dropping the places (11): the chart keeps the columns and the muted digits are visibly still there. In the "between" kind, a child divides exactly and stops; the deal shows the stub, and the stub is why there are two neighbors.

**Kit.** `numberline` (specified, not built: drop, ticks at multiples); the `digits` chart with muted places (specified); `strip` with `deal`; `dim`. Nothing beyond the family's list.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper writer, from this concept: each kind has its picture, its example and its rule-out lines named; the only craft is giving each kind its own screen and never calling them one method.

### `strip-diagram-equation` — Read a strip diagram, choose the equation

**First.** Correctly placed (4.OA.A.3 and 3.OA.D.8). Its diagram is the app's own; the lesson's picture is the problem's picture, and the kit's `strip` must match it in proportion and labels.

**Standard.** 4.OA.A.3: "Represent these problems using equations with a letter standing for the unknown quantity." 3.OA.D.8, same clause.

**Foundations first.** What a strip diagram is and how it is built from the story: the whole's bracket above, each thing that happens as a piece in story order, equal pieces for "the same number in each", the letter on the piece we are looking for (a ? with a name; foundation 5). Three facts of reading, each shown live before the examples: several equal pieces of n write as 5 × n (n + n + n + n + n); a piece set aside stays on the strip but is not shared; and a bracket under part of the strip *is* the parentheses: do that part of the picture first (foundation 7). The way to choose: read each equation aloud as the story; one of them is the story.

**Read.** The letter is in every choice, so the question is only which sentence tells the story. At a glance: where is n on the diagram? In equal pieces inside the whole: count × n, plus any extra piece, equals the whole. One of the equal pieces of what is left after setting some aside: (whole − aside) ÷ count = n. The single piece left after known equal pieces: whole − count × size = n. Nothing else fits.

**Example A (set aside, then share; worked in full).** "Student E has 27 beads, sets aside 3, and shares the rest equally among 4 friends. Each friend gets n. Which equation matches the diagram?" (1) The diagram drawn as the story is reread: whole 27 bracketed above; a rust piece 3 at the left, set aside; the rest bracketed below "27 − 3". (2) The rest is cut into 4 equal pieces, each n. (3) Written in the pieces' colors: the rest is 27 − 3; shared four ways is ÷ 4; the bracket is the parentheses: (27 − 3) ÷ 4 = n. (4) Read back as the story.
Rule out: (27 + 3) ÷ 4 adds the beads she set aside back in ("set aside is not shared, and not added"); 27 ÷ 4 − 3 shares all 27 and then takes 3 from one friend; 27 − 3 ÷ 4 has no bracket, so it shares the 3 and takes that from 27, which is not the story. Dim together.

**Example B (equal pieces plus a loose piece; quick).** Student B: 5 boxes of n, a bag of 7, 47 in all. Five equal pieces n, then a piece 7, bracket 47: 5 × n + 7 = 47. Rule out: 5 × (n + 7) puts 7 in every box, but the 7 are one bag; 5 + n + 7 adds boxes to stickers (different things); 5 × n − 7 takes the bag away, but it is inside the 47.

**Example C (known equal pieces, n is the rest; quick).** Student F: 35 dollars, 3 items at 9. Whole 35; three rust pieces of 9; n the piece left: 35 − 3 × 9 = n. Rule out: (35 − 3) × 9 takes away 3 dollars, but 3 counts items; 35 + 3 × 9 makes money grow by spending; 35 − 3 + 9 the same, and adds dollars to items.

**Fast.** Find n on the diagram, say the equation as the story, keep the one that is true. For a missing single piece, compute it and put it back.

**Sense.** The pieces must make the whole: 4 × 6 + 3 = 27; 5 × 8 + 7 = 47; 3 × 9 + 8 = 35. A choice that makes n larger than the whole, or shares what was set aside, is not the picture.

**Check.** Solve the chosen equation and add the pieces back to the whole.

**Line.** "Parentheses are the bracket. Do that part of the picture first."

**Trap.** The right numbers in the right order with no parentheses: 27 − 3 ÷ 4 = n. Children write what they hear. The lesson draws the bracket under "the rest" before it is cut, so the parentheses are a thing on the picture, not a rule. In B, 5 × (n + 7): the extra attached to every box (design misconception 9); the bag is drawn as its own piece beside the boxes.

**Kit.** The app's strip-diagram scene through `scene`, or `strip` with `cut`, `partition`, brackets `side: s`; `dim`. Nothing new, once `strip` matches the app's diagram pixel for pixel.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper writer from this concept, after `fl-two-step-story-equation` (strongest writer) is approved and its staging can be copied: every rule-out sentence and every picture is named here.

## The remainder trio: `full-kits`, `seats-needed`, `kit-leftovers`

One author, one sitting, one shared opening. The three types are one picture asked three questions, and the standard's words are exactly that: 4.OA.A.3, "including problems in which remainders must be interpreted"; 4.NBT.B.6, "Find whole-number quotients and remainders with up to four-digit dividends and one-digit divisors."

**The shared foundation (the first shot of all three lessons).** Define the deal: a strip is the whole; pieces of a given size are laid along it one at a time on the counted words; the last stretch, too short for a piece, is the *leftover*, rust. "Left over" defined in a child's words: the ones that did not fill a group. Then the same tiny picture, 7 visitors and vans that seat 5, is asked the three questions in turn, answered by pointing, not by working: *How many vans are full?* One. *How many visitors are left standing?* Two. *How many vans are needed so nobody is left?* Two: the leftover needs a van too, with seats to spare. Then the line: "The leftover is real. The question says what it means." Each lesson then works its own question on its own screen.

**Shared Fast.** Divide once (a fact, or long division for four digits), then reread the question: full pieces, leftover, or pieces plus one if there is any leftover. For small numbers, counting up by the size until you pass the whole is a sure way (7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42: six bracelets).

**Shared Check.** Pieces × size + leftover = whole; and the leftover is smaller than a piece. A leftover as big as a piece means another piece fits; a child who writes 44 ÷ 7 = 5 R9 stopped dealing too early, and this check catches it.

**Shared Kit.** `strip` with `deal`, the rust leftover, the break for large counts; `longdiv` (curriculum roadmap item 3, not yet built) for the four-digit variants, in the standard layout, never rows of small equations. The main example in each lesson is small so the deal is seen; one quick four-digit case shows that long division gives the same two numbers.

**Shared Writer.** Capable cheaper writer, all three in one sitting from these concepts, so the deal and the opening are identical across the trio.

### `full-kits` — How many complete kits

**Read.** "Complete", "finished", "full" goes gold: only pieces that fill count. At a glance: the answer is a count of boxes, a whole number, a little less than the whole divided by the size.

**Example.** "Student J has 36 beads. Each bracelet holds exactly 7 beads. How many complete bracelets can be packed?" (1) Strip of 36. (2) Deal in 7s on the words: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35; five pieces. (3) The last stretch, one bead, turns rust: not enough for a bracelet. (4) The question is reread: complete bracelets: 5. The leftover bead is on the table, not on the answer line. (5) On the board, 36 ÷ 7 = 5 R 1, with the 5 and the 1 pointed to on the picture.
Quick second case (four digits): 7,630 crayons in boxes of 8: long division in the standard layout gives 953 R 6; the strip with the break shows 953 boxes and six loose crayons: 953.

**Sense.** 5 × 7 = 35 is just under 36, so five fit and six do not.

**Line.** "Only the full ones count."

**Trap.** Answering 6, "because there is a leftover, round up", or writing "5 R1" as if it were a number of bracelets. The rust bead is visibly not a bracelet, and the answer is read back in the story: five bracelets.

### `seats-needed` — The fewest vans to hold everyone

**Read.** "Hold them all", "so nothing is left out", "fewest... needed" goes gold: everyone rides. At a glance: at least the whole divided by the size, and one more if anything is left; never more than that.

**Example.** "The coach has 11 tennis balls. Each can holds exactly 3 tennis balls. What is the fewest cans needed to hold them all?" (1) Strip of 11. (2) Deal in 3s: 3, 6, 9; three full cans. (3) Two balls, rust: not a full can. (4) Reread: hold them *all*. The two balls need a can too: a fourth can is drawn as a dashed outline over the rust piece, with one empty spot. (5) 4 cans, one of them not full. Board: 11 ÷ 3 = 3 R 2, then 3 + 1 = 4.
Quick second case: 6,861 seeds in packets of 5: long division, 1,372 R 1; one seed left, so one more packet: 1,373.

**Sense.** Three cans hold 9, too few for 11; four hold 12, enough. The answer is the first count of cans whose capacity passes the whole.

**Check.** 4 × 3 = 12 ≥ 11 and 3 × 3 = 9 < 11.

**Line.** "If anyone is left over, they need a van too."

**Trap.** Answering 3 (the full cans) or "3 R2". The dashed fourth can with its empty spot is the beat the lesson rests on. Also the weak seed: 6 juice boxes in packs of 2 has no leftover and teaches nothing here; the lesson's example always has one.

### `kit-leftovers` — How many are left over

**Read.** "Left over", "do not fit", "left" goes gold: the answer is the rust stub, counted in beads, not bracelets. At a glance: it is smaller than one group, and it may be zero.

**Example.** "Student J has 44 beads. Each bracelet holds exactly 7 beads. How many beads are left over?" (1) Strip of 44. (2) Deal in 7s: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42; six bracelets. (3) The stub, two beads, turns rust; the question asks about this piece, not the six. (4) 2. Board: 44 ÷ 7 = 6 R 2, and the same thing as a subtraction the picture shows: 44 − 6 × 7 = 2.
Quick second case (the base case, wanted): 48 crayons in boxes of 8: the deal ends exactly on 48; nothing turns rust: 0 left over, and the answer is written as zero, not left blank. Four digits (5,263 photos, pages of 4): long division, 1,315 R 3: 3.

**Sense.** The leftover must be less than 7; if it were 7 or more, another bracelet would fit.

**Check.** 6 × 7 + 2 = 44, and 2 < 7.

**Line.** "The leftover is the piece too short to be a group."

**Trap.** Answering 6, the number of bracelets, because that is what the division "gives"; the lesson points to the rust piece as the question is reread. And a leftover bigger than a group (5 R 9) from stopping the deal early; the check "is it smaller than a bracelet?" is said every time.
