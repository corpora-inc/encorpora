# Word problems and the bar model, part 1: concepts for ten types

Every film here builds the five habits of the family design (read to the end; name the ?; draw what happens; let the ? choose the operation; compute, then put it back), so those are not repeated below. Each concept gives what this type alone must define, the one example, and the fastest sure way to its answer.

---

## Addition stories (`addition-stories`, grade 1)

### `story-sentence-match-20` — A story and its number sentence, both directions

**Standard.** 1.OA.A.1: "adding to, taking from ... with unknowns in all positions, e.g., by using ... drawings, and equations with a symbol for the unknown number." The variants practice adding to (result and change unknown), taking from (result and start unknown), and the symbol. "Comparing" appears only in decoy stories; the lesson builds the compare picture once, briefly, so the decoy can be recognized, since that is the standard's own case and the one where "more" misleads.

**Foundations first** (about a minute, slow). Student K's 8 shells, drawn as shells in a row; an outline grows round them, fills, the shells fade, the 8 stays: an amount is a length. More shells, a longer bar. The box: "the number we are looking for. It is not a number yet, so we draw a box", gold. Then one drawing, four questions: 6 shells leave (cut, rust, ghost), the rest is the box: `8 − 6 = □` written beside in the pieces' colors. Move the ?: "Student K had some. Gave away 6. Now has 2. How many at first?" the ? jumps to the whole bracket, the rest shows 2: `□ − 6 = 2`. "Student K has 2. Gets 6 more": the rust piece turns teal and slides back: `2 + 6 = □`. "Gets some more. Now has 8": `2 + □ = 8`. The four sentences stand one under another on the board. "One picture. The box sits on what the question asks."

**Read.** Story to sentence: the story gives two numbers and asks for a third. The sentence must hold those two numbers, and the box must sit where the asked number is. At a glance: a choice with a number the story never said is out, because the story's missing number is the box. Sentence to story: the story must use the sentence's two numbers, and the question must ask for the box. Where the box sits says what kind of story it is: box first, "had some"; box after a plus, "gets some more"; box after the equals, "how many now" or "left".

**Example 1 (story to sentence, the hardest position).** "Student M had some marbles. Student M gave away 7. Now Student M has 9 left. How many did Student M have at first?" Choices `□ + 7 = 16 | □ − 7 = 9 | 9 + □ = 16 | 16 − 9 = □`. (1) Read to the end; "at first" gold; the ? is how many Student M had before anything happened, so more than 9. (2) Three choices say 16; the story never said 16: they dim together. (3) Draw it anyway, because the drawing is the lesson: Student M's marbles, a strip with the ? on its bracket; 7 cut away, rust; 9 stay. `□ − 7 = 9` in colors; it is the one left standing. (4) Read it back as the story: "Student M had some, seven left, nine are left." (5) Put a number in: 9 and 7 make 16; 16 take away 7 is 9. True.

**Example 2 (sentence to story).** "Which story matches `7 + □ = 17`?" (1) Read the sentence as a picture first: a piece 7, something joins, the whole is 17: "Student B has 7. Gets some. Now 17." (2) Three stories say 10; the sentence has no 10: dim together. (3) Before trusting that, look at the "how many more than Student M" story: Student B 17 and Student M 10, lined up at the left, the gap. Nobody gets or gives; that story's sentence is `17 − 10 = □`. "More than" is a gap, not a plus. (4) The surviving story is read against the picture: 7, some more, now 17. True.

**Fast.** The story's two numbers are in the sentence; the box is where the question is. Then read the sentence back as the story.
**Sense.** A start is more than what is left; "now" after getting is more than before.
**Check.** Read it back; put a number in the box and see the story come true.
**Rule out.** Numbers the story never said, in one beat; then the box's position.
**Line.** "The box sits on what the question asks. Read the sentence back as the story."
**Trap.** Solving first, then hunting for the answer in a sentence (choosing `16 − 9 = □` because 16 is "the answer"); and "more" taken as plus in a compare story. The box stays empty until the end, and the dim says aloud "the story never said sixteen".
**Kit.** `bar` from counters drawn as shells; `mark`, `cut`, `gap`, `dim`; the sentences written in piece colors on the board as the ? moves. Early-grades voice: no strike-outs.
**Writer.** Strongest. The whole lesson is the choice of what to say as the ? moves.

### `story-equation-choice` — The equation with a letter, one or two steps, to 1,000

**Misfiled.** Numbers to 1,000, a letter, two-step stories: this is 3.OA.D.8 ("Represent these problems using equations with a letter standing for the unknown quantity"), filed in a grade-1 skill. Design it as grade 3 and move it to `multi-operation-stories`.

**Foundations first.** The strip in one line ("you drew this for shells"). The letter: a box with a name; n is still a number we do not know. Then the fact this type turns on: one picture has more than one true sentence. Parts and whole: 398 and 309 make 707, so `398 + 309 = 707`, `707 − 309 = 398`, `707 − 398 = 309` are all true of the same picture. The card offers one true sentence and three false ones, so the job is not "write mine and find it"; it is "read each as the picture and keep the true one".

**Read.** Who starts with what, what joins, what leaves, in order; where the letter sits. At a glance: if n is a part, n is smaller than the whole; if n is the end after getting and giving, compare the two changes.

**Example 1.** "Student C has 707 shells and gives away 309. Let n be the number Student C has left." Choices `309 − 707 = n | 707 + 309 = n | n − 707 = 309 | n + 309 = 707`. (1) Strip 707, bracket; 309 cut away, rust, to scale (a bit under half); the rest is n, gold. (2) The sentence a child writes, `707 − 309 = n`, is not on the card; say so. (3) In the picture n is smaller than 707. Three choices make n bigger than 707 or take 707 out of n: they dim together. (4) `n + 309 = 707`: what Student C has left and what he gave away make what he started with. True of the picture. (5) Compute n with the stack from family 6: 398. Read it with the number in: 398 + 309 = 707.

**Example 2 (two changes, the "and" trap).** "Student F has 222 beads, gets 310 more, and then gives away 290. Let n be the number now." The strip changes in order: 222; 310 joins; the in-between 532 written on the bracket; 290 cut away; n. The signs are the verbs in order: gets, plus; gives away, minus: `222 + 310 − 290 = n`. The other three each have a verb's sign wrong; they dim together ("in one of these, beads come back; in one, nothing leaves; in one, nothing comes"). Sense: got 310, gave 290: a little more than 222. n = 242.

Quick beat for the hidden start (Student D: some, gives away 368, has 287): the ? on the bracket, written in story order, `n − 368 = 287`; `287 − 368 = n` is the numbers-in-order trap and dies on "you can't give away more than you have".

**Fast.** Put the letter on the picture; read each choice as parts and whole; one survives.
**Sense.** A part is less than its whole; after get-then-give, the two changes nearly cancel.
**Check.** Compute n and read the chosen equation with the number in it.
**Rule out.** Choices that make a part bigger than the whole, or take the whole out of a part, in one beat.
**Line.** "A letter is a box with a name. Read the equation as the story."
**Trap.** Writing `707 − 309 = n`, not finding it, and guessing; numbers in story order (`309 − 707`); "and" as plus. The lesson says outright that one picture has several true sentences, and reads every choice against the picture.
**Kit.** Strip with letter labels; `add`, `cut`, `mark`, `dim`; the stack.
**Writer.** Strongest. The "right form is not your form" moment needs exact words.

### `missing-start-story` — How many were in the box at first

**Standard.** 1.OA.A.1, "taking from ... with unknowns in all positions ... by using objects, drawings". The start unknown.

**Foundations first.** "At first" means before anything happened: the whole box. "Taken out" and "stayed" are the two parts of what was there. The box (the symbol) is the number we are looking for.

**Read.** Two parts are given; the whole is asked. At a glance: the answer is more than both numbers, because both groups were in the box.

**Example.** "Some blocks were in a box. 11 were taken out and 8 stayed. How many were in the box at first?" (1) Draw what is in the box now: 8 blocks, teal, become a bar labeled 8. (2) Draw the 11 that were taken out, rust, outside the box. (3) "At first, they were all in the box": the 11 slide back in, end to end with the 8; the bracket over both is the ?. (4) `11 + 8 = □`. Count on from eleven: twelve, thirteen ... nineteen (or ten more than 8 is 18, one more, 19). (5) 19 blocks. Put it back: take the 11 out of 19, and 8 stay.

**Fast.** Add the two parts; the start is the whole.
**Sense.** Bigger than 11 and bigger than 8.
**Check.** 19 − 11 = 8.
**Line.** "At first, they were all in the box. Put them back."
**Trap.** 11 − 8 = 3, the best-documented grade-1 error: "taken out" sounds like subtraction. The motion of the 11 sliding back into the box makes the answer a sum before any sign is written.
**Kit.** Counters as blocks, `bar`, `join`, `mark`. Nothing new.
**Note.** The variant "1 was taken out" is fine for the app, a poor film example.
**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept: one shape, one motion.

## Two-step stories (`two-step-stories`, grade 2)

### `story-sentence-match-1000` — A story and its number sentence, to 1,000

**Standard.** 2.OA.A.1 says "within 100"; these variants reach 1,000 (2.NBT.B.7 for the size). Cap the generator at 100 or mark it a stretch. The clauses practiced: adding to, taking from and, newly, comparing (difference unknown), with the symbol.

**Foundations first.** The strip from grade 1 in one line (an amount is a length; the box sits on what the question asks; the ? can be anywhere). New: hundreds are labeled, not counted: 349 is a strip 349 long with 349 written in it. New: the compare picture, built live: Student A's 349 and Student I's 110, lined up at the left; a dashed guide rises from Student I's end; the overhang is "how many more Student A has". Nobody gets, nobody gives; it is a gap. The box's position in the sentence (first, after a plus, after the equals) says which story it tells.

**Read.** As in grade 1: the story's two numbers must be in the sentence, the box where the question is. At a glance, a number the story never said rules a choice out.

**Example 1 (compare).** "Student A has 349 shells. Student I has 110. How many more shells does Student A have than Student I?" Choices `□ − 110 = 239 | 349 − 110 = □ | 239 + □ = 349 | 239 − 110 = □`. (1) "How many more ... than" gold; the ? is the gap, counted in shells, smaller than Student A's 349. (2) Three choices say 239; the story never said 239: dim together. (3) Line up the two strips; the guide; the gap bracket with the ?. The gap is Student A's strip with Student I's taken off: `349 − 110 = □`, Student A teal, Student I rust. (4) Read it back: "Student A's 349, take away Student I's 110, is how many more Student A has." (5) Put a number in: Student I's 110 and the gap 239 make Student A's 349.

**Example 2 (sentence to story, box first).** "Which story matches `□ − 242 = 157`?" (1) Read the sentence as a picture: the box first means the start is unknown: "Student M had some. 242 leave. 157 are left." (2) Three stories say 399; the sentence does not: dim together. (3) The one left is read against the picture: had some, gave away 242, now 157, how many at first. True. One beat on the compare decoy (Student M 399, Student L 242): two strips lined up; its sentence would be `399 − 242 = □`, box last.

**Fast.** Numbers first, then the box's position, then read it back.
**Sense.** A gap is shorter than the longer strip; a start is more than what is left.
**Check.** Read the chosen sentence as the story; put a number in the box.
**Rule out.** A number the story never said; a box in the wrong position.
**Line.** "Line them up at the left. The gap is how many more. The box sits where the story hides the number."
**Trap.** "More" read as plus (349 + 110); solving first and choosing a sentence that holds 239. The lined-up strips show that nothing joins, and the box stays empty until the end.
**Kit.** Strip with labels not counts, `gap`, `mark`, `dim`.
**Writer.** Strongest.

### `two-step-change` — Two changes: find the end, the missing change, or the start

**Standard.** 2.OA.A.1, "one- and two-step word problems involving ... adding to, taking from ... with unknowns in all positions", within 100.

**Foundations first.** The strip that changes in order: start at the left; what joins attaches at the right; what leaves is cut from the right. "Then" means after; "now" is the end. The in-between number is written on the picture before the second change. A missing change is the gap between the in-between number and the end. A missing start is found by running the story backwards: undo the last change first.

**Read.** Which of the four numbers (start, first change, second change, end) is missing; the verbs in order. At a glance: more came than left, so the end is bigger than the start, or the reverse.

**Example 1 (end missing).** "A bus has 59 people. At a stop, 37 get off and then 22 get on. How many are on the bus now?" More got off than on, so fewer than 59. Strip 59; 37 cut away, rust, ghost; the rest bracketed: `59 − 37 = 22` (the stack from family 6), 22 written on the picture; then 22 join, teal; the bracket is the ?: `22 + 22 = 44`. (The 22 who stayed and the 22 who got on happen to match; the picture keeps them as two pieces.) 44, less than 59. Put it back: 44 − 22 = 22; 22 + 37 = 59.

**Example 2 (change missing).** "32 birds. 17 more came. Then some flew away, and now there are 33. How many flew away?" 32 + 17 = 49, written on the bracket. Now 33: the 33 bracket drawn from the left under the 49 strip; the rest, rust, is the ?: `49 − 33 = 16`. Sense: 33 is just one more than 32, so almost all 17 flew; 16 fits. Check: 32 + 17 − 16 = 33.

**Example 3 (start missing).** "Some marbles were in a bag. 26 were put in, then 5 taken out. Now there are 50. How many at first?" Draw forward: ? start, 26 joins, 5 leaves, end 50. Now run it backwards: the 5 come back (`50 + 5 = 55`); the 26 come out (`55 − 26 = 29`, one trade). Sense: more went in than came out, so the start is less than 50. Check forward: 29 + 26 = 55, 55 − 5 = 50.

**Fast.** In order, with the middle number written down; a missing change is middle against end; a missing start is the story run backwards from the end.
**Sense.** From the two changes: did the amount grow or shrink?
**Check.** Run the story the other way and land on the given number.
**Line.** "Two steps. Write the middle number down. To find the start, run the story backwards."
**Trap.** Losing the middle number (both changes done to 59); for the start, doing the changes as written to the end number (50 + 26 − 5 = 71). The ? piece at the left can only be reached by undoing.
**Kit.** `add`, `cut`, ghost, the reverse of a cut (a ghost returning as a piece), `mark`; the stack.
**Writer.** Strongest: three positions and the first "undo in reverse".

### `linked-time-equations` — Minutes left after two parts

**Flags.** 3.MD.A.1 material in a grade-2 skill; and the card defines `a` ("minutes left after the first part") that appears in no choice of the single-equation form. Either offer the pair or drop `a` there.

**Standard.** 3.MD.A.1: "Solve word problems involving addition and subtraction of time intervals in minutes, e.g., by representing the problem on a number line diagram"; 3.OA.D.8 for the letter. One story shape, two asks (a pair; one combined equation).

**Foundations first.** A strip of minutes is a number line with thickness: 0 at the left end, the whole length at the right; time passes left to right and never comes back. A part that has passed is a stretch shaded from the left; "left" is the stretch not yet reached. `a` is the minutes left after the first part; `m` is the answer. Parentheses mean "this part of the picture first".

**Read.** Whole, first part, second part; the ? is what remains, less than the whole minus the first part. At a glance: time only passes, so every right sentence takes away.

**Example (the pair).** "A movie is 90 minutes long. The first part lasts 33 minutes and the second 36 minutes. How many minutes are left?" Strip 0 to 90; 33 shaded rust from the left, "passed"; the rest bracketed `a`: `90 − 33 = a`, a = 57 written on the picture; 36 more shaded; the rest `m`: `a − 36 = m`, m = 21. Three choices add somewhere (a longer than the movie; time coming back; the parts minus the whole): "time only passes; these add": dim together. The survivor is read as the story.

Quick second screen (the combined ask, 85, 32, 27): `a` is replaced by what makes it: `(85 − 32) − 27 = m`; the parentheses are the first stretch shaded first; 53, then 26.

**Fast.** Whole minus the first part, then minus the second; the first answer is used in the second.
**Sense.** Less than the whole minus the first part; roughly 90 take away 70.
**Check.** The two parts together, 33 + 36 = 69, taken from 90 is 21: pieces that leave together or one at a time leave the same amount.
**Rule out.** Any choice with a plus: time does not come back.
**Line.** "Time only passes. Take the parts away in order."
**Trap.** "And" as plus: `(33 + 36)` then `− 90`, or `90 − 33 + 36`. The shading only ever grows.
**Kit.** Strip with 0 and end labels (a thin time scale under it); `chain` from the first bracket into the second equation.
**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept: one shape, two asks.

## Multiplicative stories (`multiplicative-stories`, grade 3)

### `story-operation-choice` — The equation for an equal-groups story

**Standard.** 3.OA.A.3, "equal groups, arrays, and measurement quantities, e.g., by ... equations with a symbol for the unknown", with 3.OA.A.1 and A.2 (interpreting 8 × 10 and 60 ÷ 10). The unknown number of groups never appears in the variants; the lesson gives it one beat because the standard names it.

**Foundations first.** "Each" and "equal" mean the same in every one. Equal pieces: a strip cut into pieces of one size, the size in each, a count bracket above. The three numbers of such a story: how many pieces, how big each, the whole. The same-thing test: 60 counts centimeters, 10 counts pieces; two numbers that count different things never make a sum or a difference. Whole missing: multiply. Size of a piece missing: divide. (How many pieces missing: divide too.)

**Read.** What each number counts; which of the three is the ?. At a glance: pieces are shorter than their whole; the whole is longer than any piece.

**Example 1.** "Student H cuts a 60-centimeter ribbon into 10 pieces of equal length. Which equation finds the length of each piece?" Strip 60, titled "ribbon, centimeters"; ten cut lines grow together; a ? in each piece; "10 pieces" above. The ? is a piece: `60 ÷ 10 = ?`. Dim `60 + 10` and `60 − 10` together: centimeters and pieces are different things. Dim `60 × 10`: a piece cannot be longer than the ribbon. Sense: each piece shorter than 60; 10 pieces of 6 would make 60.

**Example 2 (quick, whole missing).** "Student J sets up 8 rows of chairs with 10 chairs in each row." Eight equal pieces of 10 snap together; the bracket is the ?: `8 × 10 = ?`. Rows and chairs are different things; `8 + 10` and `10 − 8` dim; `10 ÷ 8` makes the whole smaller than one row.

**Fast.** Name what each number counts; ? on the whole: times; ? in a piece: divide.
**Sense.** Pieces shorter than the whole.
**Check.** Count times size gives the whole.
**Rule out.** Plus and minus between different things; a times that makes a piece bigger than its whole.
**Line.** "Same thing: add or take away. Groups the same size: times or divide. Whole missing: times. Piece missing: divide."
**Trap.** "Cuts" taken as take away (`60 − 10`); "in each" taken as add. The cut ribbon is still 60 long on screen; nothing left it.
**Kit.** `equal` on a strip, `partition`, `mark`, `dim`.
**Writer.** Cheaper model, from this concept.

### `multiplication-division-story` — Solve an equal-groups or measurement story

**Standard.** 3.OA.A.3 and 3.MD.A.2, "multiply, or divide to solve one-step word problems involving masses or volumes that are given in the same units". Open answer.

**Foundations first.** The same equal-pieces build as `story-operation-choice`, shorter if the learner has seen it; plus the fact family from family 7, named not retaught: 7 × 6 = 42 is also 42 ÷ 7 = 6.

**Read.** Count, size, whole; which is missing; what the ? counts (liters, not jugs). At a glance: a share is less than the whole; a whole is more than any share.

**Example 1 (size missing).** "Student M pours 42 liters of water equally into 7 jugs. How many liters go in each jug?" "Each" gold. Liters and jugs are different things: equal pieces. Strip 42, "water, liters"; 7 equal pieces, ? in each, "7 jugs" above. `42 ÷ 7 = ?`: seven times what is 42? Seven sixes. 6 appears in every piece. 6 liters. Put it back: seven jugs of 6 is 42.

**Example 2 (quick, whole missing).** "Student G cuts a ribbon into 7 pieces that are each 8 centimeters long, with none left over. How long was the ribbon?" Seven pieces of 8 snap together: `7 × 8 = 56`. 56 centimeters.

**Fast.** Whole missing: multiply; otherwise divide, with the fact.
**Sense.** Each jug holds less than 42; the whole is longer than one piece.
**Check.** 7 × 6 = 42.
**Line.** "Groups the same size. Whole missing: times. Piece missing: divide."
**Trap.** "In all" taken as add (7 bags of 10 apples: 7 + 10); numbers in story order (7 ÷ 42). Bags are not apples, and the whole is the longest thing in the picture.
**Kit.** Nothing beyond `equal` and `partition`.
**Writer.** Cheaper model.

### `story-operations-slots` — Fill the two operations

**Standard.** 3.OA.D.8, "Solve two-step word problems using the four operations", with 3.OA.A.3. The card shows the story's three numbers in order, two empty slots and the total after the equals; the learner picks ×, + or − for each slot.

**Foundations first.** Three kinds of piece on one strip: equal pieces (groups the same size: times), a different piece set beside them (extra: plus), a piece cut away (taken away: minus). The groups are built before anything is added or removed, which is why the times is worked first. What each number counts decides which kind of piece it is.

**Read.** Three numbers; what each counts; what joins or leaves. At a glance: the total given on the card is a check you can run.

**Example 1 (groups plus an extra).** "A coach buys 4 bags of tennis balls with 5 balls in each bag, and 5 more loose balls. Find the total." Four equal pieces of 5, "4 bags" above; then one more piece of 5 with no bag round it, a different ink; the bracket 25. "4 counts bags; 5 counts balls in a bag: times. 5 loose balls join: plus." Slots fill: `4 × 5 + 5 = 25`. Work it, groups first: 20, then 25: matches the card. Then the trap, aloud: 4 + 5 + 5 is 14, not 25, and you cannot add bags to balls.

**Example 2 (quick, groups minus).** "6 boxes of 9 markers; 8 given away." Six pieces of 9 make 54; 8 cut away: `6 × 9 − 8 = 46`.

**Example 3 (quick, groups of groups).** "2 courts, each with 2 bags, each bag 5 balls." A bag is a piece of 5; two make a court, 10; two courts, 20: `2 × 2 × 5 = 20`.

**Fast.** Ask what each number counts: groups the same size are times; then plus for what joins, minus for what leaves; work the times first; compare with the total on the card.
**Sense.** Groups plus an extra is more than the groups; groups minus some is less.
**Check.** The computed total equals the card's.
**Line.** "Groups the same size: times. Build the groups first. Then what joins: plus. What leaves: minus."
**Trap.** Grabbing every number with plus (4 + 5 + 5); treating the loose 5 as another group (4 × 5 × 5). The loose piece has no bag drawn round it.
**Kit.** Slots in the problem card that fill with a gold sign on the word; an `equal` run with one unequal piece appended; copies for groups of groups.
**Writer.** Cheaper model.

### `linked-division-equations` — Share, then share again or take several groups

**Standard.** 3.OA.D.8, "two-step word problems ... equations with a letter standing for the unknown quantity", with 3.OA.A.3. Two story shapes (share, then several of the shares; share, then share each again) and two asks (a pair; one combined equation).

**Foundations first.** The share picture: a strip cut into equal pieces, the letter in each. The first letter named ("let v be the number in one vase") is the first picture's ?, found with the story's numbers for that act. The second act is drawn on the same strip: several of the pieces bracketed together, or one piece cut again. The first answer flies into the second equation. "6 of the vases" is six pieces together: more than one vase, never less.

**Read.** What v counts; what f counts; whether f is bigger or smaller than v. At a glance: 6 of 7 vases is almost all the flowers, so f is a little under 63.

**Example 1 (share, then several groups).** "63 flowers are shared equally among 7 vases. How many flowers are in 6 of the vases? Let v be the number in one vase and f the answer. Which pair of equations finds the answer?" Strip 63, 7 equal pieces, v in each: `63 ÷ 7 = v`; seven nines: v = 9 written in every piece. Six pieces bracketed below, f: `v × 6 = f`; 9 × 6 = 54. Dim `63 ÷ 6` and `63 × 7` together: the first act is 63 shared among 7, and sharing makes smaller. Dim `v ÷ 6`: six vases hold more than one vase, not less. Check: 54 and the seventh vase's 9 make 63.

**Example 2 (share, then share again).** "A club of 48 members is split into two equal groups. Each group is then shared equally among 3 tables. How many at each table?" Strip 48 cut in two, a = 24; one piece cut into 3, t in each: `a ÷ 3 = t`, t = 8. Dim `48 − 2` and `48 × 2` together (splitting in two is not taking two away or doubling); dim `a × 3` (a table holds less than a group). Check: 8 × 3 = 24, 24 × 2 = 48.

Quick beat, the combined ask: replace a by what makes it: `(48 ÷ 2) ÷ 3 = t`; the parentheses are the first cut.

**Fast.** The first equation: the story's first act, ending in the first letter. The second: drawn on the picture, using that letter. Combined: the letter replaced.
**Sense.** Several shares together are more than one; a share of a share is less.
**Check.** Multiply back up to the whole.
**Line.** "The first answer is a number in the second picture."
**Trap.** `v ÷ 6`, the second act mirroring the first; `63 ÷ 6`, the "6 vases" grabbed as the divisor; in the second shape, `a × 3` because "3 tables". Each is drawn and seen to be the wrong size on the strip.
**Kit.** `equal`, `partition` on one piece, a bracket over several pieces, `chain`, `dim`.
**Writer.** Capable writer from this complete concept, with independent review of the realistic pitfalls and their explanations.
