# Money and financial literacy, part 1: the `money-values` types

Ten problem types under one grade-2 skill linked to 2.MD.C.8. Three strata sit here, as the family design said: the seven coin-and-bill types that are 2.MD.C.8; two dollars-and-cents story types that are grade 4 (4.MD.A.2); and three vocabulary types from Texas's financial-literacy strand that touch no coin and no symbol. The concepts below are written for where each type truly belongs, and each misfiling is said first. The order follows the ladder: coins and their worth, counting a set, dollars, making a dollar, choosing and comparing sets, then the stories, then the vocabulary.

Shared across the seven coin types, said once in each lesson that needs it and never re-taught: the four coins at true relative size with their worth drawn in blocks (penny a square, nickel five squares in a row, dime a rod, quarter a 5 × 5 block, dollar the flat); "count by worth, not by coin"; biggest first with the running total and a hop per coin on the line. Grade-2 voice: "cents" and "dollars", never "decimal"; nothing is struck out; the problem is read slowly with the coins in view.

---

### `coin-name-and-cent-value` — Name a coin, find a coin, write its value with ¢

**Standard.** 2.MD.C.8, the clause "using $ and ¢ symbols appropriately" (the ¢ half). Naming the coins is the K to 1 knowledge the standard assumes; keep the type, label it a prerequisite.

**Foundations first.** The four coins appear together, to scale, faces as the app draws them: the penny is the only copper-colored coin; of the three silver ones the dime is the smallest and has a ridged edge, the quarter the biggest with a ridged edge, the nickel in between with a thick smooth edge. Each coin is named, then morphs into its worth beside it: a square, five squares in a row, a rod, a 5 × 5 block. The dime is smaller than the nickel and its rod is longer than the nickel's row: worth is the picture, not the size of the metal. Then the two symbols: ¢ comes after the number (10¢, said "ten cents"); $ goes in front (a dollar bill is $1). One line: "cents sign after."

**Read.** Three askings under one name: a picture, name it; a name, find its picture; a value, write it with ¢. The asked word goes gold ("name", "shows a dime", "cent symbol"). At a glance: a copper coin is a penny before anything else is looked at; a value question has exactly one choice with ¢ after the number.

**The example.** "How do you write the value of this coin with the cent symbol?" (a quarter; choices 25, $25, ¢25, 25¢). (1) The coin: biggest silver coin, ridged edge: a quarter, and its 5 × 5 block appears: twenty-five cents. (2) Number first, then the sign right after: 25¢ is written, the ¢ appearing on the word "after". (3) 25¢. Two quick beats, the other askings: "Which picture shows a dime?" (three silver coins A, B, C): the smallest one with the ridged edge, B. "What is the name of this coin?" (copper): the penny, the only copper coin.

**Fast.** Copper means penny; silver means look at size; a value is the number then ¢.

**Sense.** A quarter is a quarter of a dollar; twenty-five of anything fits that, twenty-five dollars does not.

**Check.** Read the chosen one aloud: "twenty-five cents." Does the picture beside it say the same?

**Rule out.** 25 has no sign at all: cents or dollars? Gone. ¢25 has the sign in front: gone. $25 is twenty-five dollars, twenty-five flats, and the coin is a quarter of one flat: gone. All three dim in one beat. For a picture choice, the wrong color or size dims first.

**Line.** "Cents sign after: 25¢."

**Trap.** Writing ¢25 because $ goes in front; thinking the nickel is worth more than the dime because it is bigger. The rod beside the dime and the five squares beside the nickel end the second; "after" written on the word ends the first.

**Kit.** `money` draws the four coins at true size with ridged and smooth edges distinguishable; `worth` morph. Nothing more.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one picture, fixed facts, the concept names every beat.

### `coin-total` — How many cents in all (pilot #1)

**Standard.** 2.MD.C.8, including its own example clause: "If you have 2 dimes and 3 pennies, how many cents do you have?"

**Foundations first.** The four coins and their worth blocks, built in front of the learner (ten seconds a coin): the penny's square, the nickel's five, the dime's rod, the quarter's block. Then the one idea: a coin is counted by what it is worth, not as one. Three quarters are three coins and seventy-five cents. Biggest first, because the big hops are the easy count and the small ones land you exactly. The number line from 0 to 100 appears under the coins; every coin is a hop as long as its worth.

**Read.** "How many cents are these coins worth in all?" "In all" goes gold: one number for the whole pile, in cents, written with ¢. At a glance: there is a quarter and no bill, so the answer is past 25 and under a dollar.

**The example.** One quarter, two dimes, one nickel, five pennies, scattered (every coin in play; the nickel lands on a ten). (1) Sort by worth: the quarter slides to the front of a row, then the dimes, the nickel, the pennies; nothing is counted yet. (2) The quarter slides into the counted row, its tag 25¢ appears, the total reads 25, a hop +25¢ lands on 25. (3) The dimes: 35, 45, two hops of 10, visibly shorter. (4) The nickel: 50; its hop is half a dime hop, and it lands on a new ten. (5) The pennies one at a time: 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, five tiny hops. (6) 55¢.

**Fast.** Biggest first; say the running total; the last number said is the answer.

**Sense.** A quarter and two dimes alone are 45; a few small coins more; well under a dollar.

**Check.** Count the other way, smallest first: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5; 10; 20, 30; 55. Same number, both directions drawn as hops.

**Line.** "Count by worth, not by coin. Biggest first."

**Trap.** Counting nine coins as nine cents; carrying on by the old coin's worth after the coin changes (25, 50, 75 for a quarter and two dimes). The tag appears on each coin as it lands and the hop is drawn to its length: a dime after a quarter is visibly a shorter jump to 35.

**Kit.** `money` with `count` (order largest, one coin per spoken number, tag and total), `worth`, and `numberline` arcs labeled with the coin and drawn to the hop's length.

**Writer.** The strongest writer for the pilot copy: the picture is born here, the kit is new, and every later coin lesson inherits its look. The type itself is one method; after the pilot a cheaper writer can redo it.

### `money-total-dollars` — Bills and coins as dollars

**Standard.** 2.MD.C.8, word problems involving dollar bills and the four coins. The answer is entered as a bare decimal, so the "$ symbol" clause is not practiced; noted for the alignment list.

**Foundations first.** A dollar bill is one dollar, and one dollar is a hundred cents: the bill beside the flat. Bills count the dollars; coins count the cents. Cents always get two places after the point: five dollars and sixteen cents is 5.16; a hundred cents is one more dollar.

**Read.** "How much money is shown? Enter the amount in dollars." At a glance: the number of bills is the least the dollars can be; if the coins pass a hundred cents, there is one more dollar than bills.

**The example.** Four one-dollar bills, four quarters, one dime, one nickel, one penny (the coins make a dollar). (1) Bills first, each sliding into a row: 1, 2, 3, 4 dollars. (2) Coins biggest first: 25, 50, 75, 100, 110, 115, 116 cents. (3) A hundred cents is a dollar: the four quarters fuse into a flat beside the bills; 116 cents is 1 dollar and 16 cents. (4) 4 dollars and 1 more is 5 dollars; 16 cents left: 5.16, the 5 written under the bills and the flat, the 16 under the remaining coins. One quick beat for the two places: three bills, a nickel and a penny is 3.06, never 3.6.

**Fast.** Count bills; count coins in cents; every hundred cents moves one to the dollars; write dollars, point, two cents digits.

**Sense.** Four bills and more than a dollar in coins: over 5 and under 6.

**Check.** Take the four bills away: 1.16 is 116 cents; recount the coins.

**Line.** "A dollar is a hundred cents; cents get two places."

**Trap.** Writing 4.116 or 5.116 (the cents stuck on without trading the hundred); writing 3.6 for three dollars and six cents. The fuse of four quarters into a flat, and the two-place rename on the word, make both visible.

**Kit.** `money` with bills; `trade` of four quarters into a dollar; `unit` rename to dollars.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model.

### `make-a-dollar` — How many more cents make a dollar

**Standard.** 2.MD.C.8 word problems with coins; 2.NBT.B.5, "add and subtract within 100 using strategies based on place value ... and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction" (counting up to 100).

**Foundations first.** A dollar is a hundred cents, drawn as the flat: a hundred cells. Each coin's worth tiles onto it (a quarter covers a 5 × 5 corner, a dime a column, a nickel half a column, a penny a cell). What is not covered is what is missing. "How many more" is the empty part, found by counting up from what you have to 100 (family 6's missing part, named in one line).

**Read.** "One dollar is 100 cents. How many more cents do these coins need to make one dollar?" "More" goes gold: the answer is not the coins' total; it is the gap from that total to 100. At a glance: if the coins are under half a dollar, the answer is over 50.

**The example.** A quarter, a dime, a nickel, a penny. (1) Count: 25, 35, 40, 41; each coin's worth tiles onto the flat as it is counted: 41 cells covered. (2) The empty cells are the answer: 59 of them, but we count up rather than count cells. (3) On the line, from 41 to the next ten: 41 → 50 is 9 (the nine empty cells finishing that column light). (4) From 50 to 100 is 50 (five empty columns light). (5) 9 and 50: 59 cents more.

**Fast.** Count the coins; hop to the next ten; then hop by tens to 100; add the two hops.

**Sense.** 41 is less than half the flat, so more than half is missing: over 50.

**Check.** 41 + 59 = 100: the covered cells and the empty cells make the whole flat.

**Line.** "What you have, then the empty part."

**Trap.** Answering 41, the coins' total, because that is the number just said. The flat shows 41 covered and the question is about the rest; "more" is gold and the empty cells are counted. Subtracting 100 − 41 column by column is not taught here: counting up needs no borrowing.

**Kit.** `money.worth` tiling onto a flat part with the uncovered cells addressable; `numberline` count-up arcs.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model.

### `pay-exact-coins` — Which set is worth exactly the price

**Standard.** 2.MD.C.8.

**Foundations first.** Counting a set biggest first (one line, the picture already owned). "Exactly" means the same number, not close and not enough: 50¢ does not pay a 45¢ price exactly. One new glance: pennies are the only coin that changes the last digit; every other coin ends in 0 or 5, so a price ending in 5 needs 0 or 5 pennies, and a set with any other number of pennies cannot be it.

**Read.** "A toy car costs 45¢. Which set of coins is worth exactly 45¢?" "Exactly" goes gold; the price is read as the target on the line. At a glance: look at each set's pennies first.

**The example.** Set A: a quarter, two dimes, a nickel. Set B: four dimes, three pennies. Set C: a quarter, a dime, two nickels. (1) The glance: B has three pennies, so its total ends in 3; 45 ends in 5; B dims, with its total never counted. (2) A: 25, 35, 45, 50: past the price; the last hop overshoots the 45 mark on the line. A dims. (3) C: 25, 35, 40, 45: lands on the mark. (4) Set C.

**Fast.** Pennies first to throw out a set; count the rest biggest first and watch for the target.

**Sense.** Both A and C have a quarter and a dime; the difference is a nickel against two nickels: C must be the smaller by 5.

**Check.** Count C from the smallest coin: 5, 10, 20, 45. Same.

**Rule out.** The pennies digit, before any counting. Then any set that passes the target on its hops.

**Line.** "Pennies set the last digit."

**Trap.** Stopping Set A's count at 45 (quarter and two dimes) and choosing it, leaving the nickel uncounted; choosing the set with the most coins (B has seven). Every coin lands in the counted row before a set is judged, and B's seven coins are worth the least.

**Kit.** Three `money` parts with titles on one line; `dim`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model.

### `compare-money-sets` — Which set is worth the most, the least, or exactly an amount

**Standard.** 2.MD.C.8, word problems involving dollar bills and the four coins.

**Foundations first.** A bill is a dollar; a hundred cents is a dollar; count biggest first (one line each). Comparing two amounts of money is comparing dollars first, then cents, the way the place-value scan compares hundreds before tens (named in one line). Not taught, because it is not always true: "more pieces is more money" and "a bill always beats coins" (four quarters are a dollar too). Every set is counted.

**Read.** "Which set of bills and coins is worth exactly 1.28 dollars?" or "the most" or "the least"; the asked word goes gold. At a glance for "exactly": 1.28 ends in 8, so the set needs 3 or 8 pennies. For most and least: count all three, then compare dollars, then cents.

**The example.** Set A: a bill, a quarter, three pennies. Set B: a bill, a quarter, a nickel. Set C: four quarters, two dimes, three pennies. (1) Glance: B has no pennies, so its cents end in 0; it dims. (2) Count A: 1 dollar; 25, 26, 27, 28 cents: 1.28. (3) Count C: 25, 50, 75, 100, 110, 120, 121, 122, 123 cents; a hundred of them is a dollar: 1.23. (4) Scan: dollars tie; cents 28 against 23. Set A is exactly 1.28. One beat for the other askings: with all three totals written (1.28, 1.30, 1.23), "most" is B and "least" is C; the same three counts, a different last read.

**Fast.** Count each set once and write it as dollars and cents; then read the three like numbers, dollars first.

**Sense.** Set C has nine pieces and is worth the least: pieces do not decide. A and B share a bill and a quarter; three pennies against a nickel is the whole difference.

**Check.** Recount the chosen set from its smallest coin; for "most" or "least", read the three totals in order.

**Rule out.** For "exactly", the pennies digit dims a set before counting. For "most" or "least", a set is dimmed only after its total is written; no set is dismissed by its look.

**Line.** "Count every set. Dollars first, then cents."

**Trap.** Choosing the set with the most pieces (C) as the most money; counting the bill as one cent or skipping it. Every total is written under its set and C's nine pieces come to the smallest number.

**Kit.** Three `money` parts with titles; the four-quarter trade; `dim`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model.

### `fewest-coins-change` — The fewest bills and coins for the change (pilot #2)

**Standard.** 2.MD.C.8 (bills, quarters, dimes, nickels, pennies) for the coins-only variants; 4.MD.A.2, "the four operations to solve word problems involving ... money, including ... decimals", for 5.00 − 1.15. Misfiled in grade 2; the family design moves it to a grade-4 "dollars and cents stories" skill.

**Foundations first.** Change is the part of the payment that comes back because you paid more than it cost. A cashier finds it by counting up from the cost to the payment, small coins first to reach a round mark, then bigger ones: that gives the change and the coins in one motion. Fewest means nothing left to trade up: five pennies trade for a nickel, two nickels for a dime, two dimes and a nickel for a quarter, four quarters for a dollar; every trade keeps the amount and drops the count by one. The four trades are shown once as fuses, the total label never changing.

**Read.** "A pack of markers costs 1.15 dollars. Student D pays with a 5-dollar bill. The cashier gives change using 1-dollar bills, quarters, dimes, nickels and pennies. What is the fewest number of bills and coins?" "Fewest number" goes gold: the answer is a count of pieces, not an amount. At a glance: the change is a little under 4 dollars, so at least three bills plus some coins; more than three.

**The example.** 1.15 paid with 5.00. (1) The line from 1.15 toward 5.00; the cost marked, the payment far right. (2) Count up: a dime to 1.25 (hop +10¢, the dime lands in the change row); three quarters to 1.50, 1.75, 2.00; then the line zooms out, the point fixed, and three dollar bills hop to 3.00, 4.00, 5.00. (3) The change row: 3 bills, 3 quarters, 1 dime; its total 3.85. (4) The trade test: three quarters cannot make a dollar, one dime trades for nothing, no nickels, no pennies: nothing to trade up, so this is the fewest. (5) Count the pieces: 7.

A quick second case, because the coins-only variants read differently: "A cashier owes 78 cents in change, using quarters, dimes, nickels and pennies." Biggest that fits, from the top: three quarters (75), a dime does not fit (3 left), no nickel, three pennies: 6 coins. The same test: nothing trades up.

**Fast.** Count up from the cost: pennies to a 5 or 10, then to a quarter mark, quarters to a dollar, bills to the payment; count the pieces. Or: biggest piece that fits, repeatedly. Both always give the fewest.

**Sense.** The change is under the payment; the count is small, between the number of bills and a dozen.

**Check.** Count the change row biggest first: 1, 2, 3, 3.25, 3.50, 3.75, 3.85; and 3.85 + 1.15 = 5.00.

**Line.** "Count up from the cost. Fewest means nothing left to trade up."

**Trap.** Counting only the coins and answering 4 (the bills are pieces too); reaching 25 with two dimes and a nickel, three pieces where a quarter is one. The bills land in the change row and are counted with everything else; the trade test is run on the row before the count is boxed.

**Kit.** `money.change` (hops that each produce the piece spanning them, landing in a change row), `trade` fuses, `numberline` zoom with the point fixed.

**Writer.** The strongest writer: two methods that must be staged as one motion, and the trade test is the insight.

### `decimal-money-change` — Change, or how much more is needed (pilot #3)

**Standard.** 4.MD.A.2, "the four operations to solve word problems involving ... money, including problems involving ... decimals"; 5.NBT.B.7 in part, "add, subtract ... decimals to hundredths ... relate the strategy to a written method." Misfiled in grade 2; belongs in the grade-4 "dollars and cents stories" skill. The goal names four stories (change, total, still needed, unknown cost), but every variant the generator produced is one of two: change after buying (sometimes several of the same item), and how much more is needed. A total is a step inside the change story; an unknown cost is never asked. The lesson works what is produced; the alignment list should say the other two are unpracticed, or the generator should produce them.

**Foundations first.** Cents are hundredths of a dollar, so an amount gets two places; a bill is whole dollars and is written 10.00 before any work (place value's point line, named in one line). Change: you pay more than it costs, and the extra comes back; on a bar, the payment is the whole, the cost is a rust part, the change is the rest. How much more: two bars from one start, have and need; the gap is the answer. The sense check is always a direction: change is less than the payment; what is still needed is less than the price.

**Read.** The cue word goes gold and is turned into money words: "change" (out, then the rest of the payment), "each" (several of the same, so multiply before anything), "how much more" (the gap between have and need). One beat says the answer's shape: dollars and cents, two places.

**The example.** "Student C buys 2 stickers at 0.55 dollars each and a folder for 1.55 dollars. Student C pays with a 10-dollar bill. How much change does Student C get?" (1) The payment bar, 10.00. (2) The cost: two stickers are two equal parts of 0.55: 1.10; plus the folder: stack 1.10 + 1.55 with headers and the point line: 2.65; the rust part of the bar is labeled 2.65. (3) The rest is "?": 10.00 − 2.65, the bill written 10.00, hundredths 0 − 5 trades, tenths trade, 7.35. (4) The cashier's way agrees: from 2.65 count up 0.35 to 3.00 and 7.00 to 10.00: 7.35. (5) 7.35.

A second example, because the gap story is a different picture: "Student A has 2.16 dollars. A toy car costs 17.37 dollars. How much more money does Student A need?" Two bars from one start: have, 2.16, short; need, 17.37, long; the gap bracketed "?". 17.37 − 2.16 = 15.21 on the stack. The answer is more than she has, which is fine: she has little and needs much.

**Fast.** "Each": multiply first. Add the prices on the point. Change: payment minus cost, or count up from the cost. More needed: price minus what you have.

**Sense.** The cost is about 0.50 + 0.50 + 1.50 = 2.50, so change about 7.50; Student A has about 2 and needs about 17, so about 15 more.

**Check.** Change + cost = payment: 7.35 + 2.65 = 10.00. Have + more = price: 2.16 + 15.21 = 17.37.

**Line.** "Money in, money out, what is left. Change: count up from the cost."

**Trap.** Giving change for one sticker (forgetting "each" means two); writing 10 above 2.65 flush right so the columns are wrong. The two equal parts on the bar come before any adding, and the bill is written 10.00 under the place headers. In the gap story, adding the two numbers because two numbers are there: the aligned bars show the gap is shorter than the price.

**Kit.** Family 8's story bar (take, two bars aligned with a bracketed gap); `stack` with the `$` unit and whole-dollar rows padded to .00.

**Writer.** The strongest writer: a reading lesson with two pictures and several story structures.

### `early-money-decisions` — Income or gift, saving or spending, deposit or withdrawal; and the cost to make one item

**Standard.** Badly conceived: two types in one. The word kind (income, gift, saving, spending, deposit, withdrawal) is Texas financial literacy for grades 1 to 2 (the text extraction job should quote the clauses); it touches no coin and is not 2.MD.C.8. The cost kind is 3.OA.D.8, "two-step word problems using the four operations", with 3.OA.A.3's "equal groups" (3 boards at 12¢ each, then 9¢). Split them; until then, one lesson in two halves, and the cost half is the worked example.

**Foundations first.** The word half: one bar for your money, and each word is what happens to it. Income: the bar grows, and you worked for it. Gift: the bar grows, and you did not. Spending: a part leaves for a thing now. Saving: a part slides to a second bar labeled "savings", still yours, kept for later. Deposit: money into the bank, that slide. Withdrawal: money out of the bank, the slide back. Each word is shown by its motion, once, with the word gold. The cost half: "each" means equal groups, so 3 boards at 12¢ each is 12 + 12 + 12, written 3 × 12; the cost to make a thing is every part added.

**Read.** Word half: "Which one shows saving?" The word goes gold; the choices are read as what happens to the bar. At a glance: three choices that all buy something are the same action, so the odd one out is the answer. Cost half: "makes one birdhouse and buys 3 boards at 12¢ each, 1 box of nails for 9¢. How much does it cost?" "Each" goes gold; "cost to make" is every part added. At a glance: three boards cost more than one, so the answer is more than 12 + 9.

**The example.** Student E's birdhouse: 3 boards at 12¢ each, nails 9¢; choices 9¢, 21¢, 57¢, 45¢. (1) The bar: three equal parts labeled 12, 12, 12 (the boards appear above them), and a part labeled 9 (the nails); the bracket says "?". (2) Equal groups: 3 × 12: 12, 24, 36, hops on the line. (3) Plus the nails: 36 + 9 = 45. (4) 45¢. For the word half, one quick beat per asked word with its motion: "Student G puts $5 in a bank account to keep for later" is saving.

**Fast.** Equal groups first; then add the single parts.

**Sense.** Three boards are about three dimes and a bit, around 36; with nails, under 50¢.

**Check.** Take the nails back off: 45 − 9 = 36 = 12 + 12 + 12.

**Rule out.** 9¢ is only the nails; 21¢ is one board and the nails (the "each" was missed); 57¢ is a fourth board. All dim together, each named.

**Line.** "'Each' means groups of the same size."

**Trap.** Adding 12 + 9 once because "3" looks like a count of items already bought (21¢ is a choice); for the words, calling any bank action "saving" and confusing deposit with withdrawal by direction. The three equal parts are drawn before any sum; the deposit and withdrawal slides go opposite ways, labeled "in" and "out".

**Kit.** The story bar with equal parts; a second bar labeled "savings" that parts slide to and from. Nothing else.

**Writer.** The strongest writer: a mixed type whose quality is the choice of motion for each word.

### `staar-money-vocabulary` — Credit; fixed and variable expenses

**Standard.** Misfiled: not 2.MD.C.8 (no coins, no symbols, no amounts counted). It serves Texas financial literacy: grade 3's credit clause and grade 4's fixed and variable expenses (the extraction job should quote them). Move it to a grade 3 to 4 "earning, spending and saving" skill. A mixed type: two unrelated askings under one name, so the lesson teaches reading, with one example of each.

**Foundations first.** An expense is money going out every month; a family's expenses can be written in a table, months across, expenses down. Fixed means the same amount every month: read across the row and the numbers match; drawn as bars, they are all one height, with a flat line across their tops. Variable means the amount changes from month to month: bars of different heights. Fixed is about the amount, not about whether you must pay it. Credit: you take the thing home now and pay for it over time; the item comes home, and a rust bar for what you owe appears beside it and shrinks each month until it is gone. Paying with saved money is your own money, paid now. A tax is money you must pay to the government, paid now, not borrowed.

**Read.** For the table: "Which expense is a fixed expense, one that stays the same each month?" "Same each month" goes gold: read across each row, not down a column. At a glance: a row with any two different numbers is out. For credit: "Which is an example of using credit?" "Credit" goes gold: does the person get the thing before paying all of it?

**The example.** The table: rent 900, 900, 900; gym membership 30, 30, 30; groceries 410, 385, 442; water bill 48, 61, 53; choices gym membership, eating out... (as the app lists them, one of which is the fixed row). (1) Each row becomes bars beside the table on its word: groceries, three different heights; water, three different heights; gym, three equal bars and a flat line across. (2) Fixed means the same: gym membership. The second example: credit. (1) The four choices, read slowly. (2) "Student B takes a bike that costs 200 dollars home today and agrees to pay the store back over 8 months": the bike comes home now; a rust bar of 200 appears beside it; each month a slice leaves. (3) The others: tax is paid to the government now; saving is her own money kept; paying with savings is her own money, paid in full now. None is borrowing.

**Fast.** Fixed: scan each row for a difference. Credit: the thing now, the money later.

**Sense.** A fixed expense's three numbers are identical, not close; credit always leaves something owed.

**Check.** Read the chosen row's three numbers aloud; read the chosen sentence aloud and ask: does she still owe something after today?

**Rule out.** Table: any row with two different numbers dims at once (for "variable", any row of equal numbers). Credit: anything paid in full today dims together: the tax, the savings deposit, the cash purchase.

**Line.** "Fixed means the same amount every month. Credit means the thing now, the money later."

**Trap.** Calling groceries fixed because the family buys them every month; calling a card payment or a savings deposit "credit" because a bank is involved. The flat line across equal bars is the definition made visible; the rust debt bar that lingers after the bike is home is what credit looks like.

**Kit.** `table.bars` (a row drawn as to-scale bars with a flat line across equal heights); a debt bar that shrinks by months beside an item. Both small.

**Writer.** The strongest writer: the lesson is definitions made by motions, and a weak author describes "fixed" instead of showing the flat line.
