## Rational unit rates (grade 7)

### `mcas-fraction-rate-select` — Check stated speeds from fractional distances and times. Select 3 of 6.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.1: "Compute unit rates associated with ratios of fractions ... if a person walks 1/2 mile in each 1/4 hour, compute the unit rate as the complex fraction (1/2)/(1/4) miles per hour, equivalently 2 miles per hour." Exactly this, with mixed numbers.

**Foundations first.** Speed is a unit rate: miles for one hour; *per* means divide by the number of hours (foundation 4, named in one line). Build the double number line: hours on top (teal), miles below (rust), 0 over 0, then **stretch** so the top line shows quarter hours (0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1, 1 1/4). Say once: with fractions nothing changes; the 1 is still on the hour line, and the number across from it is the speed. A mixed number is a whole and a fraction: 3 1/8 miles is 25 eighths; written once so the board can divide it.

**Read.** "Each statement gives the hiker's speed on one day. Choose the 3 that are correct": three days, two statements per day, so compute each day's speed once and both of its statements are settled. At a glance, before any work: a day longer than an hour has a speed *less* than its distance; a day shorter than an hour has a speed *more* than its distance.

**The example** (variant 2). Monday 3 1/8 miles in 1 1/4 hours; Wednesday 1 9/16 miles in 1 1/4 hours; Friday 2 1/4 miles in 3/4 hour. Choices: Monday 2 1/2; Wednesday 1 61/64; Friday 3; Monday 2/5; Friday 1/3; Wednesday 1 1/4.
(1) Friday on the double number line: 3/4 hour sits three quarter-ticks along; 2 1/4 miles is across from it. **Divide to one**: three equal jumps of 1/4 hour each carry 3/4 mile each (2 1/4 ÷ 3). The unit tick at 1 hour is four jumps: 4 × 3/4 = 3 miles. Friday: 3 miles per hour.
(2) Say what the picture did, on the board: 9/4 ÷ 3/4 = 9/4 × 4/3 = 3. Dividing by 3/4 hour is "divide by the 3 quarters, multiply by the 4 quarters in an hour": that is why dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its flip.
(3) Monday, same line: 1 1/4 hours is five quarter-ticks; 25/8 ÷ 5 = 5/8 mile per quarter hour; × 4 = 20/8 = 2 1/2 miles per hour. Board: 25/8 ÷ 5/4 = 25/8 × 4/5 = 2 1/2.
(4) Wednesday: 25/16 ÷ 5 = 5/16 per quarter; × 4 = 20/16 = 1 1/4 miles per hour.
(5) Choose Monday 2 1/2, Friday 3, Wednesday 1 1/4.

**Fast.** Mixed numbers to fractions; distance × (flip of time); one computation per day.

**Sense.** Monday and Wednesday took more than an hour, so their speeds are below 3 1/8 and 1 9/16; Friday took less than an hour, so above 2 1/4. All three answers fit; hiker speeds of 1 to 3 miles per hour are believable.

**Check.** Speed × time = distance: 3 × 3/4 = 2 1/4; 2 1/2 × 1 1/4 = 25/8; 1 1/4 × 1 1/4 = 25/16.

**Rule out.** Monday 2/5 and Friday 1/3 are under one mile per hour for a hiker who walked more miles than hours: both dim at once (they are hours per mile, the division done backward). Wednesday 1 61/64 is more than the day's whole distance after more than an hour: dim (it is miles *times* hours). Then prove the three that remain. In other variants the decoy is the difference (5 1/4 − 1 1/2 = 3 3/4); the same sense check kills it.

**Line.** "Per means for one. Divide by the number of hours, however fractional."

**Trap.** Dividing the wrong way (time ÷ distance) and multiplying distance by time: both are the generator's decoys and both are what children do when the numbers are fractions and the picture is gone. The unit tick sits on the hour line, and the sense check names every wrong-way answer as too slow or too fast for a hiker.

**Kit.** `dnl` with `stretch` to quarter ticks, `divide` with the step written as a fraction, labels in mixed numbers.

**Writer.** Cheaper model: one method, one picture, decoys fully named here; the review must recompute all six fractions.

### `fraction-unit-rate` — A unit rate when the amounts are fractions.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.1, the complex-fraction clause, in three stories (recipe, lawns per hour, dollars per pound).

**Foundations first.** The same twenty-second build as above: the double number line, "per" names the bottom line, the unit tick at 1, **stretch** to fractional ticks. Define *at this rate*: the same amount for every equal piece of time.

**Read.** "How many lawns in 1 hour": the 1 is on the hour line; divide the lawns by the hours. At a glance: if the given time is less than an hour, an hour gives more than the given amount; if two of the given times are more than an hour, it gives less than twice.

**The example.** "Student J mows 1/2 of a lawn in 5/8 hour. How many lawns in 1 hour?" (answer 4/5).
(1) Top line in eighths of an hour; 5/8 is five ticks; 1/2 lawn across from it. (2) **Divide to one**: five equal jumps of 1/8 hour each carry 1/2 ÷ 5 = 1/10 lawn. (3) An hour is eight jumps: 8 × 1/10 = 8/10 = 4/5 lawn. (4) Board: 1/2 ÷ 5/8 = 1/2 × 8/5 = 8/10 = 4/5. The flip is the picture: divide by the 5 eighths, multiply by the 8 eighths in an hour.
Quick second case (a different shape: a whole amount, a unit-fraction time, a whole answer): "3 dollars for 1/2 pound. Price of 1 pound?" Pounds line 0, 1/2, 1; 3 dollars at the half; one more hop of the same size: 6. Dividing by 1/2 is doubling. The recipe case (3/4 ÷ 3/8 = 2) is this method unchanged.

**Fast.** Amount × flip of the "per" fraction, then simplify.

**Sense.** An hour is more than 5/8 hour, so more than 1/2 lawn; an hour is less than 10/8 hour, which would be a whole lawn, so less than 1. 4/5 sits between.

**Check.** Rate × time = amount: 4/5 × 5/8 = 20/40 = 1/2.

**Line.** "Divide by the pieces, multiply by the pieces in one."

**Trap.** Flipping the wrong fraction (1/2 × 5/8 = 5/16) or dividing the wrong way (5/8 ÷ 1/2 = 1 1/4, hours per lawn). The gold unit tick on the hour line decides which number is divided, and the sense bracket (between 1/2 and 1) throws out both.

**Kit.** `dnl` as above.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

## Proportional relationships (grade 7)

### `unit-price` — The cost of a different number of identical items.

**Misfiled.** One variant, whole numbers, 6.RP.A.3b ("unit pricing") as the catalog itself says; it is a grade-6 `unit-rates` type sitting in grade-7 `proportional-relationships`. Design it short and move it.

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3b: "Solve unit rate problems including those involving unit pricing."

**Foundations first.** The double number line, notebooks on top, dollars below; the unit price is the dollars across from 1 notebook. Two roads to any other count: *along* (8 to 9 is not a whole factor) or *across* (find one, multiply). Here the along factor is not whole, so find one.

**Read.** "At the same unit price, how many dollars do 9 notebooks cost?" The unit price is unsaid and must be found; at a glance the answer is a little more than 64 (one more notebook).

**The example.** 8 notebooks cost 64 dollars; 9 notebooks? (1) Line with 8 ticks; 64 under the 8. (2) Divide to one: 64 ÷ 8 = 8 dollars under the 1 tick, gold, boxed. (3) **Hop** to 9: one more arc, +1 above, +8 below: 72. Board: 9 × 8 = 72.

**Fast.** Unit price × count. When the new count is one away, add or subtract one unit price.

**Sense.** More notebooks, more money, by about one notebook's worth.

**Check.** 72 ÷ 9 = 8, the same unit price.

**Line.** "Find one, then multiply."

**Trap.** "One more notebook, one more dollar": 65. The hops on the dollar line are 8 apart, drawn to scale.

**Kit.** `dnl` `divide` and `hops`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model; the shortest lesson in the family.

### `variation-constant` — Direct variation: the factor, the constant, the new value.

**Off grade.** The template's inverse-variation cases are high-school algebra, in no grade-7 standard; the lesson teaches only direct variation (every generated variant above is direct) and the inverse cases should leave this grade.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.2b: "Identify the constant of proportionality (unit rate) in ... verbal descriptions of proportional relationships"; 7.RP.A.3: "Use proportional relationships to solve multistep ratio ... problems."

**Foundations first.** Define *varies directly*: y is always the same number times x, y = kx; it is the word "proportional" in older clothes. Build the ratio table from the story, one row per pair, and name the two multipliers on it: **across** (x to y, the constant k, drawn once per row) and **along** (one row to the next, the same blue arrow on both columns). Either road gets the answer.

**Read.** "What is d when t = 3 3/4?": a new row of the table with a "?" in it. At a glance: t went up by about half again (2 1/2 to 3 3/4), so d goes up by about half again. For "by what factor does y change": no amounts are given at all, so only the factor can be known, and it is the same factor x took.

**The example** (predict a value). "At a steady speed, distance d varies directly with time t. When t = 2 1/2, d = 3 1/3. What is d when t = 3 3/4?" Choices 12 1/2, 2 2/9, 5, 4 7/12.
(1) Table: t | d; row (2 1/2, 3 1/3); row (3 3/4, ?). (2) Along: 3 3/4 ÷ 2 1/2 = 15/4 × 2/5 = 3/2; the blue arrow ×3/2 is drawn down the t column and, in the same beat, down the d column: same to both. (3) d = 10/3 × 3/2 = 5. (4) The other road, across: k = 10/3 ÷ 5/2 = 4/3 miles per hour; 4/3 × 15/4 = 5. Both roads, one answer.
Quick second case (the factor alone). "y varies directly with x. By what factor does y change when x goes from 8 to 6?" Along: 6 ÷ 8 = 3/4; y is multiplied by 3/4 too. Nothing else can be known.

**Fast.** Along factor when it is a clean fraction (×3/2); otherwise k = y ÷ x, then k × new x. Both always work.

**Sense.** Direction first: t grew, so d grows; by half again, so about 5.

**Check.** The new row has the same k: 5 ÷ 15/4 = 4/3.

**Rule out.** 2 2/9 went down while t went up: gone. 12 1/2 is nearly four times, for a time that grew by half: gone (it is d × t). 4 7/12 is d plus the 1 1/4 hours t gained: adding where the table multiplies, the family's enemy; dims with that reason said aloud. In the factor case, "y decreases by 2" is an amount, not a factor, and no amount is known; 4/3 is the factor from 6 back to 8; 9/16 is 3/4 taken twice.

**Line.** "One multiplier for every row. Whatever you do to x, do to y."

**Trap.** Adding the difference (4 7/12) and "y goes down by 2": both are generated decoys and both are the additive misread. The blue arrow drawn on both columns at once makes "same to both" visible, and a plus can never be drawn as a factor.

**Kit.** `table` with `arrow` (fraction TeX, blue, drawn twice in one beat) and `fill` on the "?" cell.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: two kinds under one name, fractions throughout, four decoys that must each be named truthfully.

### `proportional-match-table` — Is each relationship proportional?

**Presentation.** The template's single variant prints only "Decide whether each relationship between x and y is proportional" with answers r1 to r4 and does not expose the four rows; the lesson cannot be checked against them. The rows must be stated in the template. The design below uses rows of the kinds the app's method names (an equation that needs rearranging, an equation in disguise, a list of pairs, a list with equal differences) and must be re-aimed once the real rows are visible.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.2a: "Decide whether two quantities are in a proportional relationship, e.g., by testing for equivalent ratios in a table."

**Foundations first.** Proportional means y = kx: one fixed number times x and nothing added (foundation 6). Two tests, one for each shape the row can take. For an **equation**: get y alone and look at the form. For a **list of pairs**: write y ÷ x beside every pair (the table's `derive` column); one shared value, and 0 with 0, means proportional. Say why a table of three pairs can prove "no" but an equation's form is what proves "yes".

**Read.** Four rows, each decided on its own; at a glance, any equation with a number added or subtracted after simplifying, and any pair list with (0, something not 0), is not proportional.

**The example.** Row 1: 2y = 8x. Both sides halved: y = 4x; k = 4; yes. Row 2: y = 3(x + 2) − 6. Multiply out: 3x + 6 − 6 = 3x; the added 2 was a disguise; yes. Row 3: (2, 6), (5, 15), (8, 24). The y ÷ x column: 3, 3, 3; yes. Row 4: (0, 2), (1, 5), (2, 8). It climbs by the same 3 each row, which feels proportional, but (0, 2): zero x with two y; y ÷ x is 5 then 4; no. On the side, the two pair lists drop onto a small plane (`link`): row 3's points sit on a line through the corner, row 4's on a line that misses it.

**Fast.** Equation: simplify to y alone; proportional only as y = (number) × x. Pairs: y ÷ x in every row, same number, and 0 goes with 0.

**Sense.** Put x = 0 into anything proportional and y must be 0; put x = 1 and y is k.

**Check.** Rows 1 to 3: x = 1 gives 4, 3, 3; x = 0 gives 0. Row 4: x = 0 gives 2.

**Line.** "Proportional: one multiplier, nothing added, zero with zero."

**Trap.** "It goes up by the same amount each row, so it is proportional" (row 4) and "there is a plus in it, so it is not" (row 2). The derived column and the dropped points settle the first; simplifying before judging settles the second.

**Kit.** `table` `derive`; graph `link` for the two pair lists.

**Writer.** Strongest writer until the template exposes its rows; choosing rows that are the real misreads is the whole lesson.

### `proportional-graph` — Read a proportional graph: a point, the unit rate, the equation, proportional or not. Pilot lesson 5.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.2d: "Explain what a point (x, y) on the graph of a proportional relationship means in terms of the situation, with special attention to the points (0, 0) and (1, r)"; 2b (unit rate from a graph); 2c (y = kx from a graph); 2a ("a straight line through the origin").

**Foundations first.** The **fold**, once and aloud: the double number line for apples (pounds 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 above; dollars 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 below) swings its bottom line up to stand as the y axis; each matched pair rises as a point; a line draws through them and through the corner where 0 met 0. Then the words: *axis*, each with its name and unit; a point is read across first, then up: (pounds, dollars); the *origin* (0, 0) is "no pounds, no dollars"; the height of the line at x = 1 is the unit rate; the *equation* is y = that height times x. The slope triangle from 0 to 1 is drawn in the two colors and labeled k.

**Read.** Four questions come from one picture. "What does (4, 8) mean": a sentence with both units, across first. "Unit rate": the height at 1, or any point's y ÷ x. "Which equation": y = (unit rate) x. "Is it proportional": a straight line, and through (0, 0). At a glance, for a point: 4 is pounds, 8 is dollars, because the axes say so.

**The example** (the point). "Apples are sold at one price per pound. The graph shows dollars for pounds. What does the labeled point (4, 8) mean?" (1) The dot at (4, 8); a dashed drop to 4 on the pounds axis and across to 8 on the dollars axis, each lighting on its word. (2) "4 pounds of apples cost 8 dollars." (3) The dot at (1, 2) lights: that is the unit rate, 2 dollars a pound, and it is not what (4, 8) says.
Second screen (unit rate, then equation, on the pump graph through (4, 20), x minutes, y liters). k = 20 ÷ 4 = 5 liters per minute; the point (1, 5) lights and the slope triangle shows the height 5 at x = 1; answer 5 liters per minute. `clear`: "Which equation matches the graph?" y = 5x, read as "liters are 5 times the minutes".
Third screen (proportional or not). A straight dollars-for-pounds line that crosses the y axis above 0 (a 3-dollar fee before any apples). Straight, yes; through (0, 0), no; so not proportional.

**Fast.** Point: across then up, with units. Rate: y ÷ x of any labeled point. Equation: y = rate × x. Proportional: straight *and* through the origin.

**Sense.** More pounds, more dollars; the unit rate is smaller than the y of a point past x = 1.

**Check.** Rate × x lands on the labeled point: 5 × 4 = 20; x = 0 in y = 5x gives 0, the origin.

**Rule out.** For the point: "8 pounds cost 4 dollars" swaps across and up; "every pound adds 4" and "the unit rate is 8" each take one coordinate of a point at x = 4 as the rate, which lives at x = 1: all three dim together. For the equation: y = 20x reads a y as the rate, y = x/5 flips, y = x + 5 adds: dim together. For proportional: "any graph that goes up" is the misconception, named and dimmed; "through (0, 0) but not straight" is refuted by looking.

**Line.** "Across, then up. The height at one is the rate. Through zero because zero goes with zero."

**Trap.** Swapping the axes, and reading a point's y as the rate. The axis names are on the picture and lit as the point is read, and (1, k) is drawn beside (4, 20) so the two are never confused.

**Kit.** `dnl` `fold`; graph `link`, `tri`, `dim`. The app's figure for the problems; the kit's own axes for the fold.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: the fold is the design, and four kinds must each get their own screen without the lesson growing dense.

### `proportional-rule-choice` — Which rule is proportional?

**Seed.** The only variant has three choices (y = −x + 8, y = x − 8, y = x); the design already flagged it as thin. Design it short.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.2a (decide whether proportional) from an equation; 2c (the form y = kx).

**Foundations first.** y = kx, nothing added; k can be any number, and 1 counts: y = x is y = 1 · x. The test that needs no algebra: put in x = 0; a proportional rule gives 0.

**Read.** "Which rule is proportional for all inputs": at a glance, any rule with a number added or taken away is out.

**The example.** The three rules drawn on one plane: y = x through the corner, teal; y = x − 8 crossing below the corner and y = −x + 8 crossing above, muted once dimmed. (1) x = 0: y = x gives 0; the others give −8 and 8. (2) y = x is 1 times x, nothing added: proportional, k = 1. Quick beat: a rule in disguise, y = 3x + 2 − 2, is y = 3x: simplify before judging.

**Fast.** Simplify; anything added or subtracted means no; what is left is k times x.

**Sense.** The line through the corner.

**Check.** (3, 3) and (7, 7): y ÷ x = 1 both times.

**Rule out.** The two rules with ±8 give 8 and −8 at x = 0: dim together.

**Line.** "Nothing added. Zero goes with zero."

**Trap.** "y = x has no number in front, so it can't be y = kx." The lesson writes y = 1 · x.

**Kit.** `plot`, `axes`, `dim` on figure parts and choices together.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

### `graph-of-proportional-rate` — Choose the graph of a rate.

**Presentation.** The choices are sentences about graphs ("A line starting at (0, 0) and passing through (2, 40)"); a graph question should show graphs. The lesson draws the four candidate lines on one plane with their named points; the app should too. 8.EE.B.5 is linked but is a grade-8 standard; this type serves 7.RP.A.2.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.2d (the points (0, 0) and (1, r)); 2b (the unit rate from a verbal description).

**Foundations first.** The graph of a rate: x is the thing counted by ("after x hours"), y the amount; it starts at (0, 0) because no time means no distance; every point is (t, rate × t), so the given pair is already a point, and (1, k) is the one with the rate as its height.

**Read.** "4 miles every 3 hours. Which graph shows miles y after x hours?" At a glance: the line starts at the corner and passes through (3, 4), hours across, miles up. Nothing need be computed to find the point.

**The example.** Cyclist, 4 miles every 3 hours. Choices: from (0, 1) through (3, 4); from (0, 0) through (1, 4); from (0, 0) through (3, 4); from (0, 0) through (4, 3). (1) Axes named hours and miles; the pair from the story drops as the point (3, 4), across 3, up 4. (2) The line from the corner through it. (3) k = 4 ÷ 3 = 4/3 miles per hour; (1, 4/3) is on that line, drawn to scale under (1, 4). (4) The third choice.
Quick second case (the given pair is not at the x the choices use): "30 words every half minute; words y after x minutes." Through (1/2, 30), so k = 60 words per minute, and at x = 2 the height is 120: (2, 120), not (2, 12).

**Fast.** Start at (0, 0); plot the story's own pair (count across, amount up); if the choices use another x, multiply the rate by it.

**Sense.** More hours, more miles, from zero.

**Check.** The chosen point's y ÷ x is the rate: 4 ÷ 3 = 4/3, the story's "4 miles every 3 hours".

**Rule out.** A line not starting at (0, 0) dims first (a cyclist who has gone a mile at time zero). (4, 3) swaps hours and miles. (1, 4) puts the 4 miles at 1 hour when the story said 3 hours. All three dim with their reasons; the one left is proved.

**Line.** "Start at zero. The story's pair is a point."

**Trap.** Putting the rate at the start, (0, 20), as if the line began at the rate: a real misread the generator uses. The corner is "nothing yet"; the rate is a height at 1, drawn there.

**Kit.** Several `plot` lines on one `axes`, `dot` and `label` for named points, `dim` on lines and choices together.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

## Percent change (grade 7)

### `mcas-markup-price-select` — Which selling prices are correct? Select 2 of 5.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3: "Use proportional relationships to solve multistep ratio and percent problems. Examples: ... markups and markdowns."

**Foundations first.** The percent bar from `percent-amounts-blanks`, extended: the cost is 100% (foundation 7). "40% below" **cuts** the last 40% away and 60% stays: one multiplier, 0.60. "40% above" **grows** the bar to 140%: one multiplier, 1.40. Ten percent is one tenth, so 60% is six tenths and 140% is fourteen tenths. Define *cost*, *selling price*, *below* and *above the cost*.

**Read.** "40 percent below the cost. Which 2 items have the correct selling price?" Every correct price is 60% of its cost: less than the cost, more than half of it. At a glance that alone sorts most of the list.

**The example.** Lamp 240 → 144; Backpack 220 → 88; Jacket 60 → 36; Helmet 280 → 112; Tent 100 → 140.
(1) The bar for one item, cut to 60%: the rust 40% hatches and leaves, 60% stays teal, 100% still labeled at the cost. (2) Tent: 140 is more than 100, up on a markdown: gone. (3) Backpack: 88 of 220 is less than half; Helmet: 112 of 280 is less than half: both gone; they are 40% *of* the cost, the part that left. (4) Lamp: the amount scale is relabeled with 240 under 100%; 10% is 24; 60% is 144: the stated price lands on the 60% mark. (5) Jacket: 10% is 6; 60% is 36: lands. (6) Lamp and Jacket.
Quick second case (above): "40 percent above." The bar grows to 140%; Tent 260 → 364 (26 × 14) and Jacket 160 → 224 (16 × 14) land; Helmet 180 → 220 added 40 dollars, not 40% (that would be 72, price 252); Scooter and Backpack went down.

**Fast.** One multiplier: 0.60 or 1.40; or ten-percent blocks.

**Sense.** Below: between half the cost and the cost. Above: between the cost and one and a half times it.

**Check.** 144 ÷ 240 = 0.6; or 144 + 96 = 240.

**Rule out.** Wrong direction dims first; then any price under half the cost (on a 40% markdown) or over one and a half times it (on a 40% markup); then prove the rest.

**Line.** "Forty percent off leaves sixty percent. Forty percent on makes one hundred forty."

**Trap.** Taking "40% below" as "the price is 40% of the cost" (88 for 220), and adding 40 dollars for 40% (220 for 180). The bar shows what stays, not what leaves; and a percent on the bar is a share of the cost, never a fixed amount.

**Kit.** `bar` `cut`, `grow`, `max`; `whole` relabeled per item on the word (marks recompute); a stated price dropped onto the amount scale to land on the mark or miss it.

**Writer.** Cheaper model; the review recomputes all ten prices.

### `credit-card-balance` — Monthly interest and the balance after payments.

**Misfiled.** APR, finance charges and debt reduction are financial literacy (`financial-literacy-middle`, family 21); keep the percent bar and the register from here and move the type. Nothing in it is off grade: it is a percent of an amount, to the cent, repeated.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3 ("simple interest ... fees"); 6.RP.A.3c ("Find a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100").

**Foundations first.** Define *balance* (what is owed), *APR* (the yearly rate; the problem does the ÷ 12 and names the monthly rate: read it), *interest* (a percent of the balance at the start of the month, added before the payment), *payment* (taken off after). Build the **register**: Month | Start | Interest | Payment | End, one row per month, End = Start + Interest − Payment, and next month's Start is this month's End. Rounding to the cent, one beat: 9.48285 keeps two places, the third digit 2 says stay at 9.48.

**Read.** Four questions on one register: this month's interest (one cell); the end balance (one row); the balance after two months (two rows, the second from the first's End); how much of a payment reduces the debt (payment minus interest). At a glance: the end balance is the start minus the payment, nudged up by a small interest; a few percent of a few hundred is a few dollars.

**The example.** Student P: 1.5% a month, start 632.19, pays 74. Choices 567.67, 548.71, 558.19, 641.67.
(1) The row: Start 632.19. (2) Interest: 1% of 632.19 is 6.32; half of that is 3.16; together 9.48 (the stack: 632.19 × 0.015 = 9.48285, rounded to 9.48). (3) 632.19 + 9.48 = 641.67, the stack with the point. (4) − 74 = 567.67, the End cell fills. (5) 567.67 dollars.
Quick second case (two months: the rebase). Student T: 2.25%, start 2,930.32, pays 78 twice. Row 1: interest 65.93, end 2,918.25. Row 2 starts at 2,918.25, not 2,930.32: interest 65.66, end 2,905.91. The month-2 interest is of the new balance; the decoy 2,906.18 charged month 1's interest twice.
Quick third case (where the payment goes). Student O: 1.25% of 1,816.30 is 22.70; of the 74 paid, 22.70 covers the interest and 74 − 22.70 = 51.30 reduces the debt.

**Fast.** Interest = monthly rate × start, to the cent; End = Start + Interest − Payment; next month starts from End.

**Sense.** A little above start minus payment: 632.19 − 74 = 558.19, plus about 9.

**Check.** End − Start = Interest − Payment: 567.67 − 632.19 = −64.52 = 9.48 − 74.

**Rule out.** 641.67 went up after a 74-dollar payment: gone. 558.19 is exactly the payment with no interest: gone. 548.71 took the interest off instead of on: gone. For Student O: 96.70 and 74 are not less than the payment; 22.70 is the interest, not what is left.

**Line.** "Interest goes on, the payment comes off, and next month starts from what is left."

**Trap.** Subtracting the interest, skipping it, charging the first month's interest again in month 2, and counting the whole payment as debt paid: each is a generated decoy and each is what a learner does without the register.
