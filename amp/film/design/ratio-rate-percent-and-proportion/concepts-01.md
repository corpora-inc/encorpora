# Ratio, rate, percent and proportion: concepts, part 1

Ten problem types: the five of `multiplicative-comparison` (grade 4) and five of `ratios` (grade 6). Every one is drawn with the tape from the family design (section 2); the grade-4 lessons speak in the early-grades voice. Each concept names its foundations from section 1 by number.

Two kit notes that run across the whole set, so they are said once here. (1) Select-many types (`mcas-comparison-equations-select`, `mcas-ratio-statements-select`, `equivalent-ratios-select`) need a **pick** mark for a choice pill (a teal check, the pill brightens) alongside `dim`, and the count of picked pills shown against the count asked ("2 of 3"). The place-value design specified `dim` only. (2) The tape's box must be able to carry a **letter** (`n`) or a **brace reading "n"**, not only a number or "?", since the grade-4 equations types name the unknown.

## Grade 4: multiplicative-comparison (4.OA.A.1, 4.OA.A.2)

Shared foundation, built live in the first lesson of the skill and named in one line in the rest: **times as many means copies; more than means extra.** The picture: Student K's 3 songs as one teal box; Student R's 6 as two copies arriving on "one, two"; then the same 6 drawn again as Student K's box with a rust piece of 3 on the end labeled "+3". Same numbers, two sentences. The amount after "as" is the one that gets copied.

### `times-as-many-comparison` — Say a comparison with "times as many"

**Standard.** 4.OA.A.1: "Interpret a multiplication equation as a comparison, e.g., interpret 35 = 5 × 7 as a statement that 35 is 5 times as many as 7… Represent verbal statements of multiplicative comparisons as multiplication equations." And 4.OA.A.2's clause "distinguishing multiplicative comparison from additive comparison": every variant has an additive decoy. Three cases under one name: two counts to a sentence; a count and "k times as many" to an expression; an expression (4 × 15) to a sentence. Pilot lesson 1.

**Foundations first.** The shared picture above, built in full (copies against extra, both to scale). Define *times as many* ("copies of the other amount"), *more than* ("extra on top of the other amount"), and say which amount is copied: the one after "as". Say what an expression is: "7 × 2 is a short way to write seven copies of two."

**Read.** Case one asks for the number of copies: how many of the small strip fit in the big one. Case two asks for the expression: the number before "times" times the amount after "as". Case three reads the expression as a sentence. At a glance: the person with more is the one who "has times as many"; the factor is smaller than the big count; the big count is the small count times the factor.

**The example (case one, full).** "Student G has 8 pencils. Student F has 40 pencils. Which sentence compares the amounts correctly with 'times as many'?" Choices read aloud. (1) "times as many" goes gold; Student F has more, so the sentence will start "Student F has…". (2) Tape: Student G one teal box, labeled 8, brace 8. Student F's strip, rust, empty, brace 40. (3) Copies of Student G's box arrive on the count: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40. Five copies fill the strip exactly. (4) "Student F has 5 times as many pencils as Student G." Write 40 = 5 × 8 under the tape: forty is five copies of eight. (5) One beat on the extra picture: Student G's box with a rust piece to 40, labeled +32. "Student F has 32 *more*. That is a different sentence." (6) Box the sentence.

**Quick cases.** "Student I has 2 stickers. Student A has 7 times as many." Student I's box of 2; seven copies; the expression is 7 × 2; "7 × 7 copies the wrong number." Then "Student E saved 15 dollars. Student I saved 4 × 15 dollars": read the expression as the picture, four copies of 15: Student I saved 4 times as many as Student E; "15 more" would be one box and a piece.

**Fast.** Two counts: divide the big one by the small one. "k times as many as A": k × A. "k × A": k times as many as the one who has A.

**Sense.** The one who has more is named first in a "times as many" sentence. The factor is less than the big count.

**Check.** Multiply back: 5 × 8 = 40.

**Rule out.** A "times as many" sentence that names the person with less first dims (Student G's strip is shorter). A sentence whose number is one of the two counts (8 times, 3 times) dims: the factor is a count of copies, not a count of pencils. The "more" sentence is tested as extra, and 32 is not the number on it (or 3 is, not 2). For expressions: anything with + or ÷ dims together ("no extra, no sharing: copies"); k × k copies the wrong amount.

**Line.** "Times as many means copies. More than means extra."

**Trap.** "Student F has 32 times as many" or "Student R has 2 more": the difference and the factor swapped. The two pictures sit side by side once, and 32 is visibly the extra piece, 5 the number of copies.

**Kit.** `tape` with `copy` on counted words, `extra` labeled with the difference, `dim`. The two pictures of one pair of numbers need two tapes on one card, or a tape that morphs from copies to extra.

**Writer.** Strongest. The lesson is the family's seed picture; if copies-against-extra is not drawn beautifully here, nothing after it works.

### `comparison-amount` — Find the other amount

**Standard.** 4.OA.A.2: "Multiply or divide to solve word problems involving multiplicative comparison, e.g., by using drawings and equations with a symbol for the unknown number." 4.NBT.B.6 for the quotient (406 ÷ 7 "using strategies based on place value").

**Foundations first.** One line: times as many means copies; the amount after "as" is the one copied. Then the tape with the unknown in both places, the simple one first as the definition: "Student M has 4 pencils. Student T has 2 times as many." Student M's box of 4, two copies, 8. Then the question is turned round: when the long strip is the known one, the box is what we want.

**Read.** Who has more? The one with "times as many" (Student S has 7 times as many as Student Q: Student S has more). Is the known amount the long strip or the box? If the box: multiply. If the long strip: divide. At a glance: 406 ÷ 7 is a bit under 60, since 7 × 60 = 420.

**The example.** "Student S has 406 tokens. Student S has 7 times as many tokens as Student Q. How many tokens does Student Q have?" (1) "times as many" gold; Student S has more. (2) Tape: Student Q one gold box, "?"; Student S seven copies of that box, brace 406. The copies are drawn first and empty: the shape is known before the number. (3) "406 is seven copies of one box. One box is 406 shared into 7." Board: 406 ÷ 7, worked by place: 7 × 50 = 350, 56 left, 7 × 8 = 56, so 58. (4) `fill`: 58 lands in Student Q's box, copies itself into Student S's seven boxes, Student S's brace counts 58, 116, … 406. (5) Student Q has 58 tokens.

**Fast.** Smaller known: multiply by k. Larger known: divide by k. The tape says which before any arithmetic.

**Sense.** Student Q has fewer than Student S; 58 is under 60 and 7 × 60 = 420 is just over 406.

**Check.** 7 × 58 = 406 on the board, under the tape.

**Line.** "Copies: multiply to get the long strip, divide to get the box."

**Trap.** 406 × 7 = 2,842 because the problem says "times". The tape is drawn before the arithmetic: Student S's strip is the long one, and Student Q's box is one of seven, so the answer cannot be bigger than 406.

**Kit.** `tape` with a "?" box and `fill` counting the brace. Division shown as partial quotients on the board (place-value strategy, as the standard says) until `longdiv` lands; never rows of small equations with no structure.

**Writer.** Cheaper model. One method, one picture, the concept names the example and the trap.

### `comparison-factor` — How many times as many

**Standard.** 4.OA.A.2 ("Multiply or divide to solve word problems involving multiplicative comparison") and 4.OA.A.1 ("35 is 5 times as many as 7"). Note: only one variant is generated (Student Q 9, Student S 45); the template should vary the numbers. This is case one of `times-as-many-comparison` as an open answer; the lesson is short and leans on that one.

**Foundations first.** One line: times as many means copies; the question asks how many copies.

**Read.** "Student S has how many times as many tokens as Student Q?" asks for the number of copies of Student Q's 9 that make 45. At a glance: the answer is a small number (5 × 9 = 45 is a known fact), and it is a count of copies, not of tokens.

**The example.** "Student Q has 9 tokens. Student S has 45 tokens. Student S has how many times as many tokens as Student Q?" (1) Student Q one box of 9. (2) Student S's strip, brace 45, filled by copies on the count 9, 18, 27, 36, 45: five. (3) 45 ÷ 9 = 5 on the board, said as "how many nines make forty-five". (4) 5 times as many.

**Fast.** Divide the big count by the small one.

**Sense.** The factor is less than both counts' difference here (36) and far less than 45; it is a count of copies.

**Check.** 5 × 9 = 45.

**Line.** "How many copies of the small one make the big one? Divide."

**Trap.** 45 − 9 = 36: "how many more" answered instead. The copies arrive one by one and the count is what is asked; the extra piece is never drawn in this lesson because the question has no "more" in it.

**Kit.** `tape` with `copy`. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

### `times-as-many-counts` — Complete the sentence from a tally

**Standard.** 4.OA.A.1 ("Represent verbal statements of multiplicative comparisons as multiplication equations") in reverse: the counts come from a table, the sentence is built with its three slots. Three counts, exactly one pair in a whole-number ratio; the third count is a distractor. Slots: big, k, small, each chosen from a short list.

**Foundations first.** The tally table is read in one line (a bundle of five, then the loose marks; family 32 owns it). Then: times as many means copies, and a sentence "A is k times as many as B" names the big amount first, the copies in the middle, the copied amount last.

**Read.** Which pair of counts fits whole copies? At a glance: the big count goes in the first slot, so the first slot is the largest of the pair; k is small; the third count is left out of the sentence entirely.

**The example.** Student H's tally of balls: tennis 12, soccer 36, basketball 20 (numbers chosen so that only one pair divides: 36 ÷ 12 = 3; 36 ÷ 20 and 20 ÷ 12 are not whole). (1) Read the three tallies, the count labels ticking by fives. (2) Tape: tennis one box of 12. Soccer's strip, brace 36: copies 12, 24, 36: three whole copies. (3) Basketball's strip, brace 20, against the box of 12: one copy and a piece left over, not a whole number of copies; against boxes of 20, soccer is one copy and a piece too. Basketball is not in this sentence. (4) Fill the slots on the board as they are said: "Soccer balls (big) is 3 (k) times as many as tennis balls (small)." Write 36 = 3 × 12 under it.

**Fast.** Divide the biggest count by each of the others; the one that comes out whole is the pair, and the quotient is k.

**Sense.** The first slot holds the bigger count; k times the last slot gives the first.

**Check.** k × small = big: 3 × 12 = 36.

**Line.** "Big first, copies in the middle, the copied amount last."

**Trap.** Slots reversed: "tennis is 3 times as many as soccer." The tape shows which strip is long, and the sentence is filled while pointing at it. Also a miscounted tally (a bundle of five read as four); the count label ticks by fives.

**Kit.** The app's own tally table through `scene` if `facts.ts` exposes it; otherwise `table` needs tally marks (bundles of five) as cell content. A three-slot sentence on the board that fills slot by slot on the word.

**Writer.** Cheaper model, once the example numbers are fixed in the concept as above (the generator's tables are not in the facts; the writer must pick a table with exactly one whole-number pair and say so).

### `mcas-comparison-equations-select` — Which equations find n?

**Standard.** 4.OA.A.2: "…using drawings and equations with a symbol for the unknown number to represent the problem, distinguishing multiplicative comparison from additive comparison." 4.OA.A.1: "Interpret a multiplication equation as a comparison." The "more than" stories are 2.OA.A.1's "comparing, with unknowns in all positions." A mixed type: two story kinds (copies, extra) and two unknown positions (the box, the long strip), select 3 of 6.

**Foundations first.** The shared picture (copies against extra). Then the idea this type lives on: **one tape, several sentences.** A tape that says "42 is seven copies of n" can be written as a multiplication (n × 7 = 42) or as a division (42 ÷ 7 = n), and the equals sign means "the same amount," so 42 = n × 7 is the same sentence with the sides swapped. Say that the letter stands for the number we do not know yet, and that a true equation stays true whichever side the letter sits on.

**Read.** Copies or extra? The story's words decide ("times as many" or "more than", gold). Is n the box or the long strip? At a glance: in a copies story with n the small amount, every correct equation has × or ÷ and makes n smaller than the given; any equation with + or − is out before thinking.

**The example (copies, n is the box).** "A group has 42 students. There are 7 times as many students as chaperones. Let n be the number of chaperones. Which 3 equations can be used to find n?" Six choices read aloud. (1) Tape: chaperones one gold box labeled n; students seven copies, brace 42. (2) Say the tape: "42 is seven copies of n." (3) Write it three ways on the board, each said as a sentence and pointed at the tape: n × 7 = 42 (seven n's make 42); 42 = n × 7 (the same, sides swapped); 42 ÷ n = 7 (how many n's fit in 42? seven) and 42 ÷ 7 = n (42 shared into 7 boxes is one box). (4) Dim together: 42 = n + 7 ("extra, not copies"); then 42 × 7 = n and n ÷ 42 = 7 ("both make n bigger than 42, and n is the small box"). (5) Pick the three that remain. (6) Prove: one box is 42 ÷ 7 = 6; put 6 into each picked equation: 42 ÷ 6 = 7, 42 = 6 × 7, 6 × 7 = 42. All true. Exactly three.

**Second example (extra, n is the long strip), briefer.** "A group has 12 chaperones. There are 7 more students than chaperones. Let n be the number of students." Tape: chaperones 12; students the same 12 with a rust extra +7, brace n. "n is 12 and 7 more": 12 + 7 = n, 7 + 12 = n, and n − 7 = 12 (take the extra off and you are back at 12). Dim: n + 12 = 7 and 7 − 12 = n (n is more than 12, not less); n = 12 − 7 (makes n smaller). Prove with n = 19. A quick beat for the remaining shape the learner will meet, copies with n the long strip (15 pictures, 5 times as many pages): n brace on five copies, n = 15 × 5, n ÷ 5 = 15, n ÷ 15 = 5.

**Fast.** Find n from the tape first (one division or one addition), then put it into every equation and keep the true ones. Always works, and a fourth grader can do it. The reading of the tape is what makes it quick.

**Sense.** In a copies story the box is smaller than the strip; in an extra story the strip is bigger by exactly the extra. Any equation that puts n on the wrong side of the given dims.

**Check.** Each picked equation is true with n's value; exactly three picked.

**Rule out.** Wrong operation family first (+ and − in a copies story, × and ÷ in an extra story) dims together. Then direction: equations that make n bigger when n is the box, or smaller when n is the strip.

**Line.** "One tape, three sentences: multiply, or divide, or swap the sides."

**Trap.** Picking 42 × 7 = n because the story says "times"; refusing 42 = n × 7 because the letter is on the right; picking n ÷ 42 = 7 because "division is in it". The tape is drawn before the equations are read, so n is visibly one box of seven, and the three true equations are each said as the tape.

**Kit.** `tape` with a box carrying `n`, `extra`, `dim`, the pick mark and "k of 3" count. Board: equations as choices, a substitution line under each picked one.

**Writer.** Strongest. Mixed type teaching reading; the equations must be read as sentences about the picture, and the writer must choose both unknown positions well.

## Grade 6: ratios (6.RP.A.1, 6.RP.A.2, 6.RP.A.3)

Shared foundation, built live in the first lesson of the skill (`ratio-pair-blanks`, the pilot) and named in one line in the rest: **a ratio says how two amounts go together, "for every"; the order of the words is the order of the numbers; equivalent ratios are the same tape with a different box.**

### `ratio-missing` — The matching amount for a new batch

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3: "Use ratio and rate reasoning to solve real-world and mathematical problems, e.g., by reasoning about … tape diagrams." The seed "5 blue for every 5 white, 10 white" is degenerate (anything equals itself); seed 6:5 with 7 white, which is also the case with a non-whole along factor.

**Foundations first.** Define *ratio* and *for every*: "6 cups of blue for every 5 cups of white" is one batch, and both numbers belong to it. Draw the tape: blue six teal boxes, white five rust boxes, braces 6 and 5, "each box is 1 cup" in the gold box. Then say what a new batch is: the same tape, a different box. Rescale once (box 2: braces become 12 and 10) so the learner sees the shape stay.

**Read.** Which number does the new amount match? "7 cups of white" matches the 5, because both are white. At a glance: this mix has more blue than white, so the blue is a bit more than 7; and 7 white is more than one batch (5) and less than two (10), so blue is between 6 and 12.

**The example.** "A paint mix uses 6 cups of blue for every 5 cups of white. How many cups of blue are needed for 7 cups of white at the same ratio? Give the answer as a decimal." (1) Tape as built; the white brace changes to 7, the blue brace to "?". (2) "Seven cups fill the five white boxes. One box is 7 ÷ 5." Board: 7 ÷ 5 = 1.4. (3) `fill`: 1.4 lands in the gold box, copies into every box of both strips; the blue brace counts 1.4, 2.8, … 8.4. (4) 8.4 cups of blue. (5) One beat on the other multiplier: a blue arrow ×1.4 drawn on both strips, "the whole batch was multiplied by 1.4; do the same to both."

**Quick case.** "1 cup of blue for every 8 cups of white, 40 cups of white." The along factor is whole and seen at a glance: 40 is 5 batches (40 ÷ 8 = 5), so blue is 1 × 5 = 5. Box value 5, braces 5 and 40.

**Fast.** Box = the given amount ÷ its ratio number. Answer = the other ratio number × box. When the given amount is a whole number of batches, the along factor is the same thing faster.

**Sense.** More blue than white in this mix; 8.4 is a bit more than 7. Between 6 and 12.

**Check.** Same box both ways: 8.4 ÷ 6 = 1.4 and 7 ÷ 5 = 1.4. Or 8.4 to 7 scales to 6 to 5.

**Line.** "Find one box and you have them all."

**Trap.** Adding: 7 is "2 more" than 5, so blue is 6 + 2 = 8. On the tape, one more cup in each strip is a box of 1.4 in white and a box of 1.33 in blue: the strips no longer share a box, and the mix is a different color. Also dividing by the wrong ratio number (7 ÷ 6); the brace 7 sits over the white strip.

**Kit.** `tape` with `fill` of a decimal box and a blue along arrow on both strips (`rescale` draws it). Nothing new.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

### `equivalent-ratios-select` — Which groups keep the ratio?

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3 ("reasoning about tables of equivalent ratios, tape diagrams"). Select 2 or 3 of 5. The app's method mentions cross-multiplying for non-multiples; the lesson does not teach it (section 3): the same-box test decides every pair, whole or not.

**Foundations first.** Ratio and "for every" in one line. Then *equivalent ratios*, drawn: 2 students for every 5 adults is two teal boxes and five rust boxes; rescale the box from 1 to 2 (4 and 10) to 7 (14 and 35): one tape, three sizes. Then the test that decides any pair: **both strips must have the same box.** Box from the students = students ÷ 2; box from the adults = adults ÷ 5; same number, same tape.

**Read.** "Keep this ratio" asks whether the group is the same tape with a bigger box. At a glance: the given ratio is in simplest form, so a group that keeps it is a whole number of batches: an odd number of students, or adults not a multiple of 5, is gone at once (5 students; 8 adults; 6 adults).

**The example.** "Volunteer groups need 2 students for every 5 adults. Select the 3 groups that keep this ratio." Five choices read. (1) Tape 2 : 5, box 1. (2) `test` each pair, the two box values written under the strips: 4 and 10: 2 and 2, same; picked. 14 and 35: 7 and 7, same; picked. 6 and 15: 3 and 3; picked. 5 and 8: 2.5 and 1.6, different; dims. 3 and 6: 1.5 and 1.2, different; dims. (3) Three picked, three asked.

**Fast.** Glance first (whole batches), then the same-box test on whatever survives.

**Sense.** A group that keeps 2:5 has far more adults than students, two and a half times as many; 5 students and 8 adults is not even twice.

**Check.** Count the picks against the number asked; for each pick, multiply the ratio by its box and land on the group (7 × 2 = 14, 7 × 5 = 35).

**Rule out.** Odd students or adults not ending in 0 or 5 dim together ("not whole batches"); a pair in the reverse order (2 nuts and 7 raisins for 7:2) dims ("the words say nuts first").

**Line.** "Same tape, same box. Do the same to both."

**Trap.** Keeping the *difference*: 2 and 5 differ by 3, 5 and 8 differ by 3, so "same". And 3 and 6 as "one more each". The test writes 2.5 beside 1.6: the extra piece is not a box, and adding breaks the tape.

**Kit.** `tape` with `test` writing both box values, `dim`, the pick mark and count.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

### `mcas-ratio-statements-select` — Which ratio statements are true?

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.1: "Understand the concept of a ratio and use ratio language to describe a ratio relationship between two quantities… 'for every 2 wings there was 1 beak.'" Statements compare part to part, part to whole and whole to part, in either order, some scaled. Select 3 of 6.

**Foundations first.** Ratio, "for every", order of words equals order of numbers. Then *part to whole*, built: oak 12, maple 9, pine 3 as three strips of unit cells, stacked; `join` them end to end into one bar with the bracket "all the trees: 24". "To all" or "all to" uses the whole bar as one of the two amounts. Then equivalent ratios in one beat: 3 : 24 regrouped into boxes of 3 is 1 : 8, the same tape. Say plainly that a ratio can be true without being in simplest form (4 : 2 is 24 : 12 with boxes of 6).

**Read.** Each statement names two amounts in an order and gives two numbers. The question is whether those two numbers are the named amounts, or both scaled by one factor. At a glance: when "all" is named first the first number must be the bigger one; when "all" is second, the second must be. Any statement breaking that is out without arithmetic.

**The example.** "A park has 12 oak trees, 9 maple trees and 3 pine trees… Choose the 3 true statements." (1) The joined tape, 24 all, with the three parts labeled. (2) Each statement in turn, its two amounts highlighted on the tape and its true ratio written in word order: pine to all: 3 : 24, boxes of 3 make 1 : 8, true; all to pine: 24 : 3 = 8 : 1, true; all to oak: 24 : 12, boxes of 6 make 4 : 2, true; maple to oak: 9 : 12 = 3 : 4, not 3 : 7; pine to maple: 3 : 9 = 1 : 3, not 1 : 4; all to maple: 24 : 9 = 8 : 3, not 8 : 11. (3) The three true pick; the three false dim together.

**Fast.** Write the three counts and the total once. For each statement, write the two numbers in word order from that list, then ask the same-box question (can both of the statement's numbers be scaled by one factor to mine?). The "all" direction check first saves half the work.

**Sense.** A whole-to-part ratio has the bigger number first; a part-to-part ratio has neither number as big as the total.

**Check.** Exactly three picked; each read back as "for every": "for every 1 pine tree there are 8 trees in all."

**Rule out.** "All" first with a smaller first number (8 : 11; 3 : 8 for all to oak), or "all" second with a smaller second number (11 : 8 for oak to all), dims at once; a statement with a number that matches no count and no total (11 in a park of 12, 9, 3) is suspect.

**Line.** "The order of the words is the order of the numbers. All means the whole bar."

**Trap.** "Pine to all" written against the *other* parts (3 : 21) instead of the whole; and rejecting 4 : 2 because it is not simplest form. The joined bar shows pine inside the 24, and the regroup shows 24 : 12 as two boxes against one.

**Kit.** `tape` with three strips, `join` with the "all" bracket, `regroup` to show a scaled statement, `highlight` of two amounts at once, `dim`, pick marks.

**Writer.** Strongest. Six statements read as sentences, with three directions and scaled forms; the quality is in choosing the order of reading and the one true "not simplest" statement that teaches.

### `staar-ratio-statements` — Simplest form, split a total, unit rate

**Standard.** Mixed under one name, three kinds. Simplest form: 6.RP.A.1 (ratio language). Split a total: 6.RP.A.3 ("tape diagrams"). Unit rate: 6.RP.A.2 ("a rate of $5 per hamburger") and 3b ("unit rate problems"). The lesson teaches reading: the question's words say which picture to draw.

**Foundations first.** Open with the one idea true for all three: a ratio is two amounts that go together, and the picture is a tape; the words of the question say what to do with the tape. Define *simplest form* ("the biggest box that fits both"), *in all* ("the parts joined make the total"), and *per* ("for one; divide by the number of them"). Each kind gets its short "why" beat before its example.

**Kind 1, simplest form.** "A bag has 20 red marbles and 28 blue marbles. What is the ratio of red marbles to blue marbles in simplest form?" (1) "red to blue": red first. Tape: 20 teal cells, 28 rust cells. (2) `regroup` into boxes of 2 (10 and 14), then boxes of 4 (5 and 7); the biggest box that fits both strips is 4 (the GCF from family 7, named as a box). (3) 5 : 7. Rule out: 20 : 28 is not simplest; 8 : 4 says red is more, but the red strip is shorter; 5 : 12 is 20 : 48 with boxes of 4, not this tape. Check: 5 × 4 = 20, 7 × 4 = 28.

**Kind 2, split a total.** "A floor uses blue tiles and white tiles in the ratio 2 to 5. There are 28 tiles in all. How many are blue?" (1) Tape 2 : 5 joined, 7 boxes, bracket 28. (2) One box is 28 ÷ 7 = 4; `fill`, braces 8 and 20. (3) Blue is 8. Rule out: 26 and 36 are nearly the whole or more than it, and blue is a part; 4 is one box, and blue has two. Check: 8 + 20 = 28, and 8 : 20 is 2 : 5 with boxes of 4.

**Kind 3, unit rate.** "A reader finishes 171 pages in 9 minutes. Which is the unit rate in pages per minute?" (1) "per minute": the answer is for one minute. Double number line, pages on top, minutes below, 171 over 9. (2) `divide`: the 9 minutes cut into nine equal jumps down to the unit tick; pages jump down by 19 each: 171 ÷ 9 = 19. (3) 19 pages per minute. Rule out: 171 pages per minute is no division; 19 minutes per page swaps the units (one page does not take 19 minutes); 9 pages per minute is the other amount. Check: 19 × 9 = 171.

**Fast.** "Simplest form": divide both by the biggest number that fits. "In all": add the ratio numbers, divide the total by that, multiply by the part's number. "Per": divide by the number of the thing after "per".

**Sense.** A simplified ratio keeps the same strip longer; a part is less than the total; a unit rate here is less than the total pages and the units read in the order asked.

**Check.** As above, one per kind.

**Line.** None shared; the recap names each kind by its words: "simplest form: the biggest box that fits both"; "in all: join, then find one box"; "per: divide by the number of them."

**Trap.** Kind 1: simplifying one number only, or subtracting (20 : 28 to 12 : 20). Kind 2: dividing the total by 2 or by 5 instead of by 7 boxes. Kind 3: dividing the wrong way (9 ÷ 171) or swapping the unit words. The regroup moves both strips at once; the joined tape counts 7 boxes under 28; the unit tick sits on the minutes line.

**Kit.** `tape` with `regroup`, `join` with a total, `fill`; `dnl` with `divide` (shared with `unit-rate`, pilot 3); `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest. A mixed type: three stories, three pictures, and the lesson is the reading that tells them apart.

### `conversion-proportion-blanks` — Set up a conversion as equal ratios

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3d: "Use ratio reasoning to convert measurement units." One variant only (yards to meters), and the question stops at the setup: it never asks for x. Flags: the template should vary the units and should also ask for x (36.4), since the standard is to *convert*; a setup with no answer is half a problem. The lesson sets up as asked and then finds x anyway, as the check that the setup was right.

**Foundations first.** Define a *conversion*: one amount measured twice, in two units; "one yard is about 0.91 meter" is a ratio, yards to meters, 1 : 0.91. Build the double number line: yards on top (teal), meters below (rust), 0 over 0, 1 over 0.91; then 40 on top over "?". Say what a *proportion* is: two tick pairs from one double number line, written as two equal fractions, each read top then bottom. Equal because they are the same line.

**Read.** "The first ratio is the conversion factor, yards to meters": yards on top, meters below, 1 over 0.91. The second ratio is the new amount in the same order: 40 yards over x meters. At a glance: the two left slots are the conversion numbers, the two right slots are 40 and x, and yards is on top in both.

**The example.** "One yard is about 0.91 meter… Complete the proportion that finds x, the number of meters in 40 yards." (1) Double number line as built; the tick pair at 1 lights: write 1 over 0.91, said as "one yard, point nine one meter". (2) The tick at 40 lights: 40 over x. (3) The proportion on the board with four slots filled on the word: 1/0.91 = 40/x; read down each side: yards over meters, yards over meters. (4) Beyond what is asked, the check that the setup is right: ×40 along both lines (blue arrows on both), x = 0.91 × 40 = 36.4 meters.

**Fast.** Left side: the conversion as given. Right side: the amount under the same unit, x under the other. Read the units down both sides; they must match.

**Sense.** A meter is longer than a yard, so 40 yards is fewer than 40 meters: 36.4 fits.

**Check.** Units read down each side agree; 1 × 40 = 40 and 0.91 × 40 = 36.4, the same factor along both lines.

**Rule out (slot choices).** Any arrangement with yards on top on one side and meters on top on the other is out; 0.91 and 1 belong together, 40 and x belong together.

**Line.** "Same units, same order, same factor."

**Trap.** Swapping one side (1/0.91 = x/40), which is the order misread, and the real reason cross-multiplying is not taught here: a learner who writes the proportion from the picture never needs it. The tick pair is always read top then bottom.

**Kit.** `dnl` with a decimal tick and `hops`/along arrows; a proportion on the board with four blanks that fill on the word.

**Writer.** Cheaper model; one picture, one method, one variant.
