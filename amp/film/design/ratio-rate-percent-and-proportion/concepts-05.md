# Percent change (`percent-change`, grade 7): per-type concepts

Ten problem types, 7.RP.A.3 throughout. Planning sources: the family design, the generated variants, the standards' exact text. Order here is the order the ideas are built, not the catalog's: the forward change, the rate, the backward read, two changes, chains, the seven stories, then interest and the two financial-literacy types.

**What every film in this skill opens with.** The shared build from the family design, drawn live in about twenty-five seconds: the original amount as a bar labeled 100% above and its amount below; cut in ten, so 10% is one tenth and 1% one hundredth; *percent of* means multiply by the hundredths; *increase by p%* adds a piece p% of the original to the end, the scale continuing past 100; *decrease by p%* removes a piece from the end; the original keeps its 100% label the whole time. Define *original*, *new amount*, *percent change*. The first four types draw it; the later ones say it in one line while the bar appears. Foundation 7 of the family design, and foundation 5 (find 1%, count to 100) named as already owned.

---

### `percent-change-final` — The new amount after a percent change. Pilot #4. Foundation 7.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3: "percent increase and decrease."
**Foundations first.** The shared build on 160: 10% is 16, 1% is 1.60. The share scale runs past 100 (to 125). *Increase by* and *decrease by* shown as the grow and the cut before any number is asked.
**Read.** "A price of 160 increases by 23%. Final price." The piece is 23% of 160; the question asks the whole new length, not the piece. Gold on "increases" and "final". At a glance: more than 160; 23% is a bit under a quarter, so about 160 + 40 = 200; a price, two decimal places.
**Example.** 160 up 23%. (1) Bar 0 to 160, 100%, cut in ten. (2) Build the piece from tenths: 10% is 16, 20% is 32, 3% is three 1.60s, 4.80: 36.80. It grows rust past the end; the scale continues 110, 120, 123. (3) A brace over the whole new bar: 160 + 36.80 = 196.80. (4) The same bar read once: 123% of 160 is 1.23 × 160 = 196.80, stacked.
**Second example** (a decrease is a different motion, and what remains is the thing to multiply): 125 down 81%. The last 81% hatches rust and leaves; 19% remains; 0.19 × 125 = 23.75. Said once: what is left is 100 − 81. (In passing: up 66⅔% adds two thirds of the original, 250.74 × 5/3 = 417.9, a fraction percent is a fraction; 0% changes nothing, 255.)
**Fast.** One multiplier: up p% is × (1 + p/100); down p% is × (1 − p/100). Always.
**Sense.** Direction, then the quarter estimate; 81% off leaves about a fifth, about 25.
**Check.** 196.80 − 160 = 36.80 and 36.80 ÷ 160 = 0.23. 23.75 ÷ 125 = 0.19.
**Line.** "The original is one hundred percent. Up twenty-three percent is one hundred twenty-three percent of it."
**Trap.** Answering the piece (36.80; 101.25 for the decrease) as the final amount, or dropping the hundredths (160 × 23 = 3,680). The brace sits over the whole new bar under the 123 label, and the piece is visibly less than a quarter of the bar.
**Kit.** `bar` with `max`, `grow`, `cut` (family 4.3).
**Writer.** Strongest: the first lesson on this picture; the next three types stand on it.
**Note.** The app returns 984/5 and 2183/5; a price is 196.80 and a quantity 436.6. Accept and show decimals.

### `percent-change-rate` — The change as a percent of the original.

**Flag first.** The "share of a group" variants ask the "relative percent change" of a percent (0.39% to 0.26%); the arithmetic is the same as any amount but the wording is above grade 7, and the seed 10% to 10% is degenerate (relative change 0). Keep the type; reword to plain amounts. Answers 175/2 and 100/3 should display as 87.5 and 33⅓.
**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3: "percent increase and decrease."
**Foundations first.** The shared bar. *Percent change*: the piece added or removed, measured against the original, per hundred. Two questions inside one: how much did it change, and what percent of the original is that.
**Read.** "Decreases from 0.40 to 0.05. By what percent." The piece removed is 0.40 − 0.05; the question is what percent of 0.40 that piece is. Gold on "from" (the original) and "percent". At a glance: nearly all of it went, so the percent is near 100 and well over 50.
**Example.** 0.40 → 0.05. (1) Bar 0.40 at 100%, cut in ten; each tenth is 0.04, 10%. (2) 0.05 marked; the removed piece 0.35 hatched rust. (3) How many tenths of the original is 0.35? 0.04, 0.08 ... 0.32 is eight tenths, 80%; the 0.03 left is three quarters of a tenth, 7.5%: 87.5%. (4) On the board: 0.35 ÷ 0.40 = 35/40 = 7/8 = 0.875 = 87.5%. The two routes meet.
**Second example** (an increase, briefly): 452 → 847.50. Change 395.50; 395.50 ÷ 452 = 0.875: an 87.5% increase. The bar grows to 187.5; the change is the part past 100, the 87.5, never the 187.5.
**Fast.** Change ÷ original, written per hundred. The only decision is which number is the original: the one after "from".
**Sense.** More than half gone means more than 50%.
**Check.** 0.40 × 0.875 = 0.35, and 0.40 − 0.35 = 0.05. 452 × 1.875 = 847.50.
**Line.** "The change, out of the original."
**Trap.** Dividing by the new amount (0.35 ÷ 0.05 = 700%; 395.50 ÷ 847.50 = 46.7%), and for an increase, reporting the new amount as a percent of the original (187.5%) instead of the change. The 100% label never leaves the original; the change is the hatched or grown piece, never the whole bar.
**Kit.** Nothing beyond 4.3.
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one method, one picture.

### `percent-change-original` — The original from the new amount. Foundations 5 and 7.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3: "percent increase and decrease," solved backward.
**Foundations first.** The bar with "?" as its whole. *Original*: the amount before, the 100%, now unknown. The new amount sits at a known percent of it. To find the whole: find one percent, then count to a hundred (owned from grade 6, named).
**Read.** "Decreases by 42% to 277.24. Original price." 277.24 is what is left: 58% of the unknown original. Gold on "to" and "original". At a glance: a decrease, so the original was more than 277.24; 58% is a bit over half, so the original is a bit under double: about 480.
**Example.** Down 42% to 277.24. (1) Bar labeled ? at 100%; the last 42% hatched and gone; the remaining 58% carries 277.24 below. (2) 1% is 277.24 ÷ 58 = 4.78 (long division on the board; 58 × 4.78 = 277.24). (3) A hundred of them: 478; the ? becomes 478 and the amount scale fills. (4) As one divide: 277.24 ÷ 0.58 = 478.
**Second example** (an increase whose percent is a fraction): up 33⅓% to 336. A third of the original was added: the tape shows the original as three boxes and the new amount as four; four boxes are 336, one box is 84, the original is three boxes: 252. (Up 12.5% to 12.60: 112.5% is 12.60, 1% is 0.112, 100% is 11.20, one beat.)
**Fast.** New amount ÷ the multiplier (0.58, 1.125, 4/3). Or 1% then × 100: the same thing.
**Sense.** After a decrease the original was more; after an increase, less.
**Check.** Apply the change forward: 478 × 0.42 = 200.76, 478 − 200.76 = 277.24. 252 + 84 = 336.
**Line.** "To find the whole, find one percent, then count to a hundred."
**Trap.** Taking 42% of the new price and adding it back (277.24 × 1.42 = 393.68): the percent is of the original, which is the unknown. On screen the hatched 42% is a piece of the ? bar and 277.24 sits under 58%, not under 100%.
**Kit.** `bar` with an unknown whole and a known-amount mark (`"bottom"` filled, whole "?"); `tape` for the fraction case.
**Writer.** Strongest: the bar read backward is the lesson.
**Note.** 56/5 should read 11.20.

### `successive-percent-change` — Two changes, the second of the new price. Foundation 7.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3: "multistep ... percent increase and decrease."
**Foundations first.** The bar, then the rebase said once as the whole point: after a change, the new price is the new 100%, and the second percent is a percent of that. Rounding to the nearest cent in passing; the problem says to keep every digit until the end.
**Read.** "Increases by 20%, then decreases by 25% of the price after the first change." Two changes, in order, each of the price at that moment. Gold on "after the first change". At a glance: 83 up a fifth is about 100; a quarter off that is about 75; the final is below 83 although 25 is only a little more than 20, because the 25% is of a bigger number.
**Example.** 83.00 up 20%, then down 25%. (1) Bar 83 at 100%; grow by 20%: 16.60; 99.60. (2) Rebase: the bar relabels 0 to 100% at 99.60; the old 120 fades to muted. (3) Cut 25%: 24.90 leaves; 74.70. (4) Board: 83 × 1.20 = 99.60; 99.60 × 0.75 = 74.70. (5) The one factor: 1.20 × 0.75 = 0.90, so the two changes are a 10% decrease; 83 × 0.90 = 74.70.
**Second example** (same percent up and down, quick): 123.98 up 21%, then down 21%: 150.0158, then × 0.79 = 118.51, less than the start. The 21% taken off was of a bigger price. Factor 1.21 × 0.79 = 0.9559.
**Fast.** Multiply by the first factor, then the second, or by their product. Always.
**Sense.** The product factor tells the direction at once: 0.90 is below 1, so lower than the start.
**Check.** 74.70 ÷ 83 = 0.90 = 1.20 × 0.75.
**Line.** "After a change, the new price is the new one hundred percent."
**Trap.** Adding the percents (+20 − 25 = −5; 83 × 0.95 = 78.85), or taking both percents of the original. The rebase: the 25% piece is cut from a bar labeled 99.60, visibly longer than the 83 bar, so it is more than 25% of 83 would be.
**Kit.** `rebase` (4.3).
**Writer.** Strongest: the rebase motion is the lesson.

### `percent-chain` — Two changes, backward or combined. Mixed type.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3: "multistep ratio and percent problems."
Three kinds under one name: a marked price lowered, then compared with what the store paid; a sale price raised, with the price before the sale asked; two rises combined into one factor. Teaches reading: where in the chain does the unknown sit?
**Foundations first.** Each change is one factor (down 50% is × 0.5, up 50% is × 1.5) and a chain of bars, each the 100% of the next, from `successive-percent-change`. Going backward is dividing by the factor, from `percent-change-original`.
**Read.** "Marks a jacket at 390, lowers it 50%. The new price is 50% more than the price the store paid." The chain is *cost → × 1.5 → 195*; the 390 only gives the 195. Gold on "more than the price the store paid": the cost is that sentence's 100%. "Reduced 40%, later increased 50%, now 108; price before the sale": *before → × 0.6 → × 1.5 → 108*; the unknown is the start. "Rose 25%, then 50%; the original times one number": the product of the factors. At a glance: 50% more than the cost means the cost is less than 195, about 130; the bicycle's changes nearly cancel (0.6 × 1.5 = 0.9), so the original was a little more than 108.
**Example A, the jacket.** (1) 390 bar, cut in half: 195. (2) A second bar, "what the store paid", ? at 100%, grown by 50% to 195: as a tape, the cost is two boxes and 195 is three; a box is 65. (3) The cost is two boxes: 130 (or 1% is 1.30, so 130). Check: 130 + 65 = 195 = half of 390.
**Example B, the bicycle.** (1) Three bars: ? → × 0.6 → × 1.5 → 108, each rebased. (2) Undo from the end: 108 ÷ 1.5 = 72; 72 ÷ 0.6 = 120. (3) Or the one factor: 0.6 × 1.5 = 0.9; 108 ÷ 0.9 = 120. Check forward: 120 × 0.6 = 72, 72 × 1.5 = 108.
**Quick third, the fee.** 1.25 × 1.5 = 1.875; the tape reads 100, 125, 187.5.
**Fast.** Write each change as its factor, in order. Forward, multiply; backward, divide; combined, multiply the factors.
**Sense.** Direction, and the near-cancel.
**Check.** Run the chain forward from the answer and land on the given number.
**Line.** "Each change is one factor. Forward, multiply. Backward, divide."
**Trap.** Adding the percents (−40 + 50 = +10, 108 ÷ 1.1 = 98.18; 1.25 + 0.5 = 1.75), and reading "50% more than the store paid" as "the store paid 50% less" (195 × 0.5 = 97.50). The cost bar is drawn as its own 100% with 195 past its end.
**Kit.** `rebase`, `pair`, `tape`.
**Writer.** Strongest: mixed; the example must be chosen for where the unknown sits.

### `percent-applications` — Tax, tip, commission, markup, markdown, simple interest, percent error. Mixed type.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3, every example it names: "simple interest, tax, markups and markdowns, gratuities and commissions, fees, percent increase and decrease, percent error."
Teaches reading. The one idea true of all seven: find the **base**, the amount the percent is *of* (it is the bar's 100%); take the percent of it; then read what is asked: the piece, the base plus the piece, the base minus the piece, or the percent itself.
**Foundations first.** The shared bar. Each word defined in a few words as its story arrives: *tip* (a percent of the bill, added), *tax* (the same, a different word), *commission* (a percent of sales, on top of base pay), *markup* (a store adds a percent of its cost), *markdown* (taken off), *simple interest* (the same piece every year), *percent error* (the mistake as a percent of the true amount). Two `term` cards: commission and percent error.
**Read and example, one per story, each on its own screen with its choices:**
- Tip: bill 116, tip 15%, "in all". Base 116; 10% is 11.60, 5% is 5.80; tip 17.40; "in all" is base plus piece: 133.40.
- Markup: lamp cost 32, up 40%. The store's cost is the 100%; 12.80; selling price 44.80.
- Commission: 3% of sales 4,200 is 126; plus base pay 250: 376. The base of the percent is the sales, not the pay.
- Markdown as a percent: coat 180 → 72; taken off 108; 108 ÷ 180 = 60%. The question asks the percent taken off, not the percent left.
- Simple interest: 200 at 4% for 4 years: 8 a year, the same piece four times: 32. "Interest in all" is the pieces, not the 232 total.
- Percent error: 24 measured, 32 true; error 8; 8 ÷ 32 = 25%. The base is the true amount.
**Fast.** Base × percent; then add, subtract, multiply by the years, or divide for a percent.
**Sense.** A tip or markup makes more than the base, a markdown less; four years of interest is four times one year.
**Check.** Piece ÷ base gives the percent back (17.40 ÷ 116 = 0.15); percent × base gives the piece (0.60 × 180 = 108).
**Rule out.** Dim together: the piece alone when "in all" is asked (17.40, 12.80, 126); the percent added as dollars (131 = 116 + 15, 72 = 32 + 40, 253 = 250 + 3); the wrong unit (8 seconds for a percent, 108% for dollars taken off); the wrong direction (98.60 after a tip, 19.20 after a markup).
**Line.** "Find the base: the amount the percent is of. That is the one hundred percent."
**Trap.** The percent left answered for the percent taken off (72 ÷ 180 = 40%); dividing by the measured value in percent error (8 ÷ 24 = 33⅓%); the commission taken of the base pay (257.50). Each story lights its base gold on the bar before anything is computed.
**Kit.** `bar`, `dim`; nothing new.
**Writer.** Strongest: mixed; the recap names each kind by what happens in the story.

### `simple-interest-blanks` — Interest the same every year.

**Flag first.** The answer is a pair of letters (interest d, total a): the sentence has lettered options that `facts.ts` does not print. The writer must read the template to state the problem exactly as seen.
**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3: "simple interest."
**Foundations first.** *Principal* (the money put in), *rate* (a percent of the principal each year), *interest* (extra money paid), *simple interest* (the same piece each year, always of the principal), *total* (principal plus all the interest). Build: a bar of 1,200 at 100%; 1% is 12; 4% is 48, a rust piece. Then a tape of three equal 48 boxes, one per year.
**Read.** "4% simple interest each year. 1,200 for 3 years. Complete the sentence." Two blanks: the interest and the total. At a glance: 4% of 1,200 is about 50 a year, about 150 in three; the total is a little over 1,200 and never less.
**Example.** (1) 1,200 bar; 1% is 12; four of them: 48. (2) Year one, two, three: a 48 box arrives on each count; the brace reads 48, 96, 144. (3) Interest: 144. (4) Total: 1,200 + 144 = 1,344, stacked. The letter for each blank chosen and read back as a sentence.
**Fast.** Principal × rate × years is the interest; add the principal for the total. (Three years at 4% is 12% of the principal in one go, 144; say so once.)
**Sense.** About 150; the total just over 1,200.
**Check.** 144 ÷ 3 = 48 = 4% of 1,200.
**Rule out.** A total less than the principal; an interest bigger than the principal (4,800 = 1,200 × 4, the percent forgotten).
**Line.** "Simple interest: the same piece every year, always of the principal."
**Trap.** The two blanks swapped (144 as the total), and forgetting the years (48). The three boxes count and the brace reads 1,344.
**Kit.** `bar`, `tape`; nothing new.
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model.

### `interest-table-type` — Simple or compound, from a table. Above grade in part.

**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3 "simple interest" for the simple account; telling simple from compound is HSF.LE.A.1a: "linear functions grow by equal differences over equal intervals, and ... exponential functions grow by equal factors over equal intervals." High school. Keep the type as the bridge to family 16, mark it a stretch, and say "the same amount each year" against "the same percent of a growing amount"; no exponential formula. Three kinds: which account is simple, and its balance after 10 years (two wordings); one year's interest in the compound account.
**Foundations first.** *Year-end balance*; *compound interest*: each year's interest is a percent of the new balance, the rebase every year. Build: the table with a muted "rise" column written between its rows (`diffs`), and under it two bars: A gains the same 30 piece every year; B rebases every year and gains 6% of the new bar: 30, 31.80, 33.71.
**Read.** "Which account pays simple interest, and what will it hold after 10 years?" The table shows years 0 to 3; "after 10 years" counts from year 0. Gold on "simple" and "10 years". At a glance: both accounts agree at year 1 (same rate) and split at year 2; the one whose rises are equal is simple; its 10-year balance is the principal plus ten rises.
**Example.** 500; A 530, 560, 590; B 530, 561.80, 595.51. (1) Rises for A: 30, 30, 30. For B: 30, 31.80, 33.71, growing. (2) A is simple: 30 a year, 6% of 500. B's second rise is 6% of 530 = 31.80, the percent of the new balance; the bar rebases. (3) After 10 years, A holds 500 + 10 × 30 = 800.
**Second example** (one year's compound interest, and the columns swapped): 5,000; A 5,200, 5,408, 5,624.32; B 5,200, 5,400, 5,600. Here A is the compound account: read the rises, never assume. Year 3's interest is the rise from year 2 to year 3: 5,624.32 − 5,408 = 216.32, which is 4% of 5,408.
**Fast.** Subtract neighbors. Equal rises: simple; its balance after t years is principal + t × rise. One year's compound interest is one rise.
**Sense.** A compound account pulls ahead of a simple one at the same rate and stays ahead; at 6% the simple account has not doubled in ten years.
**Check.** Rise ÷ principal is the rate (30 ÷ 500 = 6%); 216.32 ÷ 5,408 = 4% matches year 1's 200 ÷ 5,000.
**Rule out.** Dim together: the account whose rises grow; 300 (less than the start: ten rises without the principal); a whole balance (5,624.32) or three years' growth (624.32) when one year's interest is asked.
**Line.** "Simple: the same piece every year. Compound: the same percent of a growing amount."
**Trap.** Counting ten years from the last row: 590 + 300 = 890, 3,250 + 2,500 = 5,750. The year column lights at 0 and the ten rises are counted from there.
**Kit.** `table` `diffs`; `bar` `rebase`, `pair`.
**Writer.** Strongest: the insight is the rebase against the fixed piece.

### `return-claim-check` — A yearly rate of return. Misfiled.

**Flag first.** Financial literacy (rates of return, ad claims): belongs with `financial-literacy-middle` in family 21, reusing this skill's bar. The arithmetic it practices is 7.RP.A.3 ("multistep ... percent problems"). Two kinds: compare two investments; check an ad's claim, over years or months.
**Foundations first.** *Rate of return*: the gain as a percent of the starting amount; *yearly rate*: that percent shared equally across the years (simple, as the problem says). Build: the start as a bar at 100%; the gain grows past the end; the gain piece is cut into equal yearly slices.
**Read.** "Which earned the higher yearly rate of return": not the bigger gain, not the bigger total return; the percent per year. Gold on "yearly rate". "An ad says 24% per year ... which statement is right": compute the yearly rate and compare with the claim. At a glance: 96 on 400 is about a quarter over three years, about 8 a year; 25 on 250 is a tenth in one year. B wins before any work.
**Example.** A: 400 → 496 in 3 years. B: 250 → 275 in 1 year. (1) A's bar: gain 96; 96 ÷ 400 = 24%; sliced into three equal 8% pieces: 8% per year. (2) B's bar: gain 25; 25 ÷ 250 = 10%; one year, one slice: 10%. (3) B: 10% per year, higher than 8%.
**Second example** (months): 6,000 → 6,120 in 3 months; the ad says 5%. Gain 120; 120 ÷ 6,000 = 2% in three months; a year is four of those slices: 8% per year; the claim of 5% is too low.
**Fast.** Gain ÷ start, per hundred; ÷ years (or × 12 ÷ months).
**Sense.** A bigger dollar gain on a bigger start over more years can be a smaller rate.
**Check.** Start × rate × years = gain: 400 × 0.08 × 3 = 96; 6,000 × 0.08 × ¼ = 120.
**Rule out.** Dim together: comparisons in dollars (gained 96 against 25); total returns over different spans (24% against 10%); the total return stated as yearly (24% for the fund); for months, the three-month rate stated as yearly (2%: "too high").
**Line.** "A rate of return is the gain, out of the start, per year."
**Trap.** Comparing gains or total returns over unequal times; stopping at the three-month 2%. The 24% piece sliced into three 8% slices sits beside B's one whole-year 10% slice.
**Kit.** A slice action on a grown piece: the gain partitioned into n equal labeled slices (small addition to `bar`).
**Writer.** Capable cheaper model from this concept (two cases, decoys named); the review recomputes every percent.

### `credit-cost-concepts` — A month on a credit card. Misfiled.

**Flag first.** Financial literacy (APR, late fees, minimum payments): belongs with `financial-literacy-middle` in family 21, reusing the percent bar and the register table from here. Four kinds under one name: APR from a monthly rate; two cards' interest compared; a month's new balance; minimum payment against paying in full. Mixed; teaches reading.
**Standard.** 7.RP.A.3: "fees ... percent" problems.
**Foundations first.** *Balance* (what is owed), *APR* (the yearly rate: twelve monthly rates), *monthly rate* (APR ÷ 12), *interest* (a percent of the unpaid balance, added each month, rounded to the cent), *minimum payment*, *late fee*. Build: a tape of twelve equal boxes of 1.25% making 15%; a bar of the balance with its 1% mark; a register whose last column is the running balance.
**Read, by kind, with the fact that decides, each on its own screen with its choices.**
- APR: "1.25% each month; the APR is the monthly rate times 12." Twelve boxes: 15%.
- Two cards, 12% and 21% APR, balance 2,948.54: monthly 1% and 1.75%; interest 29.49 and 51.60, each rounded; "how much less": 22.11. Fast: the difference in rate, 9% a year, is 0.75% a month, of the balance: 22.11, the same.
- Full example, the new balance: start 2,785.31; 1.25% of it is 34.82; buys 102, fee 25, pays 32. The register, one line each: + 34.82 → 2,820.13; + 102 → 2,922.13; + 25 → 2,947.13; − 32 → 2,915.13. Charges add, payments subtract; the interest is of the starting balance, as the problem says.
- Minimum: 2,582.19, pays 50; unpaid 2,532.19; 1% of it is 25.32; paying in full costs nothing; so 25.32 more. The bar: the 50 paid cut off, 1% of what remains lit.
At a glance: a month's interest is about 1% of the balance, tens of dollars; a balance after buying more than was paid goes up; 12% a year is 1% a month, never 12% for a month.
**Fast.** Monthly rate = APR ÷ 12; interest = monthly rate × the unpaid balance, to the cent; new balance = balance + charges − payments.
**Sense.** Interest is small next to the balance; the balance moves by about the purchases and the fee.
**Check.** The register's lines add back; 22.11 reached both ways (two interests, or the rate difference).
**Rule out.** Dim together: a year's rate charged for one month (265.37, nearly a tenth of the balance); a single card's interest when the difference is asked (29.49, 51.60); interest on the whole balance when part was paid (25.82) or on the payment (0.50); a new balance below the floor 2,785.31 + 102 + 25 − 32 = 2,880.31, since interest only adds (2,813.13, 2,880.31 itself); a monthly rate offered as the APR (1.25%).
**Line.** "The APR is twelve monthly rates. Interest is a percent of what is left unpaid."
**Trap.** Using the yearly rate for one month; charging interest on the full balance after a payment; subtracting the fee. Twelve boxes make the year; the paid part is cut from the bar before the 1% is taken.
**Kit.** `table` `register`; `tape`; `bar` `cut` with a 1% mark.
**Writer.** Strongest: four stories, no recipe.
