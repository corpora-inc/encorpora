# Ratio, rate, percent and proportion: per-type concepts, part 2

Ten problem types across `ratios`, `unit-rates`, `ratio-tables` and two of `percentages`. Each stands on the family design: the tape, the double number line, the percent bar and the hundredths flat; the five moves (Read, At a glance, Fast, Sense, Check) on every one. Where a problem's figure comes from the app (a table, a double number line), the lesson's picture is the problem's picture, with the kit's motions laid on top; where an example proposes a figure, the writer takes the seed's actual numbers from `facts.ts` and keeps the shape.

Shared openings, said once per film and drawn live:
- **Ratios and tables** (`ratio-pair-blanks`, `ratio-table-missing`): "A ratio says how two amounts go together, *for every*. 7 to 3 means 7 of one for every 3 of the other. We draw it as a tape: 7 boxes and 3 boxes, every box the same size, every box holding the same number."
- **Rates** (`unit-rate`, `better-buy`, `unit-price-sentence-slots`, `unit-rate-pair-blanks`, `double-number-line-missing`, `dollar-rate-scale`): "A rate is a ratio of two different kinds of things: dollars and hours, pages and minutes. A unit rate is the amount that goes with exactly one of the other: dollars for one hour. *Per* means *for one*. We draw two number lines that share their ticks, so what sits across from 1 is the unit rate."
- **Percents** (`percent-of`, `staar-rename-percent-fraction-decimal`): "Percent means for every hundred. The flat you know is a hundred cells; one cell is one percent, one column is ten percent, the whole flat is one hundred percent, which is one whole."

### Grade 6: ratios (6.RP.A.1)

**`ratio-pair-blanks` — Find the other part and the whole from a ratio and one amount.** Foundations 2 and 3.

Standard: 6.RP.A.3, "Use ratio and rate reasoning to solve real-world and mathematical problems, e.g., by reasoning about ... tape diagrams," with 6.RP.A.1 for the language ("for every 2 wings there was 1 beak"). The catalog links only A.1; the type is solving, so A.3 is primary.

Foundations first: define *ratio*, *a to b*, *for every*; say that the order of the words is the order of the numbers. Build the tape from the story: "yellow to blue is 7 to 3": seven teal boxes named *yellow*, three rust boxes named *blue*, stacked and left-aligned, all boxes one size. Say what the tape is for: "Every box holds the same number of gumballs. If we find what one box holds, we know everything." Then the two ratios about one jar: yellow to blue (the stacked strips) and yellow to all (slide the strips end to end: ten boxes). Slide back.

Read: "The ratio of yellow to blue is 7 to 3. There are 42 yellow. Complete the sentence." *Yellow* is named first, so 42 belongs to the 7-box strip; a brace labeled 42 closes over the yellow strip. The blanks ask for blue (a part) and all (the whole). At a glance: blue has fewer boxes than yellow, so blue is less than 42; all is more than 42; all is ten boxes, so it is ten times one box.

The example: (1) Tape as built, brace 42 on yellow, "?" braces on blue and, after a join, on all. (2) "42 gumballs in 7 equal boxes": 42 ÷ 7 = 6; the gold box in the yellow strip takes 6, then every box on screen copies it. (3) The blue brace counts up 6, 12, 18: eighteen blue. (4) Join on "altogether": ten boxes; the all-bracket counts by sixes to 60; the board also shows 42 + 18 = 60. (5) The sentence fills: 18 blue, 60 in all; the lettered choices for each blank are on the card from the start.

Quick second case (why: the known amount is the second-named, and the asked part is bigger than the known): "blue to yellow is 7 to 5, 40 yellow." Yellow is the 5-box strip now; 40 ÷ 5 = 8 a box; blue is 7 boxes, 56; all is 12 boxes, 96. Three or four beats on the same tape with the strips renamed.

Fast: divide the known amount by its own ratio number (one box); multiply the other ratio number by it; the whole is all the boxes times one box.
Sense: blue has 3 of the 10 boxes, so it is well under half of the jar; 18 of 60 is.
Check: 18 to 42, both divided by 6, is 3 to 7; 42 + 18 = 60 = 10 × 6.
Rule out: for the blue blank, anything 42 or more dims (fewer boxes); for the all blank, anything 42 or less dims (more boxes).
Line: "Find one box and you have them all."
Trap: dividing 42 by the wrong ratio number (42 ÷ 3 = 14), or reading 7 + 3 = 10 as the number of gumballs in all. The gold box sits in the yellow strip when 42 is split, so there are seven to share among; and the join shows ten boxes of six, not ten gumballs.
Kit: `tape` with `fill` and `join`; the problem card must show a sentence with blanks and lettered choices per blank; `dim`.
Writer: strongest. The tape is born here; the first lesson of a picture is the design.

### Grade 6: unit rates (6.RP.A.2)

**`unit-rate` — The amount for exactly one.** Foundation 4.

Standard: 6.RP.A.2, "Understand the concept of a unit rate a/b associated with a ratio a:b ... 'We paid $75 for 15 hamburgers, which is a rate of $5 per hamburger'"; 6.RP.A.3b, "Solve unit rate problems ... At what rate were lawns being mowed?"

Foundations first: define *rate*, *unit rate*, *per*. Build the double number line from the story: hours on top (teal) from 0 to 8, dollars below (rust), 0 under 0, 188 under 8; ticks to scale, 3 hours three times as far as 1. Say what it is for: "The lines share their ticks. Whatever sits straight across from 1 hour is the pay for one hour: the unit rate." Light the tick at hours 1 gold with "?" beneath it.

Read: "Student L earns 188 dollars for 8 hours. How many dollars per hour?" *Per hour* goes gold: the 1 sits on the hours line, so dollars are divided by hours. At a glance: 8 hours at 20 dollars would be 160, at 25 would be 200; so the answer is between 20 and 25 dollars, in dollars and cents.

The example: (1) Divide to one: 188 is cut into 8 equal jumps down the dollar line; each arc gets the same unknown step. (2) The division, standard layout, on the board: 188 ÷ 8 = 23.5 (8 × 23 = 184; 4 left; the 4 dollars become 40 tenths, 40 ÷ 8 = 5 tenths). (3) On the line: eight jumps of 20 reach 160; the 28 left over, shared by 8, is 3.50 more each; the arcs relabel +23.50. (4) The "?" under hours 1 becomes 23.50. (5) Answer: 23.50 dollars per hour, both units said.

Quick second case (why: the division does not end, so the rate stays a fraction; the standard's own example is 3/4 cup per cup): "8 cups of flour for 3 loaves. Cups per loaf?" Loaves on the "per" line; 8 ÷ 3 = 8/3; "a fraction *is* a division: eight thirds, two and two thirds cups for one loaf." Three or four beats. The app's answer is 8/3; the lesson says both names.

Fast: divide by the number of the thing after *per*. Always.
Sense: between 20 and 25, from the glance.
Check: 23.50 × 8 = 188.00, stacked.
Line: "Per means for one. Divide by the number of them."
Trap: two, both real. Dividing the wrong way (8 ÷ 188): the gold 1 sits on the hours line, so hours is the divisor. Writing the remainder as cents (23 R4 → 23.4 or 23.04): the 4 leftover dollars are shared by 8 hours on screen, fifty cents each.
Kit: `dnl` with `unit` and `divide`; `longdiv` (whole and decimal quotient, point straight up) from the roadmap, which this family needs here first; a multiplication `stack` for the check if `stack` has no ×.
Writer: strongest. The double number line is born here.

**`better-buy` — Which pack costs less for one?** Foundation 4. Multiple choice, three choices.

Standard: 6.RP.A.3b, "Solve unit rate problems including those involving unit pricing"; 6.RP.A.2.

Foundations first: the rate opening, then: "Two packs, two prices. The pack price tells you nothing until you know what one item costs. Two double number lines, one per store: items on top, cents below." Say once why cents: "Work in cents and the division is whole numbers" (the place-value shift, named, not retaught).

Read: "Store A sells 11 juice boxes for 3.74 dollars. Store B sells 8 for 2.88 dollars. Which is the better buy, with the lower price per juice box?" *Per juice box* goes gold. At a glance: the totals decide nothing; B costs less because it has fewer boxes. A shared benchmark settles the direction fast: at 35 cents each, 11 boxes would be 3.85 (more than 3.74, so A is under 35) and 8 would be 2.80 (less than 2.88, so B is over 35). A is cheaper per box before any division.

The example: (1) Two double number lines, A above B, boxes on top, cents below, 374 under 11 and 288 under 8; both unit ticks gold. (2) A: 374 ÷ 11 = 34; the "?" at 1 becomes 34 cents. (3) B: 288 ÷ 8 = 36. (4) Side by side: 34 against 36; A is lower. (5) One beat on the third choice: "They would be the same price only if both unit prices came out equal, 34 and 34. They did not." (6) Store A.

Quick second case (why: the unit is an ounce, a measure not a count, and the bigger box loses, which kills "bulk is always cheaper"): 10 ounces for 2.50 against 26 ounces for 7.54: 250 ÷ 10 = 25 cents, 754 ÷ 26 = 29 cents; the small box wins. Four beats.

Fast: cents divided by the count, for each store; the smaller number wins.
Sense: the benchmark from the glance; the two unit prices should be close, within a few cents.
Check: 11 × 34 = 374; 8 × 36 = 288.
Rule out: nothing dims from the totals, and the lesson says so; "the same price" dims the moment the two unit prices differ.
Line: "Compare the price of one, never the price of the pack."
Trap: choosing Store B because 2.88 is less than 3.74, or choosing the bigger pack as "always cheaper." Both unit prices are read at the 1 tick on screen; the second case shows the bigger box losing.
Kit: `dnl` `pair` (two lines aligned, shared top scale not required); `longdiv` or a cents `stack`; `dim`.
Writer: a capable cheaper model, from this concept; one component, two divisions, the cases named.

**`unit-price-sentence-slots` — The sentence that finds the price of one.** Foundation 4. Slots with lettered choices.

Standard: 6.RP.A.3b, "unit pricing"; 6.RP.A.2.

Foundations first: the rate opening, then the tape: eight equal boxes under a brace labeled 55.68, "every pair costs the same" is what makes the boxes equal. "The total is shared among the pairs, so the price of one pair is the total divided by the number of pairs." Then the sentence shape itself, defined: a total, divided by a count, gives the price for one; the three words *total*, *count*, *for one* are written above the three blanks.

Read: "8 pairs of socks cost 55.68 dollars in all, and every pair costs the same. Complete the sentence that finds the price of one pair." *In all* is the total; *8 pairs* is the count; *one pair* is asked. At a glance: the total is the biggest number in the problem; the count is the whole number of pairs; the price for one is less than the total; and 8 × 7 = 56, so one pair is just under 7 dollars.

The example: (1) Tape: 8 boxes, brace 55.68. (2) The sentence fills left to right as the tape is read: 55.68 dollars (total, teal) ÷ 8 pairs (count) = ? dollars per pair. (3) The division, standard layout: 55.68 ÷ 8 = 6.96, the point straight up. (4) The gold box takes 6.96 and every box copies it; the brace still reads 55.68. (5) The third blank: 6.96. The sentence is read aloud whole.

The 40 ÷ 8 = 5 variant is the same reading with a whole-number quotient; no second example.

Fast: total ÷ count; the answer is in dollars and cents.
Sense: just under 7, from 8 × 7 = 56.
Check: 6.96 × 8 = 55.68, stacked.
Rule out: for the first blank, only the biggest number can be the total; for the count, only the whole number of pairs; for the price of one, anything larger than the total dims.
Line: "Total divided by how many is the price of one."
Trap: dividing the other way (8 ÷ 55.68) or multiplying. The gold *one pair* in the question and the eight equal boxes under one brace make "share the total among the pairs" the only picture.
Kit: `tape` `fill`; `longdiv` with a decimal dividend; a slot card with lettered choices per blank.
Writer: cheaper model; one method, one picture.

**`unit-rate-pair-blanks` — Two rates, each from its own pair.** Foundation 4. Slots with lettered choices.

Standard: 6.RP.A.2, "Understand the concept of a unit rate a/b associated with a ratio a:b"; 6.RP.A.3b.

Foundations first: the rate opening; then "Two stories, two rates. Each rate uses only its own two numbers." Two double number lines stacked, minutes on top of each (teal), pages below (rust), each with its own pair across.

Read: "A printer prints 13 pages in 2 minutes in black and white and 46 pages in 8 minutes in color. Each rate is constant. Complete the sentence with the two unit rates." *Each rate is constant* is what lets us divide; *per minute* puts the 1 on the minutes line. At a glance: 13 in 2 minutes is a bit over 6 a minute; 46 in 8 minutes is under 6, because 8 × 6 = 48 is more than 46. So black and white is faster even though color printed more pages; that is the point of this type.

The example: (1) Line one: 2 over 13 (the pair that goes together is drawn across); unit tick gold. 13 ÷ 2 = 6.5; "six and a half pages each minute: a rate can be a fraction of a page." (2) Line two: 8 over 46; 46 ÷ 8 = 5.75 (standard layout). (3) The sentence fills: 6.5 pages per minute, 5.75 pages per minute. (4) One beat: "More pages did not mean faster. The rate is pages for one minute."

Fast: each amount divided by its own time.
Sense: a bit over 6, under 6, from the glance.
Check: 6.5 × 2 = 13; 5.75 × 8 = 46.
Rule out: a rate larger than its own page count (26, 368) dims; a rate for color at 6 or more dims (8 × 6 > 46).
Line: "Each rate from its own pair. Divide by its own time."
Trap: crossing the pairs (13 ÷ 8, 46 ÷ 2), or calling color faster because 46 is more pages. Each pair is drawn across on its own line; the two unit values sit one above the other at the end.
Kit: `dnl` `pair`; `longdiv`; slot card.
Writer: cheaper model.

### Grade 6: ratio tables (6.RP.A.3)

**`ratio-table-missing` — The missing cell in a table of equivalent ratios.** Foundation 3; both multipliers.

Standard: 6.RP.A.3a, "Make tables of equivalent ratios relating quantities with whole-number measurements, find missing values in the tables."

Foundations first: define *equivalent ratios*: the same tape with a different box value. Build it: red to white 3 to 5 as a tape, 3 teal boxes and 5 rust; the box value changes 2, 5, 8 (rescale) and the totals read 6 and 10, 15 and 25, 24 and 40; "every column of the table is this one tape with a different number in the box." Then the table (the app's figure, through `scene` if exposed): red 6, 15, 24, ?; white 10, 25, 40, 60. Define the two directions on it: *across* (white is 5 for every 3 red, in every column) and *along* (from one column to another, the same multiplier on both rows).

Read: "The table shows equivalent ratios of red paint to white paint, in liters. What number belongs in the cell with the question mark?" The ? is a red; its white is 60. At a glance: 10 white to 60 white is × 6, a whole number, so the answer is 6 × 6 at once; and red is less than white in every column, so it is less than 60.

The example: (1) Along: a blue arrow × 6 from 10 to 60 on the white row, and the same × 6 drawn on the red row at the same moment: 6 × 6 = 36. (2) Then the way that always works, even when the along factor is not whole: the biggest box. 6 and 10 share boxes of 2: three boxes and five; so one box in the ? column is 60 ÷ 5 = 12, and red is 3 × 12 = 36. The tape beside the table rescales to 12 as the cell fills. (3) 36 fills the cell.

Quick second case (why: the missing number is smaller than its neighbors, so the along arrow divides): laps 2, 4, 6 over minutes ?, 6, 9 (the seed's own numbers; the answer is 2): minutes 9 to 3 is ÷ 3 and laps 6 ÷ 3 = 2... taken as the seed's table shows; four beats.

Fast: a whole along factor, applied to both rows; otherwise divide a full column by its biggest common box, find one box in the ? column, multiply.
Sense: red stays under white; 36 under 60.
Check: 36 to 60, both divided by 12, is 3 to 5.
Line: "Do the same to both. Times, never plus."
Trap: adding along (10 to 60 is + 50, so 6 + 50 = 56) or using the difference between columns. The arrows are drawn as × on both rows in one beat; 56 to 60 laid on the tape has boxes that do not match.
Kit: `table` `arrow` (a pair, one per row, same label) and `fill` for the ? cell; `tape` `rescale` and `regroup`.
Writer: cheaper model, from this concept; the seed's table replaces my stand-in.

**`double-number-line-missing` — Read a missing value on a double number line.** Foundation 4.

Standard: 6.RP.A.3, "reasoning about ... double number line diagrams"; 6.RP.A.3b for cost per pound and constant speed.

Foundations first: the rate opening, then build the given line as the app shows it (the app's figure through `scene`, with the kit's arcs over it): pounds on top, dollars below, 0 over 0, the labels the problem gives, a "?" where it is asked. Say what to read: "A pair is two labels straight across from each other. Find the step on each line; the steps are different, but every tick is one jump on both."

Read: "Grapes cost the same for every pound. How many dollars do 8 pounds cost?" *Same for every pound* means one step per tick on the dollar line. At a glance: a full pair across (say 4 pounds over 12 dollars) is already on the line; 8 is double 4, so the answer is double 12, before any unit rate.

The example: (1) The pair 4 over 12 lights; the unit tick: 12 ÷ 4 = 3, so 1 pound is across from 3 dollars; the dollar step is +3, the pound step +1, written on arcs. (2) Hops on both lines at once, counted: 1, 2, 3 ... 8 above; 3, 6, 9 ... 24 below. (3) The ? at 8 pounds becomes 24. (4) Also the along way: 4 to 8 is × 2, 12 to 24 is × 2, blue arrows on both lines.

Quick second case (why: the given amount is on the other line, so the hop count is found by dividing): "How many pounds cost 28 dollars?" at 4 dollars a pound: hops of +4 on the dollar line until 28 is reached, seven hops; read straight up: 7 pounds. Or 28 ÷ 4. Four beats.

Fast: unit rate from any full pair (not 0 and 0), then multiply for the top or divide for the bottom; a whole along factor is the same thing faster.
Sense: more pounds, more dollars; 8 pounds cost more than 4 pounds' 12.
Check: 24 ÷ 8 = 3, the unit rate again; or count back from 24 by 3s to 12 at 4 pounds.
Line: "Find the step on each line. Straight across, then hop."
Trap: counting the bottom line's ticks as if each were 1 (reading 8 dollars for 8 pounds), or pairing a label with one that is not straight across. The step is written on an arc on each line before any hop; a pair is lit as one vertical band.
Kit: the app's double number line through `scene`, or `dnl` with `unit`, `hops`; `arrow` labels on both lines.
Writer: cheaper model.

**`dollar-rate-scale` — A charge per hour from a two-row table.** Foundation 4.

Standard: 6.RP.A.3b, "Solve unit rate problems including those involving unit pricing."

Foundations first: the rate opening, then the table as the app shows it (hours | charge, two rows: 4 hours 15.00, 5 hours 18.75), and the double number line under it with hours on top, the two charges placed under 4 and 5. Say: "The same price for each hour means every tick on the dollar line is the same jump. One hour's price is not in the table; we find it."

Read: "A study center charges the same price for each hour. The table shows the charge for 4 hours and for 5 hours. What is the charge for 12 hours? Enter the answer in dollars." *Same price for each hour* goes gold. At a glance: 4 to 12 hours is × 3, so the answer is 3 × 15 = 45 at once; and the two rows are one hour apart, so their difference is the price of one hour.

The example: (1) The difference beat: 18.75 − 15.00 = 3.75, stacked; "from 4 hours to 5 hours is one more hour, and 3.75 more dollars." (2) Confirm as the unit rate: 15.00 ÷ 4 = 3.75; the unit tick on the line reads 3.75. (3) 12 hours: 12 × 3.75 = 45.00, stacked; or the along arrow × 3 on both lines, 4 → 12 and 15 → 45. (4) 45.00, written in dollars and cents as asked.

Quick second case (why: the reverse question; the charge is given and the hours asked): "3 hours 9.75, 4 hours 13.00; a customer pays 29.25. How many hours?" One hour is 13.00 − 9.75 = 3.25. In cents: 2925 ÷ 325 = 9 hours; on the line, hops of 3.25 from 13.00 at 4 hours reach 29.25 at 9. Four beats; "think in cents and it is whole numbers."

Fast: one row's charge ÷ its hours (or the difference of two rows one hour apart); multiply by the hours asked, or divide the charge given by it.
Sense: 12 hours is three times 4 hours, so three times 15.
Check: 45.00 ÷ 12 = 3.75; or 9 × 3.25 = 29.25.
Line: "Find the price of one hour. The table never gives it; it is across from 1."
Trap: treating a table charge as the price for one hour (12 × 15.00), because the table is the only prices in sight. The gold tick at 1 hour has a "?" until the division fills it.
Kit: `table` with the app's cells; `dnl` `unit`, `arrow`; `stack` for the difference, a × `stack` for the product, `longdiv` for the reverse.
Writer: cheaper model.

### Grade 6: percentages (6.RP.A.3)

**`percent-of` — A percent of an amount.** Foundation 5.

Badly formed answer first: the app returns 3003/25 for 91% of 132. A percent of an amount is a decimal, 120.12; accept and display it so. One variant only; the lesson is short and teaches the general method.

Standard: 6.RP.A.3c, "Find a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100 (e.g., 30% of a quantity means 30/100 times the quantity)."

Foundations first: the percent opening on the flat: a cell is 1%, a column is 10%, the flat is 100%, one whole. Then the three names: 91 cells shaded is 91%, 91/100, 0.91. Then *of*: "91 percent *of* 132 means 91 hundredths of 132: times." Build the percent bar: 0 to 100% above, 0 to 132 below, the whole is 132.

Read: "What is 91% of 132?" At a glance: 91% is almost all of it, so the answer is a little under 132; the 9% missing is about a tenth of 132, about 13, so about 119 or 120.

The example: (1) The bar shades to 91%; the lower scale reads a little under 132. (2) One percent: 132 ÷ 100 = 1.32 (the digits slide two places right; the point stays, from family 2). The 1% mark on the bar reads 1.32. (3) 91 of them: 91 × 1.32 = 120.12, stacked as whole numbers 91 × 132 = 12,012 with two places. (4) Said once: "This is the same as 0.91 × 132. The hundredths are the percent." (5) 120.12.

Fast: multiply by the hundredths (0.91 × 132). For a quick exact mental answer, 1% and 10% build any percent: 91% is 100% − 9%, 132 − 9 × 1.32 = 132 − 11.88 = 120.12.
Sense: just under 132, about 120, from the glance.
Check: 132 − 120.12 = 11.88 is 9 × 1.32, the missing 9%.
Line: "Percent means for every hundred. Of means times."
Trap: subtracting (132 − 91 = 41) or multiplying without the hundredths (12,012). The bar shows 91% as nearly the whole 132, so 41 cannot be right and 12,012 is far past the bar's end.
Kit: `bar` with percent scale and `shade` (exists); `blocks` flat for the opening; a × `stack` with the place count (decimal products need the standard layout).
Writer: cheaper model; one method, one picture, the concept complete.

**`staar-rename-percent-fraction-decimal` — Three names for one amount.** Foundation 5. Multiple choice; a mixed type with five cases under one name.

Misfiled first: the linked standards (4.NF.C.6, 5.NF.B.3, 7.NS.A.2) miss the one that puts it in this family, 6.RP.A.3c ("as a rate per 100") for the percent cases. Fraction-to-decimal by division is 7.NS.A.2d ("Convert a rational number to a decimal using long division") standing on 5.NF.B.3 (a/b = a ÷ b). The 0.72 and 5.9 cases are 4.NF.C.6 review; 5.9 = 5 9/10 is `decimal-mixed-number-blanks` from family 2 again. Keep the type; fix the links; a lesson of five short screens.

Foundations first: the percent opening on the flat, then the one idea true for every case: "A percent is hundredths. A fraction is a division. The decimal is the meeting place: every name passes through it, and the flat shows the size." Draw once: 68 cells of a flat: 68%, 68/100, 0.68. The size check is the method's half: "Is it less than one whole flat, or more? Which choices have the right size?"

Read: each problem names a form to go to (*percent*, *decimal*, *fraction in lowest terms*, *equation true*). At a glance, the size: 21/8 is more than 2 wholes (21 > 16), so more than 200%; 8/5 is more than 1; 0.72 is under 1; 68% is under 1 and over half; 5.9 is between 5 and 6.

The example (fraction to percent, the case with the most to see): "Which percent is equal to 21/8? 21%, 262.5%, 2.625%, 26.25%." (1) Size: 8 goes into 21 twice with 5 left: two whole flats and 5/8 of a third; so more than 200%. (2) Divide: 21 ÷ 8 = 2.625, standard layout, point straight up. (3) The flats: two full flats, and 62.5 cells of the third shaded (five eighths of a hundred). (4) The decimal to percent: 2.625 is 262.5 hundredths, so 262.5%. (5) 262.5%.

Quick cases, one per kind, each on its own screen with its choices:
- Fraction to decimal: 8/5: more than one; 8 ÷ 5 = 1.6; a flat and six rods. (Why: the same division without the percent step.)
- Decimal to fraction in lowest terms: 0.72 = 72/100; the biggest box that fits both, 4: 18/25. The 72-cell shading regroups into 18 boxes of 4 cells out of 25. (Why: a different act, simplifying.)
- Percent to decimal: 68% = 0.68, straight from the opening. (Why: the one-step direction every grade-6 problem needs.)
- Decimal to mixed number: 5.9 is five flats and nine rods: 5 9/10. (Why: the whole stays whole; the digits after the point go over 10.)

Fast: fraction to decimal: divide top by bottom; decimal to percent: hundredths, two places; percent to decimal: two places back; decimal to fraction: over 10 or 100 by the digit count, then the biggest common box.
Sense: the size from the glance, every time.
Check: go back the other way: 262.5% is 2.625; 2.625 × 8 = 21.
Rule out: by size, together: 21%, 26.25% and 2.625% are all under one whole and 21/8 is over two: dim. 0.625 and 0.16 are under 1, 16 is sixteen wholes: dim. 25/18 is over 1 and 18/100 is 0.18: dim; 72/100 is the right amount but not lowest terms, said aloud. 0.068, 6.8 and 68 are the wrong size: dim. 59/100 and 9/10 are under 1, 5 9/100 is 5.09: dim.
Line: "A percent is hundredths. A fraction is a division. Check the size first."
Trap: the place slip: 8/5 as 0.16, 2.625 as 26.25%, 0.72 kept as 72/100 when lowest terms is asked. The flats show one and six tenths, and two and more; the regroup shows 72 cells as 18 boxes of 4.
Kit: `blocks` flat with a regroup into boxes of 4 (or the `tape` `regroup` on the shaded cells); `longdiv` for 21 ÷ 8; `dim`.
Writer: a capable cheaper model from this concept, with a strong review that holds it to one example per kind and no shared "steps" recap; the cases are short and the size check is the one idea.
