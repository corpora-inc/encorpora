# Percentages (grade 6, `percentages`): lesson concepts

Ten problem types. Planning sources: the family design, the generator's wordings as listed, the exact standard text. Every number below was recomputed.

**The opening every film in this skill shares (about twenty seconds, drawn live).** The hundredths flat from place value, 10 by 10, and its new name: the whole grid is *one hundred percent*; one cell is *one percent*, 1%, which is 1/100, which is 0.01; a full column is ten cells, *ten percent*, one tenth. "Percent means for every hundred. The whole is one hundred percent." Then the grid unrolls into the **percent bar**: 0 to 100% on top, 0 to the whole on the bottom, cut into ten boxes. One box is 10% of whatever the whole is. Every lesson in the skill is that bar, read in one direction or the other: a percent to an amount, an amount to a percent, a part up to the whole. Three names for one amount (35%, 35/100, 0.35) are said once over the grid and never re-derived.

**`staar-salary-percent` — A percent raise, or how much more one job earns.**

*Misfiled in part.* Two unrelated stories under one name: (A) a percent raise of a salary (percent) and (B) the table of two careers, "how many more in 6 years" (no percent; 4.OA.A.3 arithmetic). The family design proposes splitting B into family 21. Until then the lesson covers both, as two separate pictures, and never pretends they share a step.
**Standard.** A: 6.RP.A.3c, "Find a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100 (e.g., 30% of a quantity means 30/100 times the quantity)"; the "after the raise" wording is 7.RP.A.3 "percent increase". B: 4.OA.A.3, "multistep word problems posed with whole numbers... using the four operations".
**Foundations first.** The opening; then: a salary is the whole, so it is 100%; 1% of it is the salary divided by 100, every digit two places right (family 2's shift: 52,000 becomes 520). A *raise* is a piece added on top of the whole, so the bar must be allowed to grow past 100%. Define *raise*, *salary*.
**Read.** Gold word: "the raise each year" (stop at the piece) or "earn after the raise" (add the piece to the whole). At a glance: 4% is small, under half of 10%, so the raise is a small slice and the new salary is only a little more than the old.
**The example (A).** "A worker earns 52,000 dollars a year and gets a 4 percent raise. How many dollars a year does the worker earn after the raise?" Bar 0 to 52,000 under 0 to 100%, cut in ten. (1) One percent: one box cut into ten slivers; 52,000 ÷ 100 = 520, the digits sliding two places. (2) Four percent is four of those: 4 × 520 = 2,080; a rust piece grows past the 100% end, labeled 4%, 2,080, visibly less than half a 10% box. (3) After the raise: stack 52,000 + 2,080 = 54,080; the scale now reads 104%. If the question had asked for the raise alone, step 2 is the answer: say so once.
**The second example (B), because it is a different problem.** Table: pilot 98,500 a year, chef 84,000. "How many more does a pilot earn than a chef in 6 years?" Two bars aligned at zero (`pair`); the gap is rust: 98,500 − 84,000 = 14,500 a year. The gap copies itself six times along a tape, one per counted year: 6 × 14,500 = 87,000. "Six years of the same gap."
**Fast.** A: ÷ 100, × the percent, add if "after". B: yearly difference × years.
**Sense.** 5% of 52,000 is 2,600 (half of 5,200); 4% is a bit less. B: 14,500 × 6 is about 15,000 × 6 = 90,000.
**Check.** 54,080 − 52,000 = 2,080 = 4 × 520. B: 87,000 ÷ 6 = 14,500, the gap.
**Line.** "One percent is the whole divided by a hundred. Four percent is four of those."
**Trap.** 52,000 × 4 = 208,000 (the hundred forgotten) or 4% taken as a tenth (20,800): the rust piece would be four bars long or almost half a bar; the picture is drawn to scale and the child sees a 4% raise is a sliver. B: answering 14,500 and stopping; the six copies are counted aloud.
**Kit.** `bar` with `max` past 100% and `grow`; `pair`; `table` (exists); `tape` `copy` for the six years; `stack`.
**Writer.** Strongest while the type is mixed: the two stories need the two pictures chosen, not a recipe. After the split, A is a cheaper model's.

**`benchmark-percent-estimate` — Round, then build a percent from ten percent.**

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3c, "Find a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100", with 5.NBT.A.4, "round decimals to any place"; the spirit is 7.EE.B.3's "assess the reasonableness of answers using mental computation and estimation strategies", which the catalog does not link.
**Foundations first.** The opening; then the benchmarks, built on one bar and kept on the board as a small table: 10% is one tenth, divide by 10 (the digits one place right); 5% is half of 10%; 20% is two tens; 25% is a quarter, divide by 4; 50% is half. Define *estimate* (a quick answer close to the exact one), *tip*, *tax*, *discount* / *off* (a piece that leaves), *sale price* (what remains). Rounding to the nearest ten dollars is family 2's line with two neighbors; one beat.
**Read.** Gold: the percent and the word that says what is asked ("tip", "discount", "sale price"). At a glance: a tip or tax is a small slice of the bill, so the answer is well under the meal and well over a few cents; a sale price is most of the price.
**The example.** "Student D's meal costs 27.79 dollars. Round the cost to the nearest 10 dollars, then use benchmarks to estimate the tip at 20%. about 6 | about 0.60 | about 60 | about 20 dollars." (1) Line 20 to 30, halfway 25; 27.79 drops right of it: 30. (2) Bar 0 to 30 dollars under 0 to 100%, cut in ten; one box is 10%: 30 ÷ 10 = 3 dollars, written in the gold box and copied. (3) 20% is two boxes: 6 dollars, shaded. (4) About 6 dollars.
**Quick second case (a piece leaves, and the ask is what remains).** "A jacket costs 87.49 dollars and is 50% off... estimate the sale price." Round: 90. Half off: 45 hatches rust and leaves; 90 − 45 = 45 remains. Say it: half off leaves half, so the discount and the sale price happen to match here; the question asked for what remains.
**Fast.** Round; slide the digits one place for 10%; build.
**Sense.** A tip is a small slice: 6 on a 28-dollar meal. The exact 20% of 27.79 is 5.56, so 6 is right.
**Check.** 3 is a tenth of 30; two of them are 6.
**Rule out.** The decoys are place slips and the price itself: 60 dollars is twice the meal, 0.60 is less than a dollar, 20 is the percent copied as dollars. Dim all three in one beat: "a tip is a small slice of the bill."
**Line.** "Ten percent is one tenth. Build the rest from it."
**Trap.** 10% of 30 written as 0.30 or 300 (a place slip from the shift): the box on the bar is 3 dollars wide, to scale. Answering the discount when the sale price is asked: the gold word and the hatched piece leaving.
**Kit.** `bar` partition and shade (exists), `cut`; `numberline` with drop and half (specified); `dim`.
**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, one component, the benchmark table fixed here.

**`percent-grid-model` — Read a shaded hundredths grid as a percent, a fraction or a decimal.**

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3c's "rate per 100" and 4.NF.C.6, "Use decimal notation for fractions with denominators 10 or 100... rewrite 0.62 as 62/100". The simplest-form case leans on 4.NF.A.1.
**Foundations first.** The opening is the whole foundation: the grid is one hundred percent, a cell is 1% = 1/100 = 0.01, a full column is 10% = one tenth. Count full columns as tens, loose cells as ones, as in place value. Define *simplest form* as the biggest box that fits (foundation 3): the grid regroups into equal blocks.
**Read.** Which name is asked: percent, fraction (and whether simplest form), or decimal. At a glance: more than five columns shaded is more than half; fewer than one column is less than a tenth, so the decimal starts 0.0.
**The example.** "What decimal names the shaded part of the grid? 7.5 | 0.75 | 0.25 | 0.075", 75 cells shaded. (1) Full columns: 10, 20, ... 70, each column lighting as it is counted. (2) Loose cells: 71, ... 75. (3) Three names, written side by side: 75%, 75/100, 0.75 (seventy-five hundredths, the digits in the tenths and hundredths places of the chart). (4) 0.75. Then one beat for the fraction question: the biggest box that fits 75 and 100 is 25; the grid's inner lines fade into four 25-cell blocks, three shaded: 3/4.
**Quick second case (less than a column).** "What percent of the grid is shaded?", 9 cells: no full column, so no tens; 9%, 9/100, 0.09, with the empty tenths place holding its zero.
**Fast.** Count columns by tens, cells by ones; that count is the percent; over 100 is the fraction; as hundredths is the decimal.
**Sense.** 75 shaded is most of the grid: more than a half.
**Check.** Three quarters of 100 cells is 75; 0.75 is 75 hundredths.
**Rule out.** 7.5 is seven and a half whole grids; 0.075 is seven and a half cells; 0.25 is the white part. All dim together: "the shaded part is most of one grid."
**Line.** "A cell is one percent. The grid is the whole."
**Trap.** 9 cells read as 0.9 (a place slip children really make): nine cells do not fill a column, and a column is a tenth. The decimal's zero in the tenths place is written on screen.
**Kit.** `blocks` flat with cell and strip highlights (exists); a heavy outline around a block of cells for the regroup (small addition); `dim`.
**Writer.** Cheaper model.

**`percent-forms-and-parts` — Rename among forms; find the whole from a part; find the percent from a part.**

*Three cases under one name.* (A) rename (0.125 as a percent; 55% in simplest form), (B) the whole from a part and a percent, (C) the percent from a part and a whole. B is the only place in grade 6 that practices "finding the whole, given a part and the percent" (the family design's gap); it deserves its own type. A overlaps `fraction-forms-blanks` and `staar-rename-percent-fraction-decimal`. The lesson works all three, B in full.
**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3c, both clauses: "Find a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100 ... solve problems involving finding the whole, given a part and the percent."
**Foundations first.** The opening; then the bar with the whole under 100% and the part under its percent, and the family's move said once: to find a whole, find one box (10%, or 1% when the percent is not a multiple of ten), then count to a hundred. Define *whole*, *part*.
**Read.** Which of the three is known: the percent and the part (whole asked), the part and the whole (percent asked), or just a number in another form. At a glance: a whole is bigger than its part; 60% is more than half, so the whole is less than twice the part. 41 of 50 is nearly all, so the percent is in the eighties.
**The example (B).** "156 students are on the team, which is 60% of the grade. How many students are in the grade?" Bar 0 to 100% on top, "?" at the end of the amount scale, 156 marked under 60%. (1) Cut in ten; 60% is six boxes, and those six hold 156 students. (2) One box: 156 ÷ 6 = 26, in the gold box, copied to all ten. (3) Ten boxes: 260; the "?" becomes 260. (4) One beat for any percent: cut into a hundred instead; 1% is 156 ÷ 60 = 2.6, and a hundred of those is 260. Same move, finer boxes.
**Quick second cases.** (C) "Student D got 41 of 50 questions right. What percent?" The whole must be 100: 50 to 100 is doubling, so both double (the tape rescale): 82 of 100, 82%. (A) "Write 0.125 as a percent": twelve and a half hundredths, 12.5%, on the bar between the 12 and 13 marks. "Which fraction in simplest form equals 55%? 1/55 | 55/10 | 11/20 | 55/1000": 55/100, biggest box 5: 11/20.
**Fast.** Whole: part ÷ percent for 1%, × 100 (dividing by the percent as hundredths is the same). Percent: part ÷ whole, then per hundred; when the whole divides 100, scale both. Rename: hundredths is the hinge.
**Sense.** 260 is more than 156 and less than 312. 82% is nearly all. 12.5% is about an eighth.
**Check.** 60% of 260 = 156 (six boxes of 26). 82% of 50 = 41. 11/20 × 5 = 55/100.
**Rule out (A).** 55/10 is more than one whole; 1/55 is under 2%; 55/1000 is 5.5%: dim together.
**Line.** "To find the whole, find one box, then count to a hundred."
**Trap.** 156 × 0.6 = 93.6, taking the percent of the part: a whole smaller than its part, impossible on the bar where 156 sits inside the whole. 41 of 50 answered 41%: the bar's top scale ends at 100, not 50. 0.125 read as 1.25%: the grid shows twelve cells and a half.
**Kit.** `bar` `mark` with `"bottom": "?"` and `"top"` for the known part (family 4.3), partition 10 and 100; `tape` `rescale`; `dim`.
**Writer.** Cheaper model from this concept, since each case is one component with its example fixed; the reviewer recomputes every quotient.

**`circle-graph-read` — A sector's percent, central angle and count.**

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3c, "a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100" (the quantity is 360 degrees or the number of people) and 7.RP.A.3 "multistep ratio and percent problems"; it also practices 4.MD.C.5a's angle as a fraction of a circle, unlinked. The picture is family 28's; the dependency is noted in the design.
**Foundations first.** Define *circle graph*, *sector* (one wedge), *central angle* (the wedge's angle at the center). The whole circle is everything, three ways at once: 100%, 360°, and all the people. Build the link once: one tenth of the turn is 36°, so 10% is 36°, and 1% is 3.6°. Then unroll the circle into the percent bar with a third scale under it: 0 to 100% | 0 to 360° | 0 to the total. The ticks line up: 10% over 36° over one tenth of the people.
**Read.** Which name is given and which is asked (percent, degrees or people), and whether a sector is missing (then the others are added and the whole has the rest). At a glance: a count is less than the total; an angle is less than 360; a wedge that looks like a quarter is about 25%, 90°.
**The example.** "The circle graph shows the favorite pet of 80 families. How many families are in the sector for Cat? 30 | 2400 | 56 | 24", Cat 30%. The Cat wedge goes gold; the three-scale bar beside it. (1) 10% of 80 families is 8 (one box). (2) 30% is three boxes: 24 families; read straight down from 30% on the bar. (3) 24.
**Second example, a different case: the missing sector.** "The circle graph shows how students travel to school. The graph does not show the percent for the last sector, Bike. The sectors fill the whole circle, which is 360°. What is the central angle for Bike? 54° | 306° | 15° | 45°". The named sectors shade end to end on the bar to 85%; the whole is 100%, so Bike is 15%. Read the degree scale under 15%: 15 × 3.6 = 54°. (The 180° variant is the same read at 50%.)
**Quick beats.** Percent to angle (5% → 18°) and angle to percent (126° → 35%) are the same bar read in the other direction: 36° is 10%, so 126° is 3.5 tens.
**Fast.** Ten percent is 36° and a tenth of the people; one percent is 3.6° and a hundredth; build from those. Missing sector: subtract the named shares from the whole in whichever unit you have.
**Sense.** Cat is under a third of the circle: fewer than 27 of 80. Bike is a thin wedge, far under a right angle.
**Check.** 24 of 80 is 3 of 10: 30%. 54 ÷ 3.6 = 15, and 85 + 15 = 100.
**Rule out.** Anything bigger than the whole (2,400 families, 342° or 306° for a small wedge); the given number copied into the other unit (30 families, 15°); the complement (56 families, 65%). Dim together with the one reason: "a wedge is a share of everyone."
**Line.** "The whole circle is one hundred percent, three hundred sixty degrees, and everyone. Ten percent is thirty-six degrees."
**Trap.** 30% of 80 as 30 × 80 = 2,400, the hundred forgotten: more families than exist; the bar's bottom scale ends at 80. Answering the rest of the circle: the gold wedge and the gold stretch of the bar are the same thing.
**Kit.** A sector shaded by fraction (an `angle` filled at full radius serves until family 28's `sector`); `bar` with a second lower scale (degrees), the one real addition; the unroll motion is a nicety, not required (cut from the circle to the bar).
**Writer.** Cheaper model: many wordings, one idea, every answer a read on one bar; the reviewer recomputes the degrees.

**`fraction-forms-blanks` — A fraction as a decimal and a percent.**

**Standard.** 5.NF.B.3, "Interpret a fraction as division of the numerator by the denominator (a/b = a ÷ b)"; 6.RP.A.3c; 7.NS.A.2d, "Convert a rational number to a decimal using long division". One wording: 3/4.
**Foundations first.** The opening; then: hundredths is the hinge. A fraction whose bottom fits into 100 (2, 4, 5, 10, 20, 25, 50) can be re-cut into hundredths, and hundredths is both the decimal and the percent. A fraction is also a division (family 9), which is the way that always works.
**Read.** Two blanks, one number: the decimal is the hundredths with a point; the percent is the same count with the sign. At a glance: 3/4 is more than half, so more than 0.5 and more than 50%.
**The example.** "Write 3/4 as a decimal and as a percent." (1) Bar 0 to 1 cut in four, three shaded. (2) Re-cut each quarter into hundredths: 100 ÷ 4 = 25 cells a quarter; the quarter lines stay. 3 × 25 = 75 shaded cells. (3) 75/100 = 0.75 = 75%, the three names under the bar. (4) The division, as the check and the sure method: 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75 in the long-division layout (4 into 3.00: 7 tenths, 28, 20 left, 5 hundredths).
**Quick second case, beyond the variant, because it comes next.** 3/8: the bottom does not fit 100, so divide: 0.375, 37.5%.
**Fast.** Bottom fits 100: scale both to hundredths. Otherwise divide top by bottom.
**Sense.** More than a half.
**Check.** 75/100 regrouped by 25 is 3/4; 0.75 × 4 = 3.
**Line.** "Hundredths is the hinge."
**Trap.** 3/4 written as 0.34 or 34% (the digits copied): 3 of 4 is not 34 of 100; the re-cut bar shows 75. 0.75 → 7.5% (a place slip): 75 cells.
**Kit.** `bar` partition 4 then 100 keeping the quarter boundaries (exists); `longdiv` (roadmap item 3) for the division layout.
**Writer.** Cheaper model.

**`mixed-quantity-discount` — Price a mixed-number quantity, then take a fraction or percent off.**

*Off grade and wrong form, first.* Filed under grade-6 percentages but it is 7.EE.B.3 ("numbers in any form... convert between forms as appropriate") with a markdown (7.RP.A.3 "markdowns") and a mixed-number times decimal product. Move it to grade 7. Prices must be shown with two places (12.60, 31.50, 75.40; the generator's 12.6 and 31.5 are wrong forms).
**Standard.** 7.EE.B.3 as above; 7.RP.A.3 "markdowns"; 5.NF.B.6 for the product.
**Foundations first.** A mixed number as a decimal: a half is 0.5, a quarter 0.25, three quarters 0.75 (a quarter of the flat), so 4½ yards is 4.5. The cost is a rate times a count (family 4): yards on the top line, dollars on the bottom, 6.60 at the 1 tick. *Off* means a piece of the total leaves; what remains is what you pay. One fifth off is 20%, two boxes of ten; one tenth off is 10%, one box.
**Read.** Gold: "pay" (what remains, not the discount). At a glance: about 4½ × 7 is about 30; a tenth off leaves a bit under 30.
**The example.** "Fabric costs 6.60 dollars per yard. Student C buys 4½ yards of it, and the store takes 10% off the total. How much does Student C pay?" (1) 4½ = 4.5, the half tick on the yards line. (2) Cost: four hops of 6.60 then a half hop: 26.40 + 3.30 = 29.70, stacked. (3) The total becomes a percent bar, 0 to 29.70 under 0 to 100%, cut in ten; 10% is one box: 2.97 (the digits one place right). The last box hatches rust and leaves. (4) Pay: 29.70 − 2.97 = 26.73, stacked. One beat: nine boxes remain, so 0.9 × 29.70 = 26.73 is the same answer in one multiplication.
**Quick second case (a fraction off).** "Rope costs 11.20 dollars per yard. Student C buys 4½ yards... one-fifth off." 4.5 × 11.20 = 50.40; one fifth is two boxes, 10.08 off; 40.32. "One fifth and twenty percent are the same two boxes."
**Fast.** Mixed number to decimal; multiply; take the piece; subtract. Or multiply the total by what remains (0.9, 4/5).
**Sense.** About 30, then a little less.
**Check.** 26.73 + 2.97 = 29.70; 29.70 ÷ 4.5 = 6.60.
**Line.** "Price the yards, then cut the piece off the total."
**Trap.** 4½ written 4.12 or 4.5 confused with 4.2: the half of the flat is 50 cells. Answering the discount (2.97) because the arithmetic ends there: the gold word "pay" and the remaining bar. (Taking 10% off the price per yard first gives the same answer; it is not a trap, and the lesson need not warn.)
**Kit.** `dnl` with a fractional tick (`stretch`), hops; `bar` `cut`; `stack` for the sums.
**Writer.** Cheaper model, with the reviewer recomputing every cent.

**`percent-amounts-blanks` — Percents of a whole, and whether each fits its limit.** *Reference screenplay using the `bar` kit.* This concept is the yardstick for that lesson.

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3c, "Find a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100".
**Foundations first.** The opening; 10% is one box, 1% a sliver of it; any percent is built from them. Define *within*: equal to the limit or less; *limit*: the most allowed.
**Read.** Two cases: two percents of one total (books), or two percents each against its own limit (the budget); the answer slots are the amounts, then "within" or "over". At a glance: 35% is a bit over a third; 6% is well under a tenth.
**The example (the budget, the harder read).** "A family earns 3,100 dollars a month. Rent is 35% of the income, and the rent limit is 1,045 dollars. Insurance is 6%... limit 196." Bar 0 to 3,100, cut in ten: one box 310. Rent: three boxes and a half: 930 + 155 = 1,085, shaded; the limit 1,045 is a mark on the amount scale, left of the shade's edge: over. Insurance: 1% is 31; 6% is 186; the limit 196 sits right of the edge: within.
**Quick second case.** "A library shelf holds 40 books. 15% are fiction and 20% are mystery." One box 4 books; 15% is a box and a half, 6; 20% two boxes, 8.
**Fast.** 10% and 1%, then build; or × the hundredths.
**Sense.** A third of 3,100 is about 1,033; 35% is a little more. **Check.** 1,085 ÷ 3,100 = 0.35; 6 + 8 = 14 books is 35% of 40.
**Line.** "Ten percent is one tenth. Build the rest from it." And once: "Within means equal to the limit or less."
**Trap.** Comparing the percent to the limit (35 against 1,045) instead of the amount; the limit is a mark on the amount scale and the comparison is where the shade stops. 15% of 40 read as 15 books: the bar has 40 at its end, not 100.
**Kit.** Exists (`bar` with marks). **Writer.** Done; a cheaper model's job going forward.

**`share-remainder-percent` — The rest of a whole, or the gap between two groups of it.**

**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3c, "Find a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100"; 7.RP.A.3 "multistep... percent problems".
**Foundations first.** The opening; then the one new idea: the groups are shares of one whole, so their percents add to 100%, and the remainder is what is left of 100. The bar is shaded in sections, end to end, one color per group, each labeled with its percent; the unshaded stretch is the rest.
**Read.** "The rest" is 100 minus the named percents, then turned into people. "How many more drive than bike" is a difference of two groups of the same whole. At a glance: bike is under a third of 400 since 30% is less than 33%: about 130.
**The example.** "A survey asked 400 people how they get to work. 35% drive, 10% take a bus and 25% walk. The rest bike. How many of the 400 people bike?" (1) Bar 0 to 400, one box 40 people, 1% is 4. (2) Shade the groups end to end: drive to 35, bus to 45, walk to 70; the empty stretch is bike. (3) 100 − 70 = 30%. (4) Three boxes of 40: 120.
**Quick second case (the gap).** "300 people. 50% drive, 20% bus, 10% walk. The rest bike. How many more drive than bike?" Bike is 20%. Drive minus bike is 50% − 20% = 30% of 300 = 90. Said once, because grade 7 will forbid it for different wholes: the percents can be subtracted here because both are shares of the same 300. Check: 150 − 60 = 90.
**Fast.** Add the named percents, take from 100, then boxes of 10%. For a gap between two groups of one whole, subtract the percents first.
**Sense.** The bike stretch is visibly under a third of the bar.
**Check.** 140 + 40 + 100 + 120 = 400.
**Line.** "The shares of one whole add to one hundred percent."
**Trap.** Answering 30 (the percent as the count): the bar's bottom scale reads 120 under 30%. Answering 70% of 400 = 280, forgetting "the rest": the question's gold word and the empty stretch.
**Kit.** `bar` needs adjacent shaded segments in different inks with labels (today `shade` is one interval); a small extension.
**Writer.** Cheaper model.

**`show-work-percent-count` — Six steps from a percent of a total to how many more are needed.**

*Not filmable as the template stands.* The six answer slots are 32% as a fraction in simplest form; 32% of 50; that plus the new 8; the new count as a percent; the count at a *target percent*; how many more to reach it. The target percent (64%, 62%, 80%, 72%, 75%, 56% across the variants) appears nowhere in the prompt, so steps 5 and 6 cannot be read from the problem. The template must state the target ("Student N wants to reach 75% of the list") and name each step's question beside its slot before any lesson teaches it. The concept below assumes that fix.
**Standard.** 6.RP.A.3c, both clauses ("a percent of a quantity as a rate per 100"; and the percent from a part, the whole given); 7.RP.A.3 "multistep... percent problems".
**Foundations first.** The opening; then the whole story on one bar with two scales, percent above and books below, read in both directions. The key fact found first: what one book is worth in percent, 100 ÷ 20 = 5%. After that every step is a read.
**Read.** The six slots, in order, each named on the board. Filled-in slots ("Some steps are filled in") are facts to use, not to recompute. At a glance: 45% is a bit under half of 20, so about 9; two more books is 10 more percent.
**The example.** "Student N has read 45% of the 20 books on a reading list. This year Student N will read 2 more. [Target: 75%.]" Bar 0 to 100% over 0 to 20 books; each book 5%. (1) 45% as a fraction: 45/100, biggest box 5: 9/20. (2) 45% of 20: 45 ÷ 5 = 9 books, shaded teal. (3) 9 + 2 = 11; a rust piece of two books grows the shade. (4) 11 books of 20: 11 × 5 = 55%, read off the top scale. (5) The target, 75%, marked: 75 ÷ 5 = 15 books. (6) From 11 to 15: 4 more, the gap between the shade's edge and the mark.
**Fast.** Find what one is worth (100 ÷ total); then multiply or divide by it at every step. (When the total divides 100 awkwardly, 1% of the total serves instead.)
**Sense.** 9 is a little under half of 20; two books move the percent up 10; the target is three quarters, 15.
**Check.** 11 ÷ 20 = 0.55; 15 of 20 is three quarters; 9 + 2 + 4 = 15.
**Line.** "Find what one is worth, and every step is reading the bar."
**Trap.** Adding the 2 to the percent (45% + 2 = 47%) instead of to the books: the bar has two scales and two books is a 10% piece, drawn to size. Simplifying 45/100 by dividing one number only (family 3's regroup fades both strips).
**Kit.** `bar` with two scales, `grow` within 100%, `mark` for the target (mostly exists); a board card that shows the six step slots as the app does, filled one by one.
