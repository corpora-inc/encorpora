# Cavalieri principle: `cavalieri-volume-reasoning` (Geometry)

**First, the filing.** The skill is linked to HSG.GMD.A.2, "give an informal argument using Cavalieri's principle for the formulas for the volume of a sphere." No variant does that. What every variant does is *use* the principle: HSG.GMD.A.1's clause "Use dissection arguments, Cavalieri's principle, and informal limit arguments" is its true home, with HSG.GMD.A.3 ("use volume formulas for cylinders, pyramids, cones... to solve problems") for the oblique cylinder and the pair question, 7.G.B.6 (right prisms) for the sheets, and 8.G.C.9 (the cylinder formula). Relink to A.1 and A.3; A.2 stays practiced by nothing, and the sphere argument (hemisphere = can minus cone) wants its own short type rather than a tail on this one, which already holds four kinds. The type is a mixed one by the design's own count: scaled slices, a leaning stack, an oblique cylinder, and "which pair must be equal." One principle, four readings; the lesson teaches the principle once and reads four problems against it.

**Standard.** HSG.GMD.A.1, "Cavalieri's principle"; HSG.GMD.A.3, "use volume formulas for cylinders, pyramids... to solve problems."

**Foundations first** (foundations 7, 11, with 3 and 8 named in one line each). Say what we already know: a prism or cylinder is a stack of identical slices, and volume is base times height. Define *cross-section* in one line ("the flat shape where a level plane cuts the solid", grade 7's slice). Define *height measured straight up*: the distance from the ground to the top along a plumb line, drawn in blue with a right-angle mark at the ground; a slanted side is not the height, and is drawn in rust. Then build the principle, not state it: a straight stack of 15 sheets, each 10 by 6 by 2, count label 1,800. **Lean**: the stack is pushed; every sheet slides a little past the one below; the height bracket stays 30 (fifteen 2s), the count label stays 1,800. Nothing was added, nothing removed; the sheets are the same sheets. Then the smooth version: **sweep** one gold plane up through the straight stack and the leaning one side by side on one ground line; at every height both sections are a 10 by 6 rectangle, area 60, labeled live. Say the principle in plain words: *two solids of the same height, whose slices have the same area at every height, have the same volume*. And its cousin, shown by the same sweep over a 2-wide and a 1-wide slab: if one slice is always k times the other, the volume is k times. The scratch sketch the child should make: the leaning solid, a plumb line for h, "V = B × h".

**Read.** The question is always one of: a volume from k times the slices; the volume of something leaning; which pair passes the two tests (same height, same slice area at every height). At a glance: a leaning solid's volume is the upright one's with the same base and the *straight-up* height; a number labeled "slanted side" is not used; "k times the area at every height" means multiply the volume by k, once, since a slice is already an area; a pyramid can never pass the test against a prism, because its slices shrink.

**Example A, the leaning stack** (full). "15 identical foam sheets, each a rectangle 10 cm by 6 cm and 2 cm thick, are stacked straight, then pushed so the stack leans. Volume of the leaning stack?" Choices 270, 900, 120, 1800. (1) One sheet: 10 × 6 × 2 = 120 cm³, written under its blue-bracketed edges. (2) Straight stack: 15 × 120 = 1,800, the count label ticking by sheets. (3) Lean: the stack tips; the label holds; "same sheets, same volume". (4) 1,800 cm³. Rule out first: 120 is one sheet, 900 is half the stack, 270 is 15 × (10 + 6 + 2), the edges added; all three dim together with "fifteen sheets of 120 can only be 1,800".

**Example B, the oblique cylinder** (quick; adds one idea, the decoy length). "Radius 5 cm, perpendicular height 8 cm, slanted side 10 cm. Volume in terms of π." Choices 200π, 250π, 250π/3, 200π/3. Picture: the leaning cylinder beside its upright twin of radius 5 and height 8 on one ground line; the sweep shows the same disc, area 25π, at every height; the slant drawn in rust, the plumb height in blue. Board: V = πr² × h, π × 25 × 8 = 200π cm³. The 6-8-10 triangle is noted in one beat only to say the lean is 6 and irrelevant.

**Example C, k times the slices** (full). "X and Y are both 10 cm tall. At every height the slice of X has 2 times the area of the slice of Y. Y has volume 132 cm³. Volume of X?" Choices 264, 528, 66, 132. Sweep through two slabs of equal height, the sections labeled A and 2A at every height. Each slice of X is twice a slice of Y, so the stack of them is twice the stack: V = 2 × 132 = 264 cm³. The 3/2 variant is the same beat: 3/2 × 48 = 72.

**Example D, which pair** (short, the reading kind). Four choices; the lesson runs the two tests on each with the sweep. Different heights (9 and 14): fails test one. Cylinder and pyramid, same base and height: the sweep shows the pyramid's slice shrinking to a point while the cylinder's stays 49; fails test two (the pyramid is a third, a fact the learner knows from 8). Base areas 49 and 54: fails test two. Cylinder and square prism, both 9 tall, both base 49: every slice is the base, 49 at every height; equal.

**Fast.** Leaning: base times the straight-up height, same as upright. k times at every height: multiply the volume by k, once. Pair: same height, and same base area for two stacks (prism or cylinder); a pointed solid never matches a stack.

**Sense.** A leaning stack is the same sheets, so it cannot be smaller or larger. The cylinder's box test: an upright can 10 wide and 8 tall sits in an 800 box; three quarters is 600, and 200π ≈ 628. For k times, X's slices are bigger, so X's volume is bigger, and bigger by k, not k².

**Check.** Sheets: 1,800 ÷ 15 = 120, one sheet. Cylinder: compute the upright twin, π × 25 × 8, and it is the same number. k times: 264 ÷ 2 = 132. Pair: say the two tests aloud for the chosen pair.

**Rule out.** Any volume that uses the slant (250π) is out: a slant is never a height. Thirds (200π/3, 250π/3) are cone volumes; nothing here is pointed. For k times, the unchanged value and the shrunken value dim together ("X's slices are bigger, so X is bigger"), then k² (528, 108) dims: "two times an area is two times, not four". Pairs: a pyramid against a prism, unequal heights, unequal bases, each out in one phrase.

**Line.** "Same slices, same volume. Height is measured straight up."

**Trap.** Two real ones. Using the slanted side as the height: students reach for the longest labeled length; the lesson draws the slant in rust from the start and drops the blue plumb line with its right-angle mark before any number is written. Treating "2 times the area" as a scale factor and cubing or squaring it: students who have just met similar solids expect an exponent; the sweep labels the areas 2A and A at every height so the doubling is visibly already in the slice, and the board writes V = 2 × 132 under the pair of labeled sections. "The leaning stack holds less" is less common at this grade, and the lean motion kills it in passing.

**Kit.** `sheets` with `lean` and `sweep` with live area labels are in the family design. Beyond them: `cylinder` and `prism` need an `oblique: [dx, 0]` offset so a continuous slanted solid draws in the family view with its slant labeled and a plumb `height` with a right-angle mark; `sweep` must take a ratio so the two sections label `A` and `2A` (or `\tfrac32 A`) rather than numbers when no numbers are given; `pyramid` with `sweep` so its shrinking section is seen beside a cylinder's constant one; `dim` for choices.

**Writer.** Strongest. The lesson is proof by motion (lean, sweep) and four readings of one idea; a weaker writer narrates the motion or recites four recipes. Length at the family's patient pace is about five to six minutes; if review finds that too long, the pair kind is the clean cut into its own type.
