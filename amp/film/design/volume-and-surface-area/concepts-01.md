# Volume and surface area, part 1: the grade 5 and 6 concepts

Ten problem types across three skills (`volume-cubes`, `prism-volume`, `fractional-prism-volume`). Each stands on foundations 1 to 5 of the family design and uses the `solid` component in the one isometric view. Where the example numbers must match the app's figure, the writer confirms them against `facts.ts` for the seed; the method does not depend on them.

A note before the concepts, on where the app and the standards part ways here (collected in section 6 of the family design, repeated only where it changes a lesson): `cube-layer-count` is one beat of `unit-cube-volume` and is designed as such; no problem gives a base area and a height (5.MD.C.5b's V = b × h), so the lessons teach B × h from the base layer and the app should add a type; no composite is real-world (5.MD.C.5c); and no fraction-edge problem shows the packing and the formula side by side on one problem, which is the whole sentence of 6.G.A.2, so `unit-fraction-cube-packing` does that in the lesson even though each variant asks only one of the two.

### Grade 5: volume-cubes (5.MD.C.3, 5.MD.C.4)

#### `unit-cube-volume` — Count the cubes in a solid.

**Standard.** 5.MD.C.3a and b ("a cube with side length 1 unit, called a 'unit cube,' is said to have 'one cubic unit' of volume"; "a solid figure which can be packed without gaps or overlaps using n unit cubes is said to have a volume of n cubic units") and 5.MD.C.4 ("measure volumes by counting unit cubes"). The second variant ("rectangular prism") also reaches into 5.MD.C.5a, since the fast count is a product.

**Foundations first** (foundations 1 to 3). Say what *volume* is: the amount of space inside a solid. Draw one unit cube in the view, with its unit lines, and label it "1 cubic unit": one unit long, one wide, one tall. Say what a *cubic unit* is: the space one of these takes up. Then: a solid's volume is how many of these pack it with no gaps and nothing hanging out. Then build the thing in front of the learner. An empty 6 × 2 × 4 box outline on the ground line; cubes drop in one at a time, **back row first, bottom layer first**, the count ticking 1, 2, 3 ... 6, then the front row 7 ... 12. Say it as it happens: "the back row goes in first, so when the front row covers it, those six cubes are still there." Name the *layer*: one cube tall, twelve cubes. The second layer drops (13 ... 24), the third, the fourth; the back-row cubes of the lower layers disappear from view and the voice says so once more: "you can't see them now. They are still there." Define *height* as how many layers.

**Read.** "Each small cube is 1 cubic unit. What is the volume, in cubic units?" The gold word is *cubic units*: this asks how many cubes, not how many faces and not how long an edge is. At a glance: the answer is a whole number of cubes; the top face shows how many are in one layer, and the side shows how many layers.

**The example.** The 6 × 2 × 4 prism just built (the writer matches the app's seed; 48 is the app's answer; three different edges so length, width and height are told apart). (1) The top face goes gold: six along, two across; "one layer is six times two, twelve cubes." The layer's count label reads 12. (2) `lift`: the four layers separate with a gap, each labeled 12. (3) `restack`: they drop back one at a time; the total counts 12, 24, 36, 48. (4) On the board: 6 × 2 = 12 cubes in a layer; 4 layers; 12 × 4 = 48; answer 48 cubic units. (5) One beat, foundation 3: `turn onto front`: the box tips onto its 6 × 4 face; the layers relabel as two layers of 24; the count label never moves. "Any face can be the bottom. The pile is the same pile."

**Second example, because the type holds a different case.** "Every column is filled to the bottom": a solid with uneven columns, a staircase with a 3 × 3 footprint and rows of heights 6, 5, 4 from back to front (45; the writer matches the seed). Here there is no single layer count, so the count goes by *columns*: the top view is a map of heights. The gold highlight walks the columns row by row; the count ticks by column heights: "six, twelve, eighteen" for the back row, "twenty-three, twenty-eight, thirty-three", "thirty-seven, forty-one, forty-five." Board: 3 × 6 = 18; 3 × 5 = 15; 3 × 4 = 12; 18 + 15 + 12 = 45. Then the check by layers: four full layers of 9 (36), a layer of 6, a layer of 3: 45 again.

**Fast.** For a box: count the top (length times width), count the layers up one side, multiply. For uneven columns: each column is as tall as its top cube; add the columns, or count full layers and add the partial ones.

**Sense.** 48 is about a dozen cubes four times over; the picture shows four layers of a dozen. For the staircase: more than four full layers of 9 (36), less than six (54).

**Check.** Count the other way: by layers if you counted columns, or turn the box onto another face and multiply the new base by the new height; the same number.

**Line.** "The hidden cubes count too. Count a layer, then count the layers."

**Trap.** Children read the picture as flat and count the cubes they can see (the top, the front, the side), or count faces. The fill from the back row, covered as the front row arrives, is the whole answer to this: the learner watched the hidden cubes placed. A second real one, for the uneven solid: a column drawn only two tall is read as two cubes stacked on nothing, so the learner skips the cubes beneath; "filled to the bottom" is said and the column's cubes are dropped in from the ground up.

**Kit.** `solid` with `cubes`, `fill` (by cube, by row, by layer, by column, back row first), `lift`, `restack`, `turn onto`, `count`, gold `highlight` on `p.face.top` and `p.column.i.j`. This is also the 3D cube kit for place value: build it once.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: the picture is born here, the fill order and the covered cubes are the lesson, and a weak author will describe the motion instead of staging it.

#### `cube-layer-count` — How many cubes in the bottom layer.

**First:** this is one beat of `unit-cube-volume` and should not be a separate concept or a separate film of full length. It gets a short film (about ninety seconds) built from the same screenplay pieces: the foundation of the layer and the lift. Written here only to say exactly which pieces.

**Standard.** 5.MD.C.4 ("measure volumes by counting unit cubes") in its first step; the layer is the array that the standard's product multiplies.

**Foundations first** (foundation 2). The unit cube, named once. Then: a *layer* is one cube tall. In a box every layer holds the same number of cubes, because every layer is the same length and width; the bottom layer is the one the box stands on, and it is hidden under all the others, which is exactly why we count the top instead.

**Read.** "How many cubes are in its bottom layer?" Gold: *bottom layer*. At a glance: this is a count of one layer, so it is smaller than the whole solid; the top face is an array with the same count.

**The example.** A 4 × 3 × 5 prism in cubes (12 is the app's answer; the writer matches the seed). (1) The top face goes gold: four along, three across. "Four rows of three: twelve." The count label reads 12. (2) `lift`: the five layers separate, each labeled 12; the bottom one, now visible, is a plain 4 × 3 array with its twelve cubes countable. (3) Board: 4 × 3 = 12; answer 12 cubes. (4) `restack`, one beat: "sixty cubes in all, but the question asked for one layer."

**Fast.** Multiply the two edges of the top face.

**Sense.** The bottom layer is one slice of the pile, not the pile.

**Check.** Count the top face's cubes one by one on the gold grid: twelve.

**Line.** "Every layer is the same. Count the top; that is the bottom."

**Trap.** Counting only the cubes of the bottom layer that show (the front edge and the side edge, 4 + 3 − 1 = 6), or answering with the whole volume. The lift puts the bottom layer in plain view as a 4 × 3 array; the last beat says the whole count aloud and sets it aside.

**Kit.** Nothing beyond `unit-cube-volume`'s.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, from this concept and the finished `unit-cube-volume` screenplay as reference; the lesson is one picture and one product.

### Grade 5: prism-volume (5.MD.C.5)

#### `prism-volume-edges` — Volume from three edge lengths.

**Standard.** 5.MD.C.5b ("apply the formulas V = l × w × h and V = b × h for rectangular prisms to find volumes of right rectangular prisms with whole-number edge lengths"), and 5.MD.C.5a's second sentence in the one "turn" beat ("represent threefold whole-number products as volumes, e.g., to represent the associative property").

**Foundations first** (foundations 2 and 3). "You already know a rectangle's area is length times width, and that a box built from cubes has a bottom layer and a stack of layers." Now the box has no cubes drawn, only lengths: a plain 9 × 5 × 8 box, to scale, labeled 9 cm, 5 cm, 8 cm on three edges. Say what *centimeter cube* and *cubic centimeter* are: a cube one centimeter on every edge, and the space it fills. Then build the layer the learner cannot see: unit lines appear on the bottom layer only; "nine along, five across: one layer is nine times five, forty-five cubes, which is the area of the base." The base goes gold, labeled B = 45. The blue height bracket: "eight centimeters tall means eight layers." Then the formula, in the figure's colors: V = **B** × **h**, and under it V = l × w × h, "the same thing, because B is l × w."

**Read.** "What is the volume of this rectangular prism, in cubic centimeters?" Gold: *volume* and *cubic*: the inside, counted in cubes. At a glance: three lengths are given, so it is a multiplication; the answer is a number of cubic centimeters, bigger than any one edge, and ends in a zero here because 5 and 8 are factors.

**The example.** 9 × 5 × 8 (the app's 360; the writer matches the seed and keeps three different edges). (1) Base: 9 × 5 = 45, written under the gold layer. (2) Height: 8 layers, blue. (3) V = 45 × 8 = 360; the layers `lift` briefly and `restack` while the count ticks 45, 90 ... 360. (4) The unit is written: 360 cm³; "cubic, because we counted cubes." (5) Foundation 3, one beat: `turn onto` the 5 × 8 face: "now the base is five times eight, forty, and there are nine layers: forty times nine is three hundred sixty. Any face can be the bottom." The count label did not change.

**Fast.** Multiply the three edges, in any order that is easy (5 × 8 = 40 first, then × 9). Write cm³.

**Sense.** About 10 × 5 × 8 = 400; 360 is right. A box 9 by 5 by 8 is the size of a shoebox; hundreds of centimeter cubes fit a shoebox.

**Check.** Multiply in a different order, or divide back: 360 ÷ 8 = 45 = 9 × 5.

**Line.** "Base times height, for any stack."

**Trap.** Two real ones. Multiplying two edges and stopping (45, the base area given as the volume), which the blue height and the layer stack end: a layer is not a box. Adding the three edges (22), a habit from perimeter; the picture of 45 cubes in one layer alone makes 22 impossible.

**Kit.** `solid` prism with `labels` and the unit, base-layer unit lines on request (`p.base` with `cubes` on only that layer), `p.height` in blue, `turn onto`, `count`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one component, one formula, named actions; `--dry` catches the arithmetic.

#### `prism-missing-edge` — Find the edge marked ?.

**Standard.** 5.MD.C.5b (the formula applied "to solve real world and mathematical problems"); the missing factor is 4.OA.A.4 and 3.OA.A.4 territory the learner owns.

**Foundations first** (foundation 4). "V = B × h is a multiplication sentence. You already know a multiplication sentence with a missing number is a division." The picture is the missing-edge picture: a 4 × 5 base layer drawn in cubes and gold, labeled 20, and above it nothing but a dashed outline with a blue bracket marked ?. "We know the volume, sixty. Each layer is twenty. How many layers make sixty?"

**Read.** "This rectangular prism has a volume of 60 cubic centimeters. What is the length of the edge marked ?" Gold: the *?*, and *volume* (it is given, not asked). At a glance: two edges are known, one is asked; the answer is a length in centimeters, not a volume, and it is smaller than the volume.

**The example.** Volume 60, edges 4 and 5, height unknown (the app's 3). (1) The two known edges make the base: 4 × 5 = 20 cubes in a layer, gold. (2) `fill` by layer: layers land while the count ticks 20, 40, 60. "Three layers." The blue bracket grows with the layers to 3. (3) Board, as a missing factor: 4 × 5 × ? = 60; 20 × ? = 60; ? = 60 ÷ 20 = 3; answer 3 cm. (4) One beat for the other shape of the question: if the ? is on a base edge instead, "any face can be the bottom": `turn onto` so the two known edges are the base, and it is the same division.

**Fast.** Multiply the two known edges; divide the volume by that.

**Sense.** The box drawn to the found height must look like the problem's box: three layers under 60 looks like 60. A missing edge is always much smaller than the volume.

**Check.** Multiply back: 4 × 5 × 3 = 60.

**Line.** "A missing edge is a missing factor: divide the volume by the layer."

**Trap.** Dividing the volume by only one known edge (60 ÷ 4 = 15, 60 ÷ 5 = 12): the learner has two numbers and divides by the first one seen. The layer of 20 cubes counted on screen, and the stack that reaches 60 in three, make 15 layers visibly wrong. Also multiplying the two edges and stopping (20), answered as the edge; the found edge is drawn to its length, and 20 would be a tower.

**Kit.** `solid` prism with the base layer in cubes and the rest as a dashed outline, `fill` by layer with the count, the blue bracket that grows with the fill, `turn onto`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: divide, draw, multiply back.

#### `composite-prism-volume` — A solid made of two boxes.

**Standard.** 5.MD.C.5c ("recognize volume as additive. Find volumes of solid figures composed of two non-overlapping right rectangular prisms by adding the volumes of the non-overlapping parts"). The clause "applying this technique to solve real world problems" is not practiced; the type is abstract.

**Foundations first** (foundation 4). "Two boxes that do not overlap: the cubes in one plus the cubes in the other." Say it with cubes: an L-shaped solid in unit cubes, the two boxes in two tones of teal, and the count label counting all of them once. Then define what the problem's figure gives and does not give: the lengths written on it are the *outside* lengths; one length of one of the boxes is never written, and it must be found by taking away. "Same depth" is defined: both boxes go back the same distance, so the depth is written once and used twice.

**Read.** "This solid is made of two rectangular prisms of the same depth that do not overlap. What is its total volume?" Gold: *two prisms*, *total*. At a glance: it is two multiplications and an addition; one of the six lengths is missing from the figure and is a difference of two that are given; the depth is shared.

**The example.** An L-shaped solid, drawn to scale, depth 3: a column 2 wide and 5 tall on the left with a foot running right, total width 6, foot height 2 (the app's 54; the writer matches the seed's lengths, keeping one derived length). Given on the figure: 6, 5, 2 (column width), 2 (foot height), 3 (depth). (1) The cut: a dashed vertical plane down the inside corner; `cut`, and the two boxes `slide` apart on the ground line. (2) The column keeps 2, 5, 3. The foot keeps 2 (tall) and 3 (deep), and its length appears as it is found: the 6 bracket and the 2 bracket shown together, "six across in all, two of them are the column, so the foot is six minus two, four." The derived `dim` 4 drops onto the foot. (3) Board: column 2 × 5 × 3 = 30; foot 4 × 2 × 3 = 24; 30 + 24 = 54; answer 54 cm³. (4) The other cut, quickly: a horizontal plane along the top of the foot; the bottom slab is 6 × 2 × 3 = 36, the upper column is 2 × (5 − 2) × 3 = 18; 36 + 18 = 54. "Cut it either way, same cubes." (5) The third view, one beat: the whole 6 × 5 × 3 box is 90; the rust notch, 4 × 3 × 3 = 36, slides out; 90 − 36 = 54.

**Fast.** Cut at the inside corner so both pieces are boxes; find the one missing length by subtraction; multiply each; add. Choose the cut that makes the subtraction easy.

**Sense.** The L is most of the 6 × 5 × 3 box, which is 90; 54 is more than half of it and less than all of it. Or: the front face is 18 squares and the solid is 3 deep: 54.

**Check.** The other cut gives the same total; or the big box minus the notch does.

**Line.** "Cut it into boxes, then add. One length is hiding: it is a difference."

**Trap.** The one that actually happens: using an outside length for a piece after the cut, so the shared part is counted twice (the foot taken as 6 long: 6 × 2 × 3 = 36 added to the column's 30 gives 66). The pieces sliding apart with their own brackets, and the 4 appearing as "6 minus 2" on the figure, make the double count impossible to draw. Also, less often, multiplying the two pieces' volumes or adding only one.

**Kit.** `composite` solids with shared outline, `cut` with a dashed plane and pieces as separate solids (`p.1`, `p.2`) with their own `labels` and `count`, derived `dim` shown on the word, `slide` on the ground line, rust notch via a second composite piece.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: choosing the cut and staging the derived length is the lesson, and the trap lives in the reading of the figure.

#### `mcas-prism-range-select` — Choose the two prisms whose volumes together fit.

**Standard.** 5.MD.C.5 (volumes by multiplying edges) with 5.MD.C.3 and 5.MD.C.4 in the cube-built variant; the pairing is a reading and reasoning task on top.

**Foundations first.** Nothing new to build; name what is borrowed in one line ("volume is length times width times height; a box built from cubes is counted by layers"). What must be taught first is the *shape of the question*: five prisms, and a condition about *two of them added together*. Say plainly that no single prism answers it; the answer is a pair; and the way in is to find all five volumes before looking at the condition, written in a small table (prism | volume) so nothing is recomputed.

**Read.** "Choose the 2 prisms whose volumes added together equal 77 cubic centimeters" (or "are greater than 26 and less than 32"). Gold: *2*, *added together*, and the condition. At a glance: any prism whose volume alone is already bigger than the target (or than the top of the range) cannot be in the pair.

**The example.** A 4 × 4 × 2, B 6 × 8 × 4, C 8 × 4 × 8, D 3 × 3 × 5, E 6 × 2 × 3; target "equal 77". (1) Table filled one row at a time, each prism lit in the figure as its row fills: 32, 192, 256, 45, 36. (2) Rule out: 192 and 256 are each bigger than 77 by themselves; B and C dim together. (3) Three remain, so three pairs: 32 + 45 = 77, 32 + 36 = 68, 45 + 36 = 81, written as three lines. (4) A and D; both pills light; "a prism counts only with its partner."

**Second example, because the figure changes the reading.** The cube-built variant with a range (the app's answer is B and D; the writer takes each prism's cube dimensions from the seed). Each small prism is counted the grade-5 way, top face times layers, into the same table; then the same two moves: dim any prism over the top of the range, pair the rest, keep the one pair inside the range. Brief: the method was shown fully.

**Fast.** Table of five volumes first. Dim the too-big ones. Pair what is left and add; with three left there are only three sums.

**Sense.** Exactly one pair fits; if two pairs seem to, a volume was miscomputed.

**Check.** Add the chosen pair once more against the condition; count the choices: two.

**Rule out.** A prism whose volume alone exceeds the target or the range's top. Then any pair whose sum misses.

**Line.** "Find all five first. Then pair."

**Trap.** Reading the condition as being about each prism alone (choosing a prism near 77, or two prisms each inside the range). The table and the "added together" gold word, with the pair sums written as sums, end it. Also stopping after one prism is found to "work".

**Kit.** `table` (exists) beside five small solids on one ground line at one scale, each `highlight`able with its `labels` or cubes; `dim` for choices.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, once `dim` and the multi-solid figure exist; the reasoning is fixed and the arithmetic checkable.

#### `prism-volume-select` — Select the prisms with a volume of 24.

**Standard.** 5.MD.C.5b, six times over.

**Foundations first.** One line borrowed ("length times width times height"), then the only new thing: six prisms drawn with their edges, and the task is to find the two whose product is a given number, so the question is a filter, not a computation of one answer. Say before any arithmetic what makes a product 24: 24 is 2 × 3 × 4, 1 × 4 × 6, 2 × 2 × 6, 1 × 3 × 8, 1 × 2 × 12; every edge must divide 24.

**Read.** "Select the 2 prisms that have a volume of 24 cubic centimeters." Gold: *2* and *24*. At a glance: an edge of 5 or 7 cannot be in a prism of volume 24 (it does not go into 24); any prism with two edges whose product is already past 24 is out.

**The example.** A 2 × 3 × 5, B 2 × 3 × 4, C 1 × 4 × 6, D 4 × 6 × 2, E 3 × 3 × 3, F 5 × 2 × 2 (B and C are the app's answer; the writer matches the seed). (1) Glance: A and F have an edge of 5; they dim together with that one reason. (2) The rest are multiplied, each lit as its line is written: B 24, C 24, D 48, E 27. (3) D and E dim. (4) B and C are chosen; the count label on each reads 24 cm³.

**Fast.** Throw out any prism with an edge that does not divide the target; multiply the rest.

**Sense.** The two chosen prisms look different (long and thin, short and wide) and hold the same cubes; that is normal, and the layers show it (B is four layers of 6, C is six layers of 4).

**Check.** Two chosen; both products recomputed in another order.

**Rule out.** An edge that does not divide 24; two edges whose product exceeds 24.

**Line.** "Every edge has to fit into the volume."

**Trap.** Stopping after two edges: 4 × 6 = 24 on prism D, so D is chosen though it is 48. The third edge is lit in blue as "two layers of twenty-four." Less often, edges that add to the target.

**Kit.** Six small solids in one view at one scale, each with `labels` and `count`; `dim`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model.

### Grade 6: fractional-prism-volume (6.G.A.2)

#### `unit-fraction-cube-packing` — Count the small cubes; then size them.

**Standard.** 6.G.A.2's first sentence, whole: "find the volume of a right rectangular prism with fractional edge lengths by packing it with unit cubes of the appropriate unit fraction edge lengths, and show that the volume is the same as would be found by multiplying the edge lengths." The app asks each variant for only the count or only the volume; the lesson does both on one problem, because the standard's "show that" is the point.

**Foundations first** (foundation 5). Start from the grade-5 unit cube, labeled "edge 1, volume 1." `subdivide` into 2: it cuts in half along each edge; eight small cubes fall apart and are counted; one is pulled aside and labeled "edge ½, volume ⅛." Say why: ½ × ½ × ½ = ⅛, "half of half of half," the same move as the unit square cut into quarters, now in three directions. Then once, as words only: cutting into thirds each way makes 27 cubes of 1⁄27; quarters, 64 of 1⁄64. "To size a small cube, multiply its edge by itself three times." Then the second idea: how many small cubes fit along an edge is the edge divided by the small edge: 3½ ÷ ½ = 7, shown by seven half-cubes laid along a 3½ bracket.

**Read.** "A rectangular prism is 3½ cm long, 1 cm wide and 2½ cm tall. It is packed with cubes that have edges of ½ cm. What is the volume, in cubic centimeters?" (or "How many of these cubes fill the prism?"). Gold: *volume* or *how many cubes*; they are different answers, and one beat says so before any work: the count is a whole number of cubes; the volume is a number of cubic centimeters, and it is smaller than the count because each cube is less than one.

**The example.** 3½ × 1 × 2½ packed with ½-cm cubes (the app's 35⁄4 variant; three different counts so the edges are told apart). (1) Along the length: 3½ ÷ ½ = 7; seven half-cubes line the bottom front edge, counted. Along the width: 1 ÷ ½ = 2. Up the height: 2½ ÷ ½ = 5. (2) The box `fill`s with half-cubes by layer: 7 × 2 = 14 in a layer, five layers; the count ticks 14, 28, 42, 56, 70. "Seventy cubes." (3) Size them: each is ⅛; 70 × ⅛ = 70⁄8 = 35⁄4 = 8¾ cm³. (4) The other route on the same board: 7⁄2 × 1 × 5⁄2 = 35⁄4. "The same number. Packing and multiplying agree; that is why the formula works for fractions too." Answer 35⁄4 cm³.

**Quick second case** (a different ask): "How many of these cubes fill the prism?" on 2½ × 2½ × 1½ with ½-foot cubes: 5, 5, 3; 5 × 5 × 3 = 75 cubes, and stop. "It asked for cubes, not volume." Three or four beats.

**Fast.** Divide each edge by the small edge to get three counts; multiply them for the count. For volume, multiply the count by the small cube's volume (edge cubed), or multiply the three edges as fractions; use one, check with the other.

**Sense.** The box is about 3 by 1 by 3, so about 9 cubic centimeters; 8¾ is right, and 70 is not (seventy would be a much bigger box). The volume is less than the count whenever the cube is smaller than a unit.

**Check.** The two routes agree (70 × ⅛ and 7⁄2 × 1 × 5⁄2); or 35⁄4 ÷ ⅛ = 70 cubes.

**Line.** "Half an edge is an eighth of a cube. Count them, then size them."

**Trap.** Two that are real and both appear as decoys in the sister type. Giving the count as the volume (70 cubic centimeters): the beat that says "the volume is less than the count" and the box-size sense check end it. Believing a ½-edge cube has volume ½ (or ¼, from squaring): the eight small cubes falling out of one unit cube, with "edge ½, volume ⅛" on the sample, end it.

**Kit.** `subdivide` (into 2, 3, 4) with the sample cube and its tag; `fill` with small cubes and a `count` that counts them; `labels` carrying mixed numbers; brackets subdivided by the small edge (seven ticks on the 3½ bracket).

**Writer.** Strongest writer: the shrunk unit is the bridge from the unit square to three dimensions, and the two routes must land on one board in a way the learner sees as the same count.

#### `mcas-cube-prism-statements` — Which two statements are true?

**Standard.** 6.G.A.2, first sentence; the statements are its clauses (count along an edge, number of cubes, one cube's volume, volume by multiplying), and the false ones are the misconceptions.

**Foundations first.** The same foundation as `unit-fraction-cube-packing`, shorter (the sample cube cut into eight, "edge ½, volume ⅛"; counts along an edge are the edge divided by the small edge). Then the shape of the task: five statements about one packed prism; each is decided on its own, true or false, with a line of work; exactly two are true. Say before starting what each kind of statement is checked against: a length (count × small edge), a count (three counts multiplied), one cube's volume (edge cubed), the volume (count × cube, or the edges multiplied), and the one that is never true: adding edges.

**Read.** "The prism shown is packed with small cubes... Each small cube has edges ½ unit long. Choose the 2 true statements." Gold: *2 true*. At a glance: "adding the three edge lengths gives the volume" is always false and dims first; a statement giving a cube's volume equal to its edge, or to the edge squared, is false; a statement whose "volume" is a large whole number for a small box is the count wearing the wrong name.

**The example.** The app's first variant: the figure is a packed prism, 5 by 3 by 4 half-cubes, so 2½ × 1½ × 2 units. Statements: "The prism is 2 units tall" | "The volume is 15⁄2 cubic units" | "It takes 120 cubes to fill the prism" | "Each small cube has a volume of ½ cubic unit" | "Adding the three edge lengths gives the volume." (1) The two never-true kinds dim together: adding edges; a ½-edge cube is ⅛, not ½ (the sample cube is shown). (2) Height: 4 cubes up, each ½: 4 × ½ = 2 units. True; the blue bracket relabels from "4 cubes" to "2 units." (3) Count: 5 × 3 × 4 = 60 cubes; the `fill` ticks to 60. "120" is false and dims. (4) Volume: 60 × ⅛ = 60⁄8 = 15⁄2; or 5⁄2 × 3⁄2 × 2 = 15⁄2. True. (5) The two true pills light.

A quick second case is not needed; every variant is these five kinds with the numbers changed, and the method is "decide each one with its line."

**Fast.** Dim the always-false kinds first (adding edges; a cube's volume equal to its edge or its edge squared). Then one line each for the rest: count along an edge, three counts multiplied, count times ⅛ (or the edges multiplied).

**Sense.** A box about 2½ by 1½ by 2 holds about 7 or 8 cubic units; 15⁄2 fits, 60 and 120 do not. The volume is less than the count.

**Check.** Exactly two chosen; each re-read as a sentence with its number recomputed.

**Rule out.** Adding edges; cube volume given as ½, ¼ (for a ½ edge) or ⅓, ⅑ (for a ⅓ edge), or 1⁄16 for a ¼ edge; a "volume" that equals the cube count; a count along the length that is really the count along another edge.

**Line.** "Count the cubes, then size them. Adding edges is never a volume."

**Trap.** The decoys are the traps, and they are exactly what children do: read "4 cubes tall" as "4 units tall"; give the count as the volume; square the edge instead of cubing it; believe a ½ cube holds ½. The lesson names each as it dims it, with the picture (the relabeled bracket, the sample cube) rather than a rule.

**Kit.** The packing tools from `unit-fraction-cube-packing`; `dim`; a bracket that relabels from a count to a length on the word.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: the statements are the misconceptions, and each must be shown false by the picture, not asserted.

#### `fraction-edge-volume` — Volume, or a missing edge, with fraction lengths.

**Standard.** 6.G.A.2, second sentence: "apply the formulas V = l w h and V = b h to find volumes of right rectangular prisms with fractional edge lengths in the context of solving real-world and mathematical problems." Two cases, forward and backward, as in grade 5.

**Foundations first** (foundations 4 and 5). One line borrowed: "you packed a box with small cubes and saw that multiplying the edges gives the same volume; now the box has no cubes drawn, only lengths, and the lengths are fractions." The one new habit is defined and shown: a *mixed number* is written as one fraction before anything is multiplied (4½ = 9⁄2: two halves in each whole, nine halves), because a box with edge 4½ is not a 4-box plus a ½-box that can be measured separately; it is one length. Then *cancelling* in passing: dividing a top and a bottom by the same number before multiplying, so the numbers stay small. A missing edge is still a missing factor: divide the volume by the product of the two known edges.

**Read.** "A planter box is 4½ cm long, 2¼ cm wide and 2⅔ cm tall. What is its volume?" Gold: *volume*. At a glance: three lengths, so multiply; the answer is near 4 × 2 × 3 = 24 and is in cubic centimeters. For the missing-edge wording ("has a volume of ... How wide is it?"), gold: *volume* given, *wide* asked; two lengths known, so divide.

**The example.** 4½ × 2¼ × 2⅔ (the app's 27). The box drawn to scale with the three mixed numbers on its brackets. (1) Each edge rewritten under its bracket: 9⁄2, 9⁄4, 8⁄3. (2) Board: V = 9⁄2 × 9⁄4 × 8⁄3. (3) Cancel: the 8 against the 2 and the 4 (8 ÷ 2 ÷ 4 = 1), a 9 against the 3 (9 ÷ 3 = 3); what is left is 9 × 3 = 27. The cancelled numbers are struck on the one line. (4) Answer 27 cm³. (5) Sense beat: the estimate 4 × 2 × 3 = 24 written beside it.

**Second example, because undoing is a different act.** "A block of wood has a volume of 23⅚ cm³. It is 3¼ cm long and 3⅔ cm tall. How wide is it?" (the app's 2). (1) The two known edges make the base: 13⁄4 × 11⁄3 = 143⁄12, the gold base layer. (2) The volume as one fraction: 23⅚ = 143⁄6. (3) Missing factor: 143⁄12 × ? = 143⁄6; ? = 143⁄6 ÷ 143⁄12 = 143⁄6 × 12⁄143 = 12⁄6 = 2. "The base fits into the volume twice: two layers." The blue bracket reads 2 cm. (4) Check line: 13⁄4 × 11⁄3 × 2 = 286⁄12 = 143⁄6.

**Fast.** Every length as one fraction; cancel; multiply. For a missing edge: multiply the two known, then divide the volume by that (multiply by the flipped fraction).

**Sense.** Whole-number estimate: about 4 × 2 × 3 = 24 for the first; for the second, 3 × 4 = 12 base, 24 ÷ 12 = 2 layers. The found edge drawn to its length makes the block look like a block.

**Check.** Multiply all three edges back to the volume; for the volume case, divide the answer by one edge and compare with the other two multiplied.

**Line.** "Make every length one fraction. A missing edge is still a missing factor."

**Trap.** Multiplying the whole parts and the fraction parts separately (4 × 2 × 2 = 16, plus ½ × ¼ × ⅔ = 1⁄12, so 16 1⁄12). The box drawn with its 4½ edge as one unbroken bracket, and the estimate 24 that 16 misses, end it; the lesson says once why: an edge of 4½ is nine halves, one length, not two boxes.

**Kit.** `solid` prism with mixed-number `labels` and the improper fraction written under each on the word; the board's `\cancel` on fraction factors in one line (exists); the base layer gold and the blue bracket that reads the found edge.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, from this concept: the arithmetic is the lesson and `--dry`'s math check verifies every line.
