# Volume and surface area, part 3: the Geometry types

Ten problem types across four skills. Before the concepts, one finding that spans five of them: `rg-solid-density`, `geo-volume-context`, `density-volume-steps`, `density-context` and `solid-volume-context` are five names for about seven distinct problems (volume forward; volume undone; volume to liters; volume times density to a mass; mass to a count of whole objects, rounded down; volume to a count of loads, rounded up; density as a ratio compared or ranked). Each concept below is written so its film stands alone, but the two trophy stories, the two clay stories and the two bucket stories should not each get their own lesson; the consolidation in the family design (section 6) stands, and the writer of any one of these should read the sibling concept first so the picture (the tagged unit cube, the ghosted last object, the wagon) is the same in all of them.

### `section-revolution-solid` — Name the slice, name the spun solid, find its volume (sections-and-revolutions, Geometry)

Three kinds under one name, as the family design says; the lesson treats them as three, and shows that two of them are the same picture run forward and backward.

**Standard.** HSG.GMD.B.4, both clauses: "Identify the shapes of two-dimensional cross-sections of three-dimensional objects, and identify three-dimensional objects generated by rotations of two-dimensional objects." The volume kind serves HSG.GMD.A.3 ("Use volume formulas for cylinders, pyramids, cones ... to solve problems"), reusing 8.G.C.9. Foundations 12, 7, 9.

**Foundations first.** Define *axis* (the dashed line the shape spins about; it does not move), *solid of revolution* (everything the shape passes through in one full turn). Build it live: a gold right triangle with legs 3 and 4, dashed axis along the 3 leg. `spin`: the 4 leg sweeps a disc, ghosts pile up, the solid fills teal: a cone. Then say what each part became, with the shape still visible inside as half the cone's cross-section: *the side on the axis is the height* (it never moved, so it is the solid's spine, 3); *the farthest point from the axis sets the radius* (the end of the 4 leg traced the base circle: r = 4); *the hypotenuse became the cone's side* (the slant, 5). Then the slice idea in one motion: `slice` through the axis and `extract`: the section is the triangle and its mirror image, an isosceles triangle of base 8 and height 3. Slice parallel to the base: a circle, the path one point traced. A tilted plane: an ellipse. "Slicing undoes the spin: a plane through the axis shows you the shape you spun, doubled."

**Read.** Which of the three is it: "shape of the cross-section" (a flat shape), "which solid does it sweep out" (a solid with r and h), or "what is the volume" (a number times π)? For a spin, the first thing to mark on the sketch is which side lies on the dashed line. "Contains the axis" means through the apex: the doubled shape. "Parallel to the bases" means the base shape.

**The example (spin kind, then volume).** "A right triangle has legs of 3 cm and 4 cm. It is rotated one full turn about the dashed line, which contains one leg." Picture as above, to scale, the dashed axis on the 3 leg. (1) The 3 leg is on the axis: height 3, blue. (2) The 4 leg sweeps: radius 4. (3) The hypotenuse is the side: slant 5, not a height. (4) A cone with radius 4 and height 3. Then the volume: V = ⅓ × π r² × h, written in the figure's colors; d is not involved; ⅓ × π × 16 × 3 = 16π cm³.

**Second example (the slice kind), brief.** "A plane contains the axis of a cone." The gold plane slides in through the apex and the center; `extract`: a triangle. Then the same plane parallel to the base: a circle. Two beats; the picture already exists. The cylinder kind (rectangle 6 by 8 about its 8 cm side: r = 6, h = 8, V = π × 36 × 8 = 288π) is one quick beat as a different solid: the axis side is still the height.

**Fast.** Axis side = height; farthest reach = radius; the other side is the slant, for surface, never for volume. Triangle spins to a cone (⅓πr²h), rectangle to a cylinder (πr²h), semicircle to a sphere. Slice through the axis: the shape doubled; parallel to the base: the base.

**Sense.** The cone's volume is less than its cylinder's, π × 16 × 3 = 48π; 16π is a third of it. A 3-high cone of radius 4 is squat; 16π ≈ 50 fits in a 8 × 8 × 3 box of 192, about a quarter: right.

**Check.** Re-read the figure: the leg on the dashed line is the one you called h. 16π × 3 = 48π = π × 4² × 3, the cylinder it sits in.

**Rule out.** For the solid: any cylinder is out (a triangle spins to a point at the apex); "height 5" uses the hypotenuse, which is a slant; swapped r and h put the wrong leg on the axis. For the volume: 48π has no third; 80⁄3π used 5 as the height; 12π swapped the legs. For the slice of a cone through its axis: circle and ellipse have no corners and the apex is a corner; a rectangle needs parallel sides and the cone's sides lean in.

**Line.** "The side on the axis is the height. Spin it, and slicing through the axis gives it back."

**Trap.** Putting the hypotenuse in for the height (the "height 5" and 80⁄3π decoys), and swapping r and h. The spin shows the hypotenuse becoming the slanted side while the axis leg stands still as the spine; after that no one calls the slant a height.

**Kit.** `spin` with ghosts and the kept half-section; `slice` with `through: axis`, `parallel: base`, and a tilted plane; `extract`. All on the family's build list (step 4 and 6).

**Writer.** Strongest writer: the proof is the motion (spin, then the slice that returns the shape), and a weak author will describe it instead of staging it.

### `regular-polygon-area` — Area from apothem and perimeter (geometric-modeling, Geometry)

Misfiled: there is no solid here, and the type is judged to serve 7.G.B.6. It belongs in family 24 (area), where the apothem fan is one more "cut it and move it" area formula beside the triangle and trapezoid. The concept is written so it can move with it.

**Standard.** 7.G.B.6: "Solve ... mathematical problems involving area ... of two- ... dimensional objects composed of triangles, quadrilaterals, polygons." The hexagon-apothem variant also uses 8.G.B.7 (a right triangle inside the hexagon). Stands on family 24 (triangle area ½bh), family 22 (regular: all sides and angles equal), family 27 (Pythagoras).

**Foundations first.** Define *regular polygon* (all sides equal, all angles equal; it has a center), *perimeter* (p = n × s, the walk around it), *apothem* (a: the distance from the center straight to the middle of a side; it meets the side at a right angle). Build the picture: a regular pentagon (side 4, to scale), the center marked, segments to the five corners: five identical triangles, each with the side as its base and the apothem as its height. Each triangle is ½ × s × a; there are n of them: ½ × a × (n × s) = ½ × a × p. Then prove it with `slide` and `turn`: the five triangles fan out into a row, every other one flipped, and fit into a parallelogram of base ½p and height a (the apothem fan). The formula is a rectangle: the apothem by half the perimeter.

**Read.** "Area" asks for ½ap; "apothem" or "one side" asks for the formula undone; "hexagon ... apothem" asks for the altitude of an equilateral triangle. At a glance: the perimeter is n × s and must be found first when a side is given; an answer for a side is far smaller than the perimeter; a square's apothem is half its side, so a square needs no formula.

**The example.** "A regular pentagon has side length 4 cm and an apothem of 2.75 cm ... find the area in square centimeters." Choices 55, 5.5, 27.5, 6.875. Picture: the pentagon with one triangle gold, its base 4 and height 2.75 labeled. (1) Five sides of 4: p = 20, written on the figure as the sides light in turn. (2) One triangle: ½ × 4 × 2.75 = 5.5 cm². (3) Five of them: 27.5 cm². (4) The formula says the same: ½ × 2.75 × 20 = 27.5. (5) The fan: the five triangles slide into a 10-by-2.75 rectangle: 27.5.

**Second example (undone), brief.** "A regular decagon has an apothem of 6.16 cm and an area of 123.2 square centimeters. What is one side?" ½ × 6.16 × p = 123.2, so p = 123.2 ÷ 3.08 = 40; ten sides: s = 4. Different because the formula runs backward and the perimeter must be shared into sides. The hexagon apothem is one beat: six equilateral triangles of side 14; half of one is a right triangle with hypotenuse 14 and leg 7: a = √(196 − 49) = √147 = 7√3.

**Fast.** Perimeter first (n × s). A = ½ap. Backward: p = 2A ÷ a, then s = p ÷ n. Hexagon: a = s√3 ⁄ 2, from the half-triangle, every time.

**Sense.** The polygon sits inside a circle of radius a bit more than the apothem and contains one of radius a; πa² is a floor: π × 2.75² ≈ 24 < 27.5, and not far above. A square with apothem 12 has side 24 and area 576; ½ × 12 × 96 = 576 agrees, which is the check that the formula is right.

**Check.** Multiply back: ½ × 6.16 × 40 = 123.2. For the hexagon: 7² + (7√3)² = 49 + 147 = 196 = 14².

**Rule out.** 55 forgot the half (it is the parallelogram made from two copies). 5.5 is one triangle. For the side: 40 is the whole perimeter; 2 and 8 are half and double of it with the decagon's ten sides miscounted. For the hexagon apothem: 14 equals the side, but the apothem is a leg and shorter than the hypotenuse; 7 is the half-side, a leg too, so the apothem is between 7 and 14; Pythagoras picks 7√3 over 7√2.

**Line.** "A regular polygon is a fan of triangles: half the apothem times the whole perimeter."

**Trap.** Dropping the ½ (55), and giving the perimeter as the side (40). The fan shows the half: the triangles make a parallelogram of base ½p, not p. The ten lit sides show 40 is a walk around, not one step.

**Kit.** Nothing beyond `poly`, `slide`, `turn`, `dim`; the family design already notes the fan needs no new component.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one formula forward and backward, one picture with named motions, and the math check catches slips.

### `curved-solid-surface-area` — Skin of a cone, and of solids joined at a circle (geometric-modeling, Geometry)

**Standard.** HSG.MG.A.1: "Use geometric shapes, their measures, and their properties to describe objects." The closed cone given by r and h practices 8.G.B.7: "Apply the Pythagorean Theorem to determine unknown side lengths in right triangles in real-world and mathematical problems in two and three dimensions." Surface area of cones and spheres is in no Common Core clause by name; HSG.MG.A.1 is the honest home. Foundations 6, 9 (the sphere's skin is its can's label), 13.

**Foundations first.** Say once: surface area is the *skin*, in square units, and a joined solid's skin is the list of its *outside* surfaces; where two solids are glued, the two circles that touch are inside and count for nothing. Then the four skins, each from a picture the learner has: a circle, πr². A cylinder's wall unrolls (`unroll`: the circle rolls one turn along the top edge) into a rectangle 2πr by h: 2πrh. A cone's side unrolls into a sector of radius ℓ (the *slant height*, the distance from the rim to the apex along the surface) whose arc is the rim, 2πr; like a triangle, it is half the arc times the radius: ½ × 2πr × ℓ = πrℓ. A hemisphere's dome is half the sphere's skin, which is the can's label: ½ × 4πr² = 2πr². Then the one right triangle every cone hides: r along the base, h up the axis (dashed), ℓ down the side; ℓ² = r² + h². "The slant is the hypotenuse; the height is a leg. Only the slant touches the surface."

**Read.** The question tells which surfaces are in and out: "closed", "including the base" puts the base disc in; "outside surfaces" or "counting the cone's side and the hemisphere's curved surface" leaves out every glued circle; "the bottom ... is a flat base" puts one disc in. Given r and ℓ: no Pythagoras. Given r and h: find ℓ first, and h is never used again. At a glance: the answer is a sum of two or three terms, each a whole number times π; it is at least as large as the largest single surface.

**The example.** "A silo is a cylinder of radius 8 cm and height 7 cm with a cone of slant height 17 cm and the same radius on top. The bottom of the cylinder is a flat base. What is the surface area of the silo, counting only the outside surfaces?" Choices 232π, 248π, 312π, 376π. Picture: the silo to scale (`model`), r = 8 on the base, h = 7 blue on the wall, ℓ = 17 on the cone's side; the seam where cone meets cylinder drawn as a dashed circle. (1) List the outside, lighting each gold: the flat base (a circle), the wall, the cone's side. The seam: the cylinder's top and the cone's base are the same circle, glued, inside; it lights rust and is crossed off the list. (2) Base: π × 8² = 64π. (3) Wall: 2π × 8 × 7 = 112π, with the unroll. (4) Cone side: π × 8 × 17 = 136π, with the sector. (5) Add: 312π cm².

**Second example (slant to find), brief.** "A closed cone has a base radius of 9 cm and a height of 12 cm. What is its total surface area, including the base?" The hidden triangle: ℓ = √(81 + 144) = 15. Side π × 9 × 15 = 135π; base 81π; total 216π. Different because ℓ is not given and the trap of using h is live. The ice-cream cone (cone side 156π plus dome 2π × 12² = 288π, the glued circle left out: 444π) is the silo's list with a dome; one quick beat if the dome has not yet been used.

**Fast.** List the outside surfaces, cross off every glued circle, one formula each, add. Find ℓ before anything else when only h is given.

**Sense.** Each term is a real patch of the picture; the sum must exceed the biggest one. For the silo, the cone's side (136π) is bigger than the wall (112π) because 17 > 2 × 7, and the picture shows the tall cone.

**Check.** Count the surfaces in the picture and the terms on the board: silo three and three; closed cone two and two. For a found ℓ: 9, 12, 15 is 3, 4, 5 times three.

**Rule out.** 376π = 312π + 64π counted the glued circle; 248π forgot the flat base. For the cone: 135π is the side alone ("including the base" was asked); 189π = 81π + 9 × 12π put h where ℓ belongs; 432π doubled. For the ice-cream cone: 588π added the glued circle; 732π used a whole sphere's skin for a dome.

**Line.** "List the skin. A circle where two solids touch is not skin."

**Trap.** Adding the glued circle (376π, 588π), and using the height as the slant (189π). The rust seam crossed off the list ends the first; the hidden right triangle with h as a leg and ℓ as the hypotenuse ends the second.

**Kit.** `model`; `unroll` for cylinder (rectangle) and cone (sector); a `seam` highlight on a composite of round solids (the shared circle, rust); the hidden right triangle inside a cone (`p.axis`, `p.radius`, `p.slant` as a triangle). On the build list except the seam, which is small.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, once the unroll and the seam exist: the concept supplies the list, the formulas and every decoy's factor.

### `geometric-model-choice` — The solid for the object, the measure for the task (geometric-modeling, Geometry)

A mixed type that teaches reading: three kinds (name the solid; name the measure; compute the measure), and no shared recipe. The film reads each story.

**Standard.** HSG.MG.A.1: "Use geometric shapes, their measures, and their properties to describe objects (e.g., modeling a tree trunk or a human torso as a cylinder)." The tent and lawn practice 7.G.B.6 ("area, volume and surface area of two- and three-dimensional objects composed of triangles, quadrilaterals, polygons, cubes, and right prisms"). Foundation 13 on 6, 8, 9.

**Foundations first.** Two readings, said plainly. *The shape of the object names the solid*: same round slice all the way along is a cylinder; same distance from the center to every point is a sphere; comes to a point is a cone or pyramid; flat sides and corners is a prism. *The verb of the task names the measure*: "fills" is the inside, volume, cubic units; "covers" a flat thing is area, square units; "goes around the edge" is perimeter, a length; "covers the skin" is surface area; "wraps the side only" is lateral surface, the label. Picture: `model`: a can fades to its cylinder; "fills" and the inside lights teal; "wraps" and the side unrolls into the label; "covers" and the whole skin lights. One object, three measures, three units. "Shape gives the solid. The verb gives the measure."

**Read.** Underline the shape words and the verb. The unit on the choices confirms the measure: cm² is skin or flat, cm³ is inside, "which solid" wants a name. "No part of the ends" means lateral. "Diameter" means halve first. "Lying on one rectangular face" means the triangle is the base and the length is the height of the prism.

**The example (compute).** "A can of beans is modeled as a cylinder with a radius of 4 cm and a height of 14 cm. Its paper label wraps once around the curved side with no overlap, and covers no part of the ends. What is the area of the label, in square centimeters? Give the area in terms of π." Choices 112π, 144π, 56π, 224π. Picture: the can to scale, r = 4 on the lid, h = 14 blue. (1) "Wraps ... the curved side ... no part of the ends": lateral; the ends dim. (2) `unroll`: the label is a rectangle 14 tall and one trip around, 2π × 4 = 8π, wide. (3) Area = 8π × 14 = 112π cm².

**One example per other kind, brief.** Name the solid: "A basketball is the same distance from its center to every point on its surface": that is the definition of a sphere; the ball fades to one. Name the measure: "A planter box ... how much soil fills it": fills, inside: volume (the box fills teal); and "a lawn ... cover it with sod": a flat rectangle covered: area, not perimeter (the edge lights rust and dims). Compute, two more shapes: the soccer ball, d = 12 so r = 6 first, leather covers the skin: 4π × 36 = 144π; the tent, triangle end ½ × 2 × 2 = 2 m² as the base, times the 3 m length: 6 m³, with the prism drawn lying down.

**Fast.** Shape → solid; verb → measure; unit → confirm; then the one formula. Label: 2πrh. Skin of a sphere: 4πr². Prism: base area times length, whatever face it lies on.

**Sense.** The label is a sheet 14 by about 25 (8π ≈ 25.1): about 350 cm², and 112π ≈ 352. Sod for a lawn is measured in square meters, not meters: the unit of the answer must match the verb.

**Check.** Re-read the verb against the lit part of the picture. 112π: the two ends, 2 × 16π = 32π, are not in it, as the problem said.

**Rule out.** For the label: 224π = π × 16 × 14 is the volume, cubic units in a square-unit question; 144π = 112π + 32π added the ends the label does not cover; 56π is half the wrap. For the leather: 288π is the volume (⁴⁄₃π × 216); 576π used the diameter as the radius; 36π is one great circle. For the tent: 12 forgot the half of the triangle. For the measure questions: a choice whose unit does not match the verb is out at once.

**Line.** "Shape gives the solid. The verb gives the measure."

**Trap.** Computing the volume when the task is about the skin (224π, 288π), and adding the ends to a label (144π). The unrolled label and the dimmed ends end both; saying the unit aloud ("square centimeters of leather") ends the first.

**Kit.** `model` with `p.inside`, `p.side`, `p.skin`; `unroll`; a lying `triangular-prism`. On the build list.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: mixed reading with no recipe..

### `rg-solid-density` — Mass from volume and density; whole objects from material; liters from a tank (solid-volume-models, Geometry)

Three kinds under one name; the tank variant has no density at all (it is unit renaming, HSN.Q.A.1) and belongs with its liters sibling in `solid-volume-context`. The design's consolidation under `density-modeling` stands; this concept covers the three kinds so the film can stand alone meanwhile.

**Standard.** HSG.MG.A.2: "Apply concepts of density based on area and volume in modeling situations." HSG.GMD.A.3: "Use volume formulas for cylinders, pyramids, cones, and spheres to solve problems." HSN.Q.A.1: "choose and interpret units consistently in formulas." 8.G.C.9 for the cylinder, 7.G.B.6 for the tank. Foundation 13 on 9, 4.

**Foundations first.** Define *density* as what one unit cube holds: `weigh`: a lone 1 cm cube tagged "1 cm³ weighs 1.8 g". A solid of V cubic centimeters is V such cubes, so its mass is V × 1.8. Say the unit as a fraction and read it as a rule: grams *per* cubic centimeter means "for every one cubic centimeter". Then the two things the unit forces: every length must be in the unit under the "per" before anything is cubed (a cubic foot is 12 × 12 × 12 cubic inches, not 12); and a kilogram is 1,000 grams, so 50 kg is 50,000 g before it meets grams per cubic centimeter. Define a *liter* with a picture: a 10 cm cube, 1,000 cm³.

**Read.** "How many whole ..." is a count: material ÷ one object, rounded *down*. "How much does it weigh" is mass: volume × density, rounded as asked. "How many liters" is volume ÷ 1,000. Gold on "below the top" (the water's height is 40 − 6 = 34), on "diameter", on "inches" beside "per cubic foot". At a glance: the answer to a count is a whole number smaller than material ÷ object; the mass of a log is hundreds of pounds, not thousands.

**The example (count).** "A sculptor has 50 kilograms of clay. The clay has a density of 1.8 grams per cubic centimeter. Each pyramid has a square base with side 10 centimeters and a height of 6 centimeters. How many whole pyramids can be made?" Picture: one pyramid to scale beside the tagged cube. (1) One pyramid's volume: ⅓ × 10 × 10 × 6 = 200 cm³. (2) Weigh it: 200 cubes of 1.8 g: 360 g. One pyramid is 360 g, written on its tag. (3) The clay in grams: 50 kg = 50,000 g. (4) Share the clay into pyramids: 50,000 ÷ 360 = 138.9. (5) `deal`: pyramids line up as the count ticks; the 139th appears muted, nine tenths of a pyramid, not a pyramid: 138.

**Two more kinds, brief.** Mass: "A log ... diameter of 20 inches and a height of 7 feet ... 51 pounds per cubic foot." Halve: r = 10 in; the unit says feet: 10⁄12 ft; V = π × (10⁄12)² × 7 = 15.27 ft³; × 51 = 778.9: 779 lb. Liters: the tank 60 by 20 by 40 filled to 34 (`liquid` to 34): 60 × 20 × 34 = 40,800 cm³; the liter cube is 10 × 10 × 10, so the water is 6 by 2 by 3.4 liter cubes: 40.8 L.

**Fast.** Weigh one object (volume × density), then share the material; or find the whole volume the material makes and divide by one object; both give the same count. Rename every length to the density's unit before cubing. Liters: cm³ ÷ 1,000.

**Sense.** A pyramid is a bit more than a third of a kilogram, so 50 kg makes a bit under 150: 138 is right. 50 ÷ 1.8 = 27.8 "cubic centimeters" would be a thimble of clay; the kilograms had to become grams. The log is about 15 cubic feet of wood; 779 pounds, about a third of a ton, is a log two people cannot lift: right.

**Check.** 138 × 360 = 49,680 g of clay, under 50,000; 139 × 360 = 50,040, over. 40.8 L × 1,000 = 40,800 = 60 × 20 × 34.

**Line.** "Density is what one cube holds. Whole objects round down."

**Trap.** Rounding 138.9 up to 139 (the ghosted last pyramid shows there is not enough clay for it); dividing kilograms by grams per cubic centimeter without renaming (the 27.8 "thimble"); cubing with the radius in inches against a density per cubic foot (the unit under the "per" is written first, in gold, and every length is renamed to it before cubing).

**Kit.** `weigh` (tagged unit cube), `deal` for a count of objects with a ghosted partial, `liquid`, a liter cube drawn inside the tank. `weigh` and `deal` are build step 6.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: a mixed reading type where the units decide the route, and the renaming trap is live in two of three kinds.

### `solid-volume-context` — Volume formulas forward, backward, in liters, and compared (solid-volume-models, Geometry)

**Standard.** HSG.GMD.A.3: "Use volume formulas for cylinders, pyramids, cones, and spheres to solve problems." The liters variants practice HSN.Q.A.1 ("choose and interpret units consistently in formulas"); the formulas are 8.G.C.9. Foundations 8, 9, 10, and 13's unit renaming.

**Foundations first.** The four formulas as the pictures the learner already has, said in ten seconds each with the figure: a cylinder is a stack of discs, πr² × h; a pyramid or cone is a third of the box or can it fits in, ⅓Bh (the `pour`, three pours fill the can); a sphere is two thirds of its can, ⁴⁄₃πr³. Then two readings: *diameter* is across the middle, halve it first on its own line; *in terms of π* means the answer keeps π as a letter; *use 3.14* means substitute on the last line. And the liter, drawn: a 10 cm cube, 1,000 cm³.

**Read.** Forward ("how many cubic meters"), backward ("how tall", with a volume given), to liters ("how many liters ... one liter is 1000 cm³"), or compared ("how many full cups fill the jar"). At a glance: a sphere's volume is about half the cube on its diameter; a cone is a quarter of the box on its diameter and height; a found height, drawn, must make the picture match.

**The example (forward, diameter, sense check).** "A spherical water tank has a diameter of 18 m. How many cubic meters of water does it hold when full? Give the volume in terms of π." Choices 2916π, 486π, 7776π, 972π. Picture: the sphere to scale in its can (radius 9, height 18), d = 18 across the middle. (1) Halve first: d = 18, so r = 9, its own line. (2) V = ⁴⁄₃ × π × r³; cube: 9³ = 729. (3) ⁴⁄₃ × 729 = 972; V = 972π m³. (4) The can: π × 81 × 18 = 1,458π; two thirds of it is 972π: the picture agrees.

**Second example (backward), brief.** "A conical pile of gravel has a base radius of 4 m and a volume of 80π cubic meters. How tall is the pile?" ⅓ × π × 16 × h = 80π; the π goes; 16h ⁄ 3 = 80; h = 15. Drawn to 15 on the radius-4 base, the pile is a steep cone, taller than it is wide; the problem said "pile", and 15 looks like one. Different because the formula is undone. Two quick beats for the other kinds: the paint can, 3.14 × 36 × 20 = 2,260.8 cm³, which is 2.26 liter cubes, 2.3 L to the nearest tenth; the cups: same height, the jar's radius is double so its disc is four times, and a cylinder is three cones: 4 × 3 = 12 cups, no arithmetic.

**Fast.** Halve d. Write the formula with the letters, the numbers under them, π kept to the end. Backward: divide the volume by everything that is known. Liters: ÷ 1,000. Compared with the same height: the radius ratio squared, times 3 if a cone is poured into a cylinder.

**Sense.** The box test: 18³ = 5,832, about half is 2,900, and 972π ≈ 3,054; 7,776π ≈ 24,400 is four times the box and impossible. The cone's height 15 drawn to scale against the 4 m base looks like a pile. 2.3 liters is a paint can; 22.6 liters is a bucket.

**Check.** ⅓ × π × 16 × 15 = 80π. 972π × 3 ⁄ 2 = 1,458π = π × 9² × 18, the can. 12 cups × 48π = 576π = π × 64 × 9.

**Rule out.** 7776π used the diameter as the radius (×8 on a sphere); 2916π forgot the ⁴⁄₃'s third... exactly: it is 4 × 729 with no ÷3; 486π is the hemisphere. For the cone: 60 and 20 come from dividing by 4 instead of 16, or forgetting the third. For the cups: 4 is the radius ratio squared without the pour; 8 is neither.

**Line.** "Diameter? Halve it first. Sphere: about half the box on its diameter."

**Trap.** Using the diameter as the radius (the ×8 decoy), and dropping the third of the cone or sphere. The halving line written first, and the sphere drawn two thirds full inside its can, end them.

**Kit.** `sphere` in its can, `cone` with `liquid`, `pour`, the liter cube, `dim`. Build step 5.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: four kinds under one name; the cups kind is reasoning, not a formula, and the sense checks carry the lesson.

### `geo-volume-context` — Volume to a decimal, then a mass or a count of loads (solid-volume-models, Geometry)

Overlaps `rg-solid-density` (mass from density) and `density-volume-steps` (the same trophy, scaffolded). The one thing this type alone practices is rounding *up* for loads; the consolidation keeps that kind. Note for the app: the bucket's answer, 35,191, needs the π key; 3.14 gives 35,173, so the problem must say which, as the paint-can variant elsewhere does.

**Standard.** HSG.GMD.A.3 ("Use volume formulas for cylinders, pyramids, cones ... to solve problems"), HSG.MG.A.2 ("Apply concepts of density based on ... volume in modeling situations"), 8.G.C.9. Foundation 13 on 9, 4, 6.

**Foundations first.** Say what joins and what rounds: the volumes of two solids glued together add (foundation 4, family 6's part-whole, drawn as the `composite` of a prism and a pyramid on one base); density is what one cube holds (`weigh`); a wagon is a bin of 18 cubic feet, and the last bin goes even if it is not full. Then: π stays a letter until the last line, and rounding happens once, at the end, to what the question names ("nearest gram", "fewest trips").

**Read.** "Mass" or "weight" with a density: volume × density, nearest unit. "Fewest trips" with a load: volume ÷ load, *up*. "Prism with a pyramid on top": two volumes, add. "Inside diameter": halve. At a glance: a trip count is a whole number just above volume ÷ load; a trophy of 8 cm base is a few hundred cubic centimeters, so a few kilograms of metal.

**The example (joined solids, then density).** "A solid trophy is a square prism with a square pyramid on top. The base of the prism is 8 cm by 8 cm and the prism is 4 cm tall. The pyramid has the same base and is 9 cm tall. The trophy is made of metal with a density of 7.1 grams per cubic centimeter. Find the mass of the trophy to the nearest gram." Picture: the trophy to scale (prism 8 × 8 × 4, pyramid of height 9 on it), the shared base a dashed square, the tagged cube "1 cm³ weighs 7.1 g" beside it. (1) Prism: B = 64, h = 4: 256 cm³. (2) Pyramid: ⅓ × 64 × 9 = 192 cm³ (a third of the 64 × 9 box it fits in, ghosted). (3) Together: 256 + 192 = 448 cm³. (4) 448 cubes of 7.1 g: 448 × 7.1 = 3,180.8; nearest gram: 3,181 g.

**Second example (loads, round up), brief.** "A pile of sand is a cone with a radius of 11 feet and a height of 7 feet. A wagon carries 18 cubic feet of sand per trip. What is the fewest number of trips?" V = ⅓ × π × 121 × 7 = 847π ⁄ 3 ≈ 887.1 ft³. 887.1 ÷ 18 = 49.3. `deal` into wagons: 49 full wagons, and sand left on the ground: a 50th trip. 50. Different because the rounding goes the other way and the picture must show why. The bucket (d = 6.5, r = 3.25; π × 3.25² × 10.5 ≈ 348.4 ft³; × 101 ≈ 35,191 lb) is the trophy's last step with a halving; one beat.

**Fast.** Each solid's formula, add, then × density or ÷ load. Keep π and every decimal in the calculator; round once at the end: mass to the nearest, trips up, whole objects down.

**Sense.** 448 cm³ of metal at about 7 g each is about 3,100 g, three kilograms: a trophy you lift with one hand. 887 cubic feet of sand is a pile wider than a room; 50 wagon loads of 18 is right, and 49 would leave 5 cubic feet behind.

**Check.** 49 × 18 = 882 < 887.1 < 900 = 50 × 18. 3,181 ÷ 7.1 ≈ 448.

**Line.** "Whole objects round down; trips round up. Round once, at the end."

**Trap.** Rounding 49.3 trips down to 49 (the sand left on the ground), and rounding π or a volume early (3.14 gives a different pound count). The dealt wagons and the "π stays a letter" line end them.

**Kit.** `composite` of prism and pyramid with the shared base; `weigh`; `deal` into loads with a partial last load. Build steps 5 and 6.

**Writer.** Strongest writer for the first film because the rounding direction is read from the story, not from a rule; after `rg-solid-density` exists, a cheaper model could write this one from both concepts.

### `density-volume-steps` — The volume-to-mass chain, one step at a time (solid-volume-models, Geometry)

The scaffolded form of the trophy, clay and bucket stories above; the lesson's job is to make the chain's order come from the units, so the learner can fill any blank the app leaves open. The same film serves `geo-volume-context` once the two are consolidated.

**Standard.** HSG.GMD.A.3, HSG.MG.A.2, and HSN.Q.A.1: "Use units as a way to understand problems and to guide the solution of multi-step problems." Foundation 13.

**Foundations first.** The chain as a picture: each step is a box on the board joined by an arrow carrying its unit. Length (cm) → volume (cm³) → mass (g) via the tagged cube; or, for a count, one object's volume (cm³) → one object's mass (g) → the material in grams (g) → how many (÷, down). Say what each arrow is: "×, because one cube holds 4.4 grams"; "÷, because each pyramid takes 226.8 grams"; "× 1,000, because a kilogram is a thousand grams". The learner who reads the units on the two ends of an arrow knows the operation between them. For a cylinder the chain starts one step earlier: diameter → radius.

**Read.** Which chain: mass (the last box is grams or pounds) or count (the last box is a whole number). Which boxes are filled, and what unit each must hold. At a glance: a volume box for a pyramid is a third of the box for a prism of the same base and height; a mass box is bigger than its volume box when the density is more than one; a count box is smaller than material ÷ one object.

**The example (mass chain, two solids).** "A solid trophy is a square prism with a square pyramid on top ... square with side 5 centimeters. The prism is 5 centimeters tall and the pyramid is 12 centimeters tall. The metal has a density of 8.8 grams per cubic centimeter. Find the mass of the trophy to the nearest gram. Enter each step." Picture: the trophy to scale beside the empty chain of four boxes labeled with their units. (1) Prism: 5 × 5 × 5 = 125 cm³, into the first box. (2) Pyramid: ⅓ × 25 × 12 = 100 cm³, the second. (3) Add: 225 cm³. (4) The arrow to grams is × 8.8, read from the tagged cube: 225 × 8.8 = 1,980 g. Each box fills on the word (`slot`).

**Second example (count chain), brief.** "A sculptor has 30 kilograms of clay with a density of 2.4 grams per cubic centimeter. Each pyramid has a square base with side 8 centimeters and a height of 12 centimeters. How many whole pyramids?" One pyramid: ⅓ × 64 × 12 = 256 cm³; weigh it: × 2.4 = 614.4 g; the clay: 30 × 1,000 = 30,000 g; share: 30,000 ÷ 614.4 = 48.8, down: 48. Different chain, different last arrow (÷ and down). The bucket chain (d = 7 → r = 3.5 → π × 3.5² × 6 = 230.907 ft³ → × 98 = 22,629 lb) is the mass chain with a halving at the front; one beat, and the note that the volume box is written to three places but the full value stays in the calculator.

**Fast.** Read the units on the boxes; the unit change names the operation: cm to cm³ is a formula, cm³ to g is × density, g to objects is ÷ one object's mass, kg to g is × 1,000. Fill left to right; a filled box is a check on the step before it.

**Sense.** The pyramid box is less than the prism box when the heights are near (a third); the mass box dwarfs the volume box for metal; the count is under 50 because each pyramid is more than half a kilogram and there are 30 kilograms.

**Check.** A given filled box must come out of the step before it: if the app shows 100 for the pyramid, ⅓ × 25 × 12 must give 100. 48 × 614.4 = 29,491 < 30,000 < 49 × 614.4.

**Line.** "The units on the two ends of an arrow tell you the operation between them."

**Trap.** Dividing where the units say multiply, or forgetting kg → g (30 ÷ 614.4 is a fraction of a pyramid, which is nonsense). The unit written on every arrow ends it.

**Kit.** `slots` (the place-value spec: boxes with units that fill on the word); the tagged cube; the composite trophy. The lesson needs the board chain to show units on arrows: a small addition to `slots`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: a chain with named boxes and units, and the math check verifies every box.

### `density-context` — Density is what one unit of space holds (density-modeling, Geometry)

**Standard.** HSG.MG.A.2: "Apply concepts of density based on area and volume in modeling situations (e.g., persons per square mile, BTUs per cubic foot)." The rod practices HSG.GMD.A.3. Foundation 13 on family 10 (a rate is "for every one") and 7 (equal shares).

**Foundations first.** Define *density* with two pictures, one for area and one for volume. Area: a rectangular reserve 6 km by 5 km, gridded into 30 unit squares of one square kilometer each (`grid`); 1,980 people `deal` equally into the squares, one square counting up as the others fill: 66 in each. Density is what one square holds: 66 people per square kilometer. Volume: the tagged cube, "1 cm³ of iron weighs 7.9 g": density is what one cube holds. Then the three sentences from one picture: density = amount ÷ space (what one unit holds); amount = density × space (every unit holds that much); space = amount ÷ density (how many units it takes to hold the amount). The unit says which: g/cm³ is grams for every one cubic centimeter. "Denser" means one unit of it holds more; it has nothing to do with being bigger or heavier in total.

**Read.** Which of the three is asked: the density (people per km², g/cm³), the amount (mass, in grams or in terms of π), or the space (cm³)? "Which is denser" asks for each block's density, never its mass. At a glance: a density of people is a smaller number than the population; a volume from a mass is smaller than the mass when the density is over 1; a block that is heavier *and* smaller is denser without dividing.

**The example (density from area).** "A rectangular nature reserve is 6 km long and 5 km wide, and 1980 people live inside it. What is its population density, in people per square kilometer?" Choices 330, 90, 66, 180. Picture: the 6 × 5 grid to scale, each cell 1 km². (1) The space: 6 × 5 = 30 square kilometers; the cells light as they are counted. (2) Deal 1,980 people into 30 cells: 1,980 ÷ 30 = 66; one cell's count ticks to 66, then every cell reads 66. (3) 66 people per square kilometer: what one square holds.

**Second example (compare), brief.** "Block B has a mass of 40 g and a volume of 10 cm³. Block A has a mass of 24 g and a volume of 4 cm³. Which block is denser?" Picture: B as ten cubes sharing 40 g, 4 g each; A as four cubes sharing 24 g, 6 g each. A's cube is heavier: A is denser, 6 g/cm³, though B is the heavier block. Different because the question is a comparison and the trap is the total mass. Two quick beats: "474 g of iron at 7.9 g per cubic centimeter": how many 7.9-g cubes make 474 g? 474 ÷ 7.9 = 60 cm³; and the steel rod: V = π × 9 × 25 = 225π cm³, each cubic centimeter 8 g: 1,800π g.

**Fast.** Write the unit as a fraction and read it: "per one". Density: divide the amount by the space. Amount: multiply. Space: divide the amount by the density. To compare, divide each; or notice when one has more amount in less space.

**Sense.** A density is per one square or one cube, so it is small next to the population; 330 and 180 would put a whole town in every square kilometer of a reserve. 60 cm³ of iron is a small block that weighs about a pound: right. 24 g in 4 cm³ beats 40 g in 10 cm³ because 24⁄4 is six and 40⁄10 is four.

**Check.** 66 × 30 = 1,980. 60 × 7.9 = 474. 6 × 4 = 24 and 4 × 10 = 40.

**Rule out.** 90 = 1,980 ÷ 22 divided by the perimeter and 180 by half of it: people are spread over area, not along the fence. 3,744.6 multiplied a mass by a density and got something bigger than the sample in grams, with the wrong unit. 1⁄60 and 1⁄124 are the fraction upside down. "Block B, 40 g/cm³" and "Block A, 24 g/cm³" are masses, not densities; "Block B, 4 g/cm³" is a true density of the less dense block.

**Line.** "Density is what one square, or one cube, holds."

**Trap.** Calling the heavier block denser (B weighs more), and dividing by the perimeter (6 + 5 + 6 + 5 = 22). The deal into cubes shows A's cube heavier; the grid of 30 squares shows people live on the area, not the fence.

**Kit.** `deal` into a `grid` (a cell counting up, then all cells), `weigh`, blocks drawn as stacks of tagged cubes. Build step 6.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: three directions of one relation plus a comparison, with the decoys being exactly the misconceptions; the deal picture must land before any formula.

### `geo-density-rank` — Rank regions by population density (density-modeling, Geometry)

**Standard.** HSG.MG.A.2: "Apply concepts of density based on area and volume in modeling situations (e.g., persons per square mile)." Foundation 13 on family 10.

**Foundations first.** Density as what one square mile holds (one beat of the `deal` picture from `density-context`, or the line "you already know: people divided by square miles"). Then the one idea of this type: the question is about the *density column*, which is not on the table until you write it. Picture: the `table` of counties with population and area, and a third column, "people per sq mi", filled cell by cell. "Fewer people on more land is less dense."

**Read.** The direction, gold: "least to greatest" or "greatest to least". At a glance: a county with more people *and* less land than another is denser than it, no division needed; only when one has more people and more land must both be divided. The population column alone and the area column alone are both wrong orders by design, and one of the decoys is each.

**The example.** The facts give the table only as "the table shows"; the film uses its own: Alder 36,000 people on 900 sq mi; Cedar 20,000 on 1,000; Dunes 30,000 on 500; Grove 25,000 on 125. "Which list ranks the counties from least to greatest population density?" Choices: Alder, Cedar, Dunes, Grove | Cedar, Alder, Dunes, Grove | Grove, Dunes, Alder, Cedar | Cedar, Dunes, Alder, Grove. (1) The glance: Cedar has the fewest people on the most land: least dense, no division. Grove has far fewer square miles than any other with a middling population: likely the densest. (2) Fill the column: Alder 36,000 ÷ 900 = 40; Cedar 20; Dunes 60; Grove 200, each cell lighting as it fills. (3) Sort by that column, least first: Cedar 20, Alder 40, Dunes 60, Grove 200. (4) The density values drop onto a number line from 0 to 200 so the order is seen, not just read.

**Fast.** Write the density column; sort it in the direction asked. Skip the division only when one county wins on both counts.

**Sense.** Grove, a small county with a lot of people, is densest: a town; Cedar, a big county with few, is emptiest: farmland. Any list that puts Grove anywhere but the dense end is wrong.

**Check.** Read the chosen list against the density column, left to right, each number larger than the last (or smaller). 40 × 900 = 36,000.

**Rule out.** "Grove, Dunes, Alder, Cedar" is the area column smallest first; "Alder, Cedar, Dunes, Grove" is alphabetical; a list ordered by population alone (here Cedar, Grove, Dunes, Alder) is never the answer either. Any list that puts Cedar (fewest people, most land) anywhere but the least-dense end is out at once.

**Line.** "Rank the column you wrote, not the columns you were given."

**Trap.** Ranking by population alone, or by area alone. The filled density column beside the two given columns, with its order visibly different from both, ends it.

**Kit.** `table` with a column filled cell by cell (exists) and dropping values onto a `numberline` (place-value spec). `dim`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one table, one column, one sort, with the decoys named here.
