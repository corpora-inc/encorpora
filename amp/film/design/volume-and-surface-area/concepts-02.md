### Grade 6 (filed): `cylinder-prism-surface-area`

**Misfiled.** Three of the four variants are cylinders with π, which 6.G.A.4 ("nets made up of rectangles and triangles") does not cover and 7.G.B.6 ("cubes and right prisms") does not either; cylinder surface area is grade 8 (or Geometry, beside `curved-solid-surface-area`) in every state sequence that names it. The triangular prism variant is the one that belongs at grade 6. Recommendation: split; the cylinder kinds move up, the prism stays. The concept below serves the type as it stands, because one picture carries all four kinds.

**Standard.** 7.G.B.6, "surface area of ... right prisms", for the triangular prism; the cylinder kinds truly serve the lateral/total surface clause of grade 8 state standards and HSG.MG.A.1.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 6: surface area is the skin, in square units. The one new idea, built live: the side of any straight-up solid unrolls into **one rectangle whose width is the distance around the base and whose height is the solid's height**. First on a triangular prism with a 5-12-13 base: the three side rectangles hinge flat and sit side by side, 5 + 12 + 13 = 30 along, 4 tall: one rectangle, 30 × 4. Then on a cylinder: the top circle (r = 4) rolls one full turn along a line, ticking 2πr = 8π as it goes, and the side unrolls into a rectangle 8π wide by h tall. Say once: the distance around is the *circumference*, 2πr; the disc's area is πr². Two different things; the label uses the first, the lids use the second. Then: *lateral* = the side only; *total* = side plus both bases.

**Read.** "Lateral" or "curved side only" (gold) means no discs; "total" or "including both bases" means add two discs. "aπ" means π stays a letter. A given total with a missing height is the formula run backward. At a glance: a radius is given (no halving); lateral is 2rh times π; total adds 2r² times π.

**Example A (total, forward).** "A cylinder has a radius of 4 cm and a height of 3 cm. Its total surface area, including both circular bases, is aπ square centimeters. What is a?" Picture: the cylinder to scale, r = 4 on the top face, h = 3 in blue. (1) The side unrolls: width 2π·4 = 8π, height 3: area 8π × 3 = 24π. (2) "Including both bases" lights the two discs; each πr² = 16π; both, 32π. (3) 24π + 32π = 56π. (4) a = 56. Lateral alone would have stopped at 24π; one beat says so.

**Example B (missing height, backward).** "A cylinder has a radius of 2 cm and a total surface area of 32π square centimeters. What is its height?" A different act: undo. (1) Two discs: 2 × π·2² = 8π; the discs light and 8π leaves the total: 32π − 8π = 24π is the side. (2) The side is 2π·2 × h = 4π × h, so h = 24π ÷ 4π = 6. The rectangle is drawn 4π wide and grows to 6 tall. Check: 8π + 4π·6 = 32π.

**Quick case (the prism).** "Right triangular prism, right-triangle bases with legs 5 and 12 and hypotenuse 13, length 4." Two triangles: 2 × ½ × 5 × 12 = 60. Side: the unrolled rectangle, (5 + 12 + 13) × 4 = 120. Total 180. Three beats; the unroll was shown in the foundation.

**Fast.** Side = (distance around the base) × height: 2πr·h for a cylinder, perimeter × length for a prism. Total adds the two bases. Missing height: total minus two bases, divided by 2πr.

**Sense.** The side of a cylinder is bigger than one lid whenever h is bigger than r/2; here 24π against 16π. A missing height, drawn, must make the cylinder look like a cylinder, not a coin or a straw.

**Check.** Add the parts back: 24 + 32 = 56. For the height: rebuild the total with h = 6.

**Line.** "Unroll the side: around the base, times the height."

**Trap.** Swapping circumference and area: writing πr² for the width of the label or 2πr for a lid. Realistic and constant. The rolling circle that ticks 2πr and the disc shaded πr² keep the two apart; the lesson never says "pi r" without saying what it measures. Also: adding one disc to a total. "Both" goes gold and two discs light.

**Kit.** `unfold` for the prism's three side faces hinging into one row; `unroll` with the circle rolling one turn and the running 2πr label. Both in the family's step 3. Nothing beyond.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model, once `unroll` exists: one picture, one formula forward and backward, the math check catches slips.

### Grade 6: `net-surface-area`

**Standard.** 6.G.A.4: "Represent three-dimensional figures using nets made up of rectangles and triangles, and use the nets to find the surface area of these figures." The second clause is practiced; the first (choose or draw the net) is not, as the family design notes.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 6, built with the fold. A box 7 × 8 × 9 stands in the one view with its three edges labeled. Define *face*, *edge*, *net*: the box is cut along edges and the faces hinge down flat, one by one, into the cross the problem shows; the camera tilts to look straight down. Define *surface area*: the areas of all the faces, added, in **square** units, because faces are flat. Then **fold** it back up: every face lands, none missing, none doubled. Say what the net is for: a flat drawing where every face can be measured, since on the solid you cannot see the back or the bottom. Twin faces light together: top and bottom are both 7 × 8; front and back 7 × 9; left and right 8 × 9. Every face of a box has a twin. The net is read by shared edges: two faces that touch in the net share that edge's length, which is how each face's two lengths are found when only some are written.

**Read.** "Surface area" (gold) and "square" tell the measure: skin, not inside. "Folds into a rectangular prism" means six faces in three pairs; "pyramid with a square base" means one square and four matching triangles. At a glance: count the faces in the net first (six, or five); the answer is the sum of that many areas.

**Example A (prism).** "The net folds into a rectangular prism. Lengths are in inches. What is the surface area, in square inches?" The app's net for the 7 × 8 × 9 box, to scale. (1) Count: six faces. (2) Fold once, so the learner sees which faces are twins; unfold. (3) Each face gets its two lengths from shared edges, then its area written inside: 7 × 8 = 56, 7 × 9 = 63, 8 × 9 = 72, each twice. (4) Board: 2(56 + 63 + 72) = 2 × 191 = 382 square inches. The unit is said as "square" with a reason: faces are flat.

**Example B (square pyramid).** A different case: triangles, and a length that is not an edge of the base. "The net folds into a pyramid with a square base. Lengths in centimeters." Base 10 × 10; each triangle has base 10 and height 8, the height drawn on the triangle's face in the net. (1) Five faces: one square, four triangles that fold up to meet at a point. (2) Square: 100. (3) One triangle: ½ × 10 × 8 = 40; four of them, 160. (4) 100 + 160 = 260 square centimeters. One beat on the fold: the 8 is the triangle's own height, measured along the face. It is called the *slant height*. The pyramid's upright height (dashed inside, shorter) is never in a net and never in this sum.

**Fast.** Box: three different faces, each twice: 2(lw + lh + wh). Square pyramid: s² + 4 × ½ × s × slant = s² + 2 × s × slant.

**Sense.** A box's skin is more than four of its biggest face and less than six: 382 sits between 288 and 432. A pyramid's four triangles together are less than four copies of the base square (160 < 400).

**Check.** Count the faces in the net and the areas on the board: six and six, five and five. Add again from the fold: each pair as you see it land.

**Line.** "Every face of a box has a twin. Square units for the skin."

**Trap.** Reading a side face's lengths wrong in the cross: a face that touches the bottom along an 8 cm edge is 8 wide, not 7. The lesson finds every length by the shared edge, lit on both faces at once. And five faces for a box, when the sixth is hidden on the solid: the fold shows the bottom land. Both are what children do.

**Kit.** The problem's own net figure through `scene`, to scale, with `fold` to the solid and back, twin faces as a highlight pair, and a per-face area label that appears on the word. Family step 3.

**Writer.** Strongest writer, as the family design says: the fold is the proof, and the shared-edge reading of the net must be staged on the figure, not told.

### Grade 7: `cross-section-shape`

**Standard.** 7.G.A.3: "Describe the two-dimensional figures that result from slicing three-dimensional figures, as in plane sections of right rectangular prisms and right rectangular pyramids." Both solids are generated; good.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 7, with the plane. Define *cross-section*: the flat shape where a plane cuts through a solid, drawn gold where the translucent plane passes. Build it on a 7 × 3 × 5 box. Plane parallel to the base: it glides up and down and the gold shape never changes, a 7 × 3 rectangle, the base's copy; that sameness is what makes a prism a prism. Plane perpendicular to the base, running along the 7 cm edges: a rectangle 7 by 5; the slice stands up, as tall as the box. Then the one idea that decides every question: **the plane makes one straight side for each face it crosses.** Four faces crossed, four sides; three faces, three sides. Lift each section out (extract) and lay it flat so it is a plane shape the learner already names. For "most specific name": square beats rectangle only when all four sides match.

**Read.** Which solid (gold: prism or pyramid), and how the plane sits: "parallel to its base" copies the base; "perpendicular to its base and parallel to the ___ edges" stands up and runs along those edges; "through the three vertices next to one corner" crosses three faces; "not through the apex" matters. At a glance: count the faces the plane crosses; that is the number of sides, and Triangle is out whenever it is four.

**The example (pyramid, perpendicular, not through the apex).** "A right rectangular pyramid is 4 mm tall, with a base 11 mm long and 10 mm wide. A plane slices it perpendicular to its base and parallel to the 11 mm edges, but not through the apex." Choices: Rectangle | Square | Triangle | Trapezoid. Picture: the pyramid to scale (squat: 4 tall on an 11 × 10 base), apex above the center, height dashed. (1) The plane stands up and runs along the 11 mm direction, off center. (2) It crosses four faces: the base (bottom side, 11 mm, flat), two slanted faces on the long sides (two sides going up), and... no: it crosses the base, the front slanted face, and the back slanted face, and the top of the section is where the plane meets the two end faces? Say it on the picture, which is exact: the section's bottom lies on the base; its two upright sides lie on the two end faces (the 10 mm sides), which lean inward, so those sides tilt toward each other; its top lies along the long slanted face the plane passes under, parallel to the base. Four sides, two of them parallel and unequal: a **trapezoid**. (3) Extract: lay it flat; the two parallel sides are visibly 11 and shorter. (4) Then glide the plane to the apex: the top side shrinks to a point and the shape is a triangle. That is the "not through the apex" clause doing its work. Answer: Trapezoid.

**Quick cases (three beats each).** Box, parallel to the base: a copy of the base, 7 × 3, Rectangle. Box, perpendicular along the 13 m edges, 6 m tall: 13 by 6, Rectangle (Square only if the two match; they do not). Box, through the three vertices next to a corner: three faces crossed, three sides, Triangle, the corner falling away as the plane passes. Pyramid 5 × 5 base, parallel: a smaller copy of the base, Square.

**Fast.** Parallel to the base: the base's shape (smaller for a pyramid). Perpendicular on a box: a rectangle, height by the edge it runs along. Count the faces crossed for anything else.

**Sense.** The gold shape on the picture is the answer; the extracted section should look like the named shape, to scale.

**Check.** Count the section's sides and parallel pairs on the extracted shape, and compare the side lengths before saying "square".

**Rule out.** Four faces crossed: Triangle dims. Sides unequal: Square dims. Only a pyramid off the apex can make a trapezoid, so for any box slice Trapezoid dims.

**Line.** "One straight side for every face the plane crosses."

**Trap.** "A slice of a box is always a rectangle," so the corner cut gets Rectangle; and "a pyramid's faces are triangles," so its slices get Triangle. The glide through the apex (triangle) and away from it (trapezoid), and the corner falling off as three sides appear, make both impossible.

**Kit.** `slice` with `parallel`, `perpendicular ... along` and `through` planes on `prism` and `pyramid`; `glide` of the plane; `extract` to lay the section flat. Family step 4. The pyramid must be drawn to its given height so a 4 mm pyramid on an 11 × 10 base looks squat.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: the reasoning is in what the moving plane shows.

### Grade 7: `cross-section-area`

**Standard.** 7.G.A.3 with 7.G.B.6 ("area ... of two- and three-dimensional objects"). The 4.MD.A.3 link is noise; drop it.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 7 in one short build, borrowing from `cross-section-shape`: the plane through a box, parallel (a copy of the base) and perpendicular (a standing rectangle). The one new sentence: a cross-section is flat, so its area is in **square** units and uses **two** of the three lengths; the third length is the one the plane cuts across, and it does not appear. Which two is what the lesson teaches.

**Read.** "Area of the cross-section" (gold), "square". Parallel to the base: length × width. Perpendicular and "parallel to the 7 m edges": the section is 7 m long and as tall as the solid; the width is the length cut across and is not used. At a glance: pick the two lengths, multiply.

**The example (perpendicular).** "A right rectangular prism is 7 m long, 9 m wide and 6 m tall. A plane slices it perpendicular to its base and parallel to the 7 m edges. What is the area of the cross-section, in square meters?" Picture: the 7 × 9 × 6 box, three edges labeled, the plane standing up and running along the 7 m direction. (1) The section fills gold; it runs the whole length (7) and the whole height (6); the 9 m width is what the plane cuts through, and the 9 goes muted. (2) Extract: a rectangle 7 by 6, labeled. (3) 7 × 6 = 42 square meters. One beat: the plane could sit anywhere across the 9 m and the section would be the same 7 by 6, so the 9 cannot matter.

**Example B (pyramid, halfway up).** A genuinely different case: a smaller copy, and the area shrinks faster than the lengths. "A right rectangular pyramid is 11 cm tall, base 14 cm by 10 cm. A plane slices it parallel to its base, halfway between the base and the apex." (1) The plane glides up to the halfway mark on the blue height; the section is a rectangle, a shrunken copy of the base. (2) Extract and lay it beside the base: at half the height, each edge is half: 7 by 5. (3) 7 × 5 = 35 square centimeters. (4) Beside the base's 140: the slice is a quarter of the base, because both lengths halved. Halving the base area (70) is wrong, and the two rectangles side by side show why.

**Fast.** Parallel: the base's two lengths (halved for a pyramid cut halfway). Perpendicular: height × the edge it runs along.

**Sense.** A slice is never bigger than the face it runs parallel to; 42 against the 7 × 9 base is right for a standing slice of a 6-tall box. The pyramid's half-height slice is less than half the base.

**Check.** The extracted rectangle's two labels multiply to the answer; the third length never appears.

**Line.** "A slice is flat: two lengths, square units."

**Trap.** Multiplying all three lengths (378, the volume) because three are given; and using the width the plane cuts across instead of the edge it runs along (54). The muted third length and the extracted rectangle with its two labels end both.

**Kit.** Family step 4 (`slice`, `extract`, pyramid). Nothing beyond.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model from this concept: one picture, two lengths, multiply.

### Grade 7 (filed): `geo-section-impossible`

**Misfiled in part.** The prism case is 7.G.A.3; the cylinder and cone cases are HSG.GMD.B.4 ("identify the shapes of two-dimensional cross-sections of three-dimensional objects") and belong in `sections-and-revolutions`. One lesson can serve all three because the method is one sentence, but the type should be split by grade.

**Standard.** 7.G.A.3 (prism); HSG.GMD.B.4 (cylinder, cone).

**Foundations first.** Foundation 7, extended to curved surfaces. Build on three solids side by side: a box, a cylinder, a cone. The rule from `cross-section-shape`: a flat face gives the section one straight side. New: a curved surface gives a **curve**, except where the plane runs along it: a cylinder's side cut parallel to its axis gives two straight lines (show it); a cone's side cut through its apex gives two straight lines (show it). So each solid has a **largest number of straight sides** it can give, and can only give a curve if it has a curved surface. Box: six flat faces, so three to six straight sides, never a curve. Cylinder: two flat bases plus at most two straight lines from the side: at most four straight sides; or a circle (parallel to the base), or an ellipse (tilted). Cone: one flat base plus at most two lines through the apex: at most three straight sides; or a circle, or an ellipse. Each named shape is produced on screen by the plane as it is said.

**Read.** "Cannot" (gold). Which solid. At a glance: a curved choice against a box is out; a straight-sided choice with more sides than the solid can give is out.

**The example (cylinder).** "A plane cuts through a cylinder. Which shape cannot be the cross section?" Choices: an ellipse | a rectangle | a pentagon | a circle. (1) The plane parallel to the base: a circle. Tilted: an ellipse. Parallel to the axis, through both bases: a rectangle, two straight sides from the bases, two from the side. (2) A pentagon needs five straight sides. The cylinder's straight sides come from two flat bases and at most two lines along its side: four at most. (3) A pentagon cannot be made.

**Quick cases.** Box: an ellipse needs a curve and a box has no curved surface; the triangle, hexagon (six faces, six sides, shown with the plane tilted through all six) and trapezoid all appear. Cone: a rectangle needs four straight sides; the cone has one flat face and two lines through the apex, three at most; the triangle, ellipse and circle appear.

**Fast.** Curve needs a curved surface. Count the straight sides the solid can give: box 6, cylinder 4, cone 3; anything with more is out.

**Sense.** Every possible choice should have been drawn by the plane on screen; the impossible one was never drawn because it cannot be.

**Check.** Count the choice's straight sides against the solid's most.

**Rule out.** For a box, any curved shape at once. For a cylinder or cone, any shape with more straight sides than four or three. What remains must be one shape; prove the others by slicing them.

**Line.** "A flat face gives one straight side. A curve needs a curved surface."

**Trap.** "A box can only make rectangles," so the hexagon looks impossible, which is the decoy. The tilted plane through all six faces, drawn, ends it.

**Kit.** `slice` on `cylinder` and `cone` with tilted and axis-parallel planes (ellipse, rectangle, triangle), and a `through` plane on a box crossing six faces. Family steps 4 and 5.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: the argument is a count made visible by planes, and a weak author lists shapes instead of cutting them.

### Grade 8: `mcas-cylinder-scaling-select`

**Standard.** 8.G.C.9, "Know the formulas for the volumes of ... cylinders ... and use them". Select 2 of 5.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 10. Write V = π · r · r · h with the radius in teal twice and the height in blue once, and say why: the base disc is πr², the radius counted twice; the height stacks it once. Then the scale picture on a cylinder with r = 1, h = 1 (volume π): double the radius, the ghost of the old one inside, and the base disc's bounding square becomes four of the old squares: volume 4π. Double the height instead: a second cylinder stacks on the first: 2π. Say the sentence: a change to the radius hits twice; a change to the height hits once.

**Read.** "Choose the 2 true statements" (gold: 2). Each statement names what is multiplied and by how much. At a glance: a statement that multiplies the radius by f and claims the volume multiplies by f (not f × f) is false.

**The example.** "The volume of a cylinder with radius r and height h is V = πr²h. Choose the 2 true statements." Five statements as the app shows them. On the board, each becomes a line under the formula with the multiplier in place of the length: (a) radius × ½: π(½r)²h = ¼πr²h: ¼, true. (b) radius × 2 and height × ½: π(2r)²(½h) = 4 × ½ = 2 times, not 1: false. (c) radius × 2: 4 times, not 2: false. (d) radius × 3: 9 times, not 3: false. (e) height × 2: 2 times: true. (f) a and e.

**Fast.** Radius multiplier squared, height multiplier as is, multiply the two.

**Sense.** A bigger cylinder has more volume; a half radius should shrink it a lot (to a quarter), a half height only to a half. The ghost picture.

**Check.** Exactly two chosen; each recomputed from the formula with the multipliers in.

**Rule out.** Three statements claim the radius multiplier passes straight through to the volume (c, d, and b, which treats 2 × ½ as 1): all three are the same mistake and dim together.

**Line.** "The radius is squared, so it counts twice."

**Trap.** Believing doubling the radius doubles the volume. Children do, every time. The four old squares inside the new base end it.

**Kit.** `scale` with the ghost and the bounding squares (family step 5); a select-many card whose statements wrap, and `dim` on several at once.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one picture, one substitution per statement, the math check verifies each line.

### Grade 8: `cylinder-volume`

**Standard.** 8.G.C.9: "Know the formulas for the volumes of cones, cylinders, and spheres and use them to solve real-world and mathematical problems."

**Foundations first.** Foundation 8, with the stack of discs. A disc of radius 7 is drawn; its area is πr², borrowed from family 28 in one line: π × 7 × 7 = 49π. Discs stack to height 13 while the count reads 49π, 98π, ... : the cylinder is a stack of discs, so volume is base times height, exactly as a box is a stack of layers. V = πr² × h, B gold, h blue. One more line before any problem: a *diameter* goes all the way across; a *radius* is half of it; the formula wants the radius. "In terms of π" means the answer is a number times π, and π stays a letter to the end; "use 3.14" means multiply on the last line only.

**Read.** Radius or diameter (gold on the word); volume asked, or height asked. "aπ" means report the number in front of π. At a glance: if a diameter is given, the first thing written is r = d ÷ 2. If the volume is given, the base area divides it.

**Example A (forward).** "A cylinder has a radius of 7 inches and a height of 13 inches. Its volume is aπ cubic inches. What is a?" Picture to scale, r = 7 on the top, h = 13 blue. (1) Base: π × 7² = 49π. (2) Times the height: 49π × 13 = 637π. (3) a = 637. Then, one beat, the other form: with 3.14, 637 × 3.14 ≈ 2000.18 cubic inches, multiplied last.

**Example B (backward, with a diameter).** A different act: halve, then undo. "A cylinder has a volume of 1134π cubic inches and a diameter of 18 inches. What is its height?" (1) d = 18, so r = 9, written first on its own line, the radius drawn as half the top. (2) Base: π × 81 = 81π. (3) The volume is 81π layers' worth: h = 1134π ÷ 81π = 14 inches. The cylinder grows to 14 as the discs stack and the count reaches 1134π.

**Fast.** Halve a diameter; square the radius; times π; times the height. Height asked: divide the volume by πr².

**Sense.** The box test: a cylinder is about three quarters of the box that just holds it. Example A's box is 14 × 14 × 13 = 2,548; three quarters is about 1,900; 637π ≈ 2,000. Right size. A found height drawn to 14 beside a base of 18 should look like the problem's can.

**Check.** Divide back: 637 ÷ 49 = 13. For the height: 81 × 14 = 1,134.

**Line.** "Diameter? Halve it first. Base times height, for any stack."

**Trap.** Using the diameter as the radius, which multiplies the answer by four; and forgetting to square (writing πrh). Both are the most common grade-8 errors on this standard. The halving line written first and the disc area drawn as a square count (49 unit squares in the bounding square, roughly three quarters shaded) end them.

**Kit.** `cylinder` with `stack discs` and the running count, `d` and `r` labels on the top face. Family step 5.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model, and the family's own candidate for the blind test: one formula forward and backward, every line machine-checkable.

### Grade 8: `cone-sphere-volume`

**Alignment note.** The goal names cone, sphere and hemisphere; every variant the generator produced is a cone. Either the sphere branch is never reached or the sample missed it; confirm with `facts.ts`. 8.G.C.9 names spheres, so the lesson covers them regardless. The answer 15/4 for "a" must also accept 3.75 (noted in the family design).

**Standard.** 8.G.C.9: "Know the formulas for the volumes of cones, cylinders, and spheres and use them".

**Foundations first.** Foundation 9, two pictures, each under a minute, each staying on screen. **The pour:** a cone and a cylinder with the same base (r = 5) and same height (6) side by side; the cone fills with teal and pours in; the level rises to a mark labeled ⅓; again, ⅔; again, full. A cone is a third of its can: V = ⅓ × πr² × h. **The can:** a sphere of radius r just fits a can of radius r and height 2r; the sphere turns to liquid and fills the can to the ⅔ mark. The can holds πr² × 2r = 2πr³, so the sphere is ⅔ × 2πr³ = ⁴⁄₃πr³. A hemisphere is half: ⅔πr³. "Cone, sphere, can: one, two, three." Then the two reading words: a diameter is halved first; "aπ" keeps π a letter and reports a, as a fraction if it is one ("four hundred twenty-five over three; do not round").

**Read.** Which solid (gold); radius or diameter; "aπ" or "use 3.14". At a glance: a diameter means r = d ÷ 2 is the first line; a cone's answer is a third of the cylinder's, so it is smaller than πr²h; a hemisphere's is half a sphere's.

**Example A (cone, diameter, 3.14).** "A cone has a diameter of 10 centimeters and a height of 6 centimeters. Use 3.14 for π. What is its volume?" Picture: the cone to scale, d = 10 across the top, h = 6 blue, the ghost of its can around it. (1) d = 10, so r = 5. (2) The can: π × 25 × 6 = 150π. (3) The cone is a third: 150π ÷ 3 = 50π. (4) Last line: 50 × 3.14 = 157 cubic centimeters. The three pours play once more, quickly, as the ÷ 3 is written.

**Example B (sphere, then hemisphere).** "A sphere has a diameter of 18 centimeters. Its volume is aπ cubic centimeters." (1) r = 9. (2) Cube it: 9 × 9 × 9 = 729. (3) ⁴⁄₃ × 729 = 972; a = 972. (4) One beat: the hemisphere is half, 486π. The can beside it: 2 × 729 = 1,458π, and the sphere is two thirds of it.

**Fast.** Cone: πr²h, then divide by 3. Sphere: r³, times 4, divided by 3. Hemisphere: r³, times 2, divided by 3.

**Sense.** The box test: a cone is about a quarter of its box (10 × 10 × 6 = 600; a quarter is 150; 157 fits); a sphere about half of its box (18³ = 5,832; half is about 2,900; 972π ≈ 3,054). A cone is less than its can; a hemisphere is less than its sphere.

**Check.** Multiply back: 50 × 3 = 150 = 25 × 6. 972 ÷ 4 × 3 = 729 = 9³.

**Rule out** (where the variant has choices): the decoys are the factors: 4× (diameter used as radius on a cone), 3× (the third dropped), 8× (diameter cubed), 2× (sphere given for a hemisphere). Name them as the estimate kills them.

**Line.** "A cone fills its can in three pours; a sphere fills two thirds."

**Trap.** Dropping the third, or using a half for it; using the diameter; for the sphere, squaring instead of cubing. All three are what eighth graders do. The three pours, the halving line first, and 9 × 9 × 9 said as three factors with the cube drawn make them impossible.

**Kit.** `cone`, `sphere`, `hemisphere`, `pour` with the ⅓, ⅔, 1 scale, and the sphere-in-can liquid at ⅔. Family step 5.

**Writer.** Strongest writer, as the pilot says: the two "why" pictures must land in under a minute each and stay with the learner.

### Grade 8: `cylinder-volume-slots`

**Standard.** 8.G.C.9, the "cylinders" and "real-world problems" clauses (a pool).

**Foundations first.** Short, after `cylinder-volume` in the learner's path but self-contained here: the base is a disc, πr², the radius counted twice; the height stacks it once. V = π × r × r × h, written with the two r slots in teal and the h slot in blue, over a cylinder drawn with the same colors. The two reading words a pool uses: "across" is the diameter, the whole top; "from its center to its edge" is the radius; "deep" is the height.

**Read.** Which words describe the pool (gold): "18 feet across" is a diameter, so the radius is 9; "5 feet deep" is the height. At a glance: the radius fills two slots, the depth one; the number in the bank that is the whole width (18) belongs in no slot.

**The example.** "A round pool is 18 feet across and 5 feet deep. Complete the equation for the volume V of the pool in cubic feet." The bank as the app shows it. Picture: the pool as a cylinder, to scale (18 across, 5 deep: a wide, shallow can), the full width labeled 18 and the radius drawn as half of it. (1) "Across" is the diameter: 18 ÷ 2 = 9 is the radius, written on the figure. (2) The first slot takes the radius, 9, on the word; the second slot takes it again: the disc needs r twice. (3) The third slot takes the depth, 5. V = π · 9 · 9 · 5. (4) One beat: the pool's radius variant, "3 feet from its center to its edge": no halving; 3, 3, then 5.

**Fast.** Find the radius (halve "across"); write it twice; write the depth once.

**Sense.** The two matching slots must hold matching numbers, and the figure's radius is visibly half the width.

**Check.** Read the finished equation against the figure: radius, radius, height; each number points at its length.

**Rule out.** 18 in a radius slot is the whole width, not half; any slot pattern with three different numbers, or the depth twice, is wrong because the disc needs the radius twice.

**Line.** "Radius, radius, height."

**Trap.** Putting the width across in the radius slots. The radius drawn as half the top ends it.

**Kit.** `slots` on the board (family 4.2): lettered blanks and a bank, a blank filled on the word.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: two lines of thought and one picture.

### Grade 8 (filed): `geo-sphere-diameter`

**Misfiled.** 8.G.C.9 uses the formulas forward, and 8.EE.A.2 takes cube roots of perfect cubes only; this type undoes ⁴⁄₃πr³ with a calculator cube root and rounds. It belongs in Geometry (`solid-volume-models`, HSG.GMD.A.3 "use volume formulas ... to solve problems"). Designed here as a Geometry lesson.

**Standard.** HSG.GMD.A.3 (use the sphere volume formula to solve a problem); the undoing is family 11's.

**Foundations first.** The sphere in its can, one beat: V = ⁴⁄₃πr³, the radius cubed because a sphere grows three ways. Then the one idea: a formula is a recipe, and undoing it runs the recipe backward in reverse order. Cubing is the last thing done to r, so it is the first thing undone, and its undo is the **cube root**, not the square root: 10³ = 1,000, so ∛1,000 = 10, while √1,000 ≈ 31.6 is a different number. Two lines on the board, side by side, so the two roots are never confused. Rounding: nothing is rounded until the last line.

**Read.** Radius or diameter asked (gold); "nearest tenth". At a glance: the answer is a radius, then possibly doubled; the volume given with no π means a decimal answer and a calculator.

**The example.** "A sphere has a volume of 4576 in³. Find its diameter to the nearest tenth of an inch." Picture: the sphere with its diameter drawn across, r as half, the can ghosted. (1) ⁴⁄₃πr³ = 4576. (2) Undo the ⁴⁄₃: multiply both sides by 3 and divide by 4: πr³ = 3,432. (3) Undo the π: r³ = 3,432 ÷ π ≈ 1,092.4, kept to a decimal place, not rounded further. (4) Undo the cube: r = ∛1,092.4 ≈ 10.3. (5) The question asked for the diameter: d = 2 × 10.3 ≈ 20.6 inches; the diameter on the figure labels 20.6.

**Fast.** r³ = 3V ÷ (4π); cube root; double if a diameter is asked. Round once, at the end.

**Sense.** The box test: a sphere is about half of the cube that just holds it, so d³ ≈ 2V ≈ 9,152 and d ≈ ∛9,152 ≈ 20.9. The answer 20.6 is right beside it. A radius of 31.6 (the square root) would make a sphere of 132,000 in³: nonsense.

**Check.** Forward: ⁴⁄₃ × π × 10.3³ ≈ 4,577. Close to 4,576 by the rounding.

**Line.** "Undo in reverse: the cube came last, so the cube root comes first."

**Trap.** Taking a square root; and reporting the radius when the diameter was asked (or the reverse). Both are what students do. The two roots written side by side with 1,000, and the last beat that rereads the question with "diameter" gold, end them.

**Kit.** Nothing beyond `sphere` with `r` and `d` labels. A board check that tolerates ≈ with a stated rounding.

**Writer.** Capable cheaper model: one undoing chain, machine-checkable, once the concept names the two traps.
