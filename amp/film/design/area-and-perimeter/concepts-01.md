# Area and perimeter, part 1: the ten grade 3 and 4 concepts

Written from the family design. Conventions it inherits: every grade 3 and 4 lesson here opens with the fifteen-second picture (teal floods the inside: "area, how much it covers, counted in squares"; the rust string walks the edge: "perimeter, how far around, counted in plain units"), then reads the problem with its deciding word gold. Grade 3 lessons use no letters and never say "formula"; grade 4 lessons may write a box for the unknown. Figures are to scale on a unit grid in both grades. A written number on a figure is a fact the learner has; a gold "?" is a fact to find.

---

### Grade 3: rectangle-area (3.MD.C.5, C.6, C.7)

**`rectangle-area` — Area of a rectangle.** Foundations 1 and 2. The picture is born here.

Standard: 3.MD.C.7a, "Find the area of a rectangle with whole-number side lengths by tiling it, and show that the area is the same as would be found by multiplying the side lengths," and 7b, "Multiply side lengths to find areas of rectangles." The lesson also does 3.MD.C.5a ("a square with side length 1 unit ... one square unit") and C.6 (counting squares) on screen, because the app never asks for the counting.

Foundations first: a segment one centimeter long; a square built on it, grid line visible, labeled "1 square centimeter": the unit is called *square* because it *is* a square, one centimeter on each side. Area: how many of these squares cover a shape, no gaps, no overlaps. Then the shared opening: the inside floods teal (area), the string walks the edge (perimeter), so the learner sees from the first minute that two different things can be measured on one rectangle.

Read: "A rectangle is 7 cm wide and 5 cm high. What is its area in square centimeters?" "Area" and "square centimeters" go gold: the inside, a count of squares. "Wide" is the top side, "high" the right side, both labeled on brackets. At a glance: it is far more than 7 + 5 = 12 squares; twelve squares would not fill two rows.

The example: 7 by 5, drawn 7 to 5 on the grid. (1) One unit square lands in the top-left corner, labeled "1 square cm". (2) Tile: squares fill row by row, the count ticking to 35. That count is the area; no multiplication yet. (3) One row lights gold: "one row of seven." Row labels appear as the rows are counted: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35. Five rows of seven. (4) Under the figure: 5 × 7 = 35. "Rows of squares: that is why we multiply, and why the answer is in square centimeters: it is thirty-five of those squares." (5) Answer: 35 square centimeters; the inside label appears centered in the teal.

Fast: multiply the two sides. Always works for a rectangle.
Sense: five sevens; the count on screen agrees with the skip count.
Check: count the other way: seven columns of five, 5, 10, 15 ... 35. Same squares, same number, either way round.
Line: "Rows of squares: that's why we multiply." "Area covers."
Trap: 7 + 5 = 12, the two labels added. The tile makes it impossible: 12 squares are on screen before the second row is finished. A second, quieter one: "square centimeters" heard as "the shape is a square"; the unit square is drawn and named as the square.
Kit: `region` with `grid`, `dims`, `tile` (count ticking), `rows` (one row lit, per-row labels), `inside`. Nothing beyond the family's tools.
Writer: a capable cheaper model, once `region` exists: one picture, one method, named actions.

**`area-tiling-distributive` — Area from the parts.** Foundations 2 and 3. A mixed type, and the design says so: three figures under one name, (A) a rectangle split in two with expression choices (two wordings, one of them a NOT question), (B) a split rectangle with one part's area given, (C) an L cut by a dashed line. One opening, three examples.

Standard: 3.MD.C.7c, "Use tiling to show in a concrete case that the area of a rectangle with whole-number side lengths a and b + c is the sum of a × b and a × c," and 7d, "Recognize area as additive. Find areas of rectilinear figures by decomposing them into non-overlapping rectangles and adding." 3.OA.B.5 (the distributive property) rides along.

Foundations first: after the shared opening, a tiled 16 by 6 rectangle; a gold cut draws down at 10; the left piece takes teal, the right blue. Left: 6 rows of 10, 60. Right: 6 rows of 6, 36. 60 + 36 = 96, and the whole is 6 rows of 16, 96. Cut anywhere and the areas add, because every square is still counted once. Define "expression" on a `term` card as the problem does: numbers joined by signs. Then the reading rule for this lesson: *a times is a rectangle* (rows × squares in a row); *a lone number is just that many loose squares*, not a piece.

Read: which figure is it, and what is asked: an expression, or a number of square units? In the NOT wording, NOT goes gold and the lesson says aloud that three choices are right and one is wrong. At a glance: a number answer is less than the box the figure sits in and more than either piece; an expression answer has one product per piece.

Example A (expression, the NOT wording): 6 tall, 16 wide, split 10 and 6; choices 6 × 10 + 6 × 6 | 16 × 6 | 6 + 10 × 6 | 6 × 6 + 6 × 10. The figure from the foundation. (1) 16 × 6 is the whole, lit as one. (2) 6 × 10 + 6 × 6 is left then right; 6 × 6 + 6 × 10 is right then left; each product lights its piece. (3) 6 + 10 × 6: the rectangle 10 × 6 lights, and then six loose squares appear beside it in gold, nothing like the 36-square right piece. It adds a side to an area. Answer: 6 + 10 × 6. Quick case, the "region A and region B" wording (6 rows of 5 cut across into 4 rows and 2 rows): the cut goes the other way, each product is rows × 5: 4 × 5 + 2 × 5; three beats.

Example B (a number): 3 tall; left part 20 wide with area 60; right part 3 wide; choices 83 | 9 | 69 | 63. (1) "Area of the whole" gold. (2) The left piece is already counted: 60, written inside. (3) The right piece: 3 rows of 3, 9 squares, written inside. (4) 60 + 9 = 69 square units. Check: 3 × 23 = 69.

Example C (the L): figure 6 ft across the top, right side 2 ft, the arm's underside 4 ft, inner vertical 5 ft, bottom 2 ft, left side 7 ft; the dashed line runs under the arm. Choices 42 | 12 | 22 | 26. (1) The dashed line is a cut already drawn, muted; two rectangles, teal and blue. (2) Top: 6 × 2 = 12. Leg: 2 × 5 = 10. (3) 12 + 10 = 22 square feet. Check: the box is 6 × 7 = 42, the missing corner 4 × 5 = 20, 42 − 20 = 22; the two ways agree.

Fast: one product per rectangle, add. For an expression: products are rectangles, plus signs join pieces.
Sense: less than the box, more than any one piece.
Check: compute the whole another way (whole width × height; or box minus corner).
Rule out: a number equal to one piece alone (9, 12) is gone; a number as big as the box (42) or bigger (83) is gone; an expression with a lone number added to a product (6 + 10 × 6, 4 + 2 × 5, 6 × 5 + 5) is gone, because it adds loose squares that are not in the picture.
Line: "Cut it, the areas add." "A times is a rectangle; a lone number is loose squares."
Trap: reading 6 + 10 × 6 left to right as 16 × 6 = 96, which looks right. The six loose gold squares beside the 10 × 6 rectangle kill it without a word about order of operations, which grade 3 has not met. For the L: multiplying the two overall lengths (42) and adding a width to an area (63). The L's corner is shown as not there; 60 + 3 is shown as sixty squares and three loose ones.
Kit: `region` with `cut`, piece colors, `inside` labels; expression choices lit piece by piece (each product in its piece's color, the kit's TeX colors); `dim`. The loose-squares beat is a few gold unit squares placed beside the figure: `grid` and `counters` do it.
Writer: the strongest writer. The NOT question, the order-of-operations decoy and three figures are reasoning the screenplay must stage, and the distributive picture for families 7 and 12 is laid here.

### Grade 3: perimeter (3.MD.D.8)

**`rectangle-border-length` — Ribbon around a rectangle.** Foundations 4 and 5.

Standard: 3.MD.D.8, "finding the perimeter given the side lengths."

Foundations first: the shared opening, then the definition: perimeter is how long the trip around the edge is, starting at a corner and ending back at it; a length, so plain centimeters. One line from family 22: a rectangle's opposite sides are the same length. "Ribbon that goes around" is a perimeter question whatever the shape.

Read: "How many cm of ribbon go once around this rectangle?" The figure shows 7 cm on top and 4 cm on the right. "Around" goes gold: a trip, plain cm. At a glance: more than 14, since the two long sides alone are 14; less than 28, four long sides.

The example: 7 by 4 to scale on the grid, two sides labeled. (1) The bottom and left have no numbers; opposite sides match, so 7 and 4 are written in muted, then plain. (2) Walk: the rust string starts at the top-left corner and draws along the top; its running label reads 7. Down the right: 11. (3) Pause at the opposite corner: "Half the trip. One long side and one short side, 7 and 4, 11." (4) The string continues: 18, 22, back at the start corner. (5) Board: 7 + 4 + 7 + 4 = 22, then under it 11 + 11 = 22: the second half is the same as the first. Answer: 22 cm of ribbon.

Fast: one of each side, add, double. Always works for a rectangle.
Sense: more than twice the long side (14), less than four of it (28).
Check: walk from a different corner; the label ends at 22 again.
Line: "Half the trip is one of each side." "Perimeter goes around."
Trap: 7 + 4 = 11, half the trip; and 7 × 4 = 28, the inside. The walk visibly passes 11 at the opposite corner and keeps going; the teal and the string in the opening keep the two measures apart. On a gridded figure a third one: counting the squares along the border (18 for a 7 by 4) instead of the edge, because the corner squares are counted once but touch two edges. The string ticks at corners, not squares, so the counts never meet.
Kit: `region` with `dims` (deduced sides written on the word), `walk` with `half` and the running label. Nothing new.
Writer: a capable cheaper model.

**`rectangle-missing-border-side` — The missing side from the perimeter.** Foundation 5. Pilot lesson 2.

Standard: 3.MD.D.8, "finding an unknown side length."

Foundations first: shared opening; perimeter as the trip; then "half the trip is one of each side", built: a labeled rectangle is walked, the string pauses at the opposite corner, and the half and the whole are both read off the label. That is the fact the whole lesson uses.

Read: "This rectangle has perimeter 32 cm. Find its missing side in cm." The figure shows 4 cm on the short side and a gold "?" on the long one. "Perimeter 32" and "missing side" go gold. The answer is a length in plain cm, and it must be less than 16, because 16 is the whole half-trip and the 4 is part of it. At a glance the drawn side is about three times the 4.

The example: a 12 by 4 rectangle to scale, "4 cm" on the right, "?" on top. (1) Walk the whole string once with the label running to 32 at the start corner: that is what "perimeter 32" says. (2) Walk again, pause at the opposite corner: half the trip is 16; the string drawn so far is one "?" and one 4. Board: ? + 4 = 16. (3) 16 − 4 = 12; the "?" becomes 12, and the opposite side gets 12 too. (4) Answer: 12 cm. (5) Check: walk all four: 12, 16, 28, 32.

Fast: halve the perimeter, subtract the known side.
Sense: less than 16; about three times as long as the 4 in the picture.
Check: add all four sides back to 32. Or the other sure route, shown once as the check: take away both 4s, 32 − 8 = 24, and share it between the two long sides, 12 each.
Line: "Half the trip is one of each side."
Trap: 32 − 4 = 28 (the whole trip minus one side) and (32 − 4) ÷ 2 = 14 (one 4 forgotten). Both come from working with the whole trip. The lesson never subtracts from 32; the pause makes 16 the only number the 4 is ever taken from, and 28 fails the glance (more than 16) on sight.
Kit: `walk` with `half`, the "?" dim turning into its value. Nothing new.
Writer: a capable cheaper model.

### Grade 4: area-perimeter-models (4.MD.A.3)

**`rectangle-missing-area-side` — The missing side from the area.** Foundation 5, the area half.

Standard: 4.MD.A.3, "find the width of a rectangular room given the area of the flooring and the length, by viewing the area formula as a multiplication equation with an unknown factor."

Foundations first: shared opening; area as rows of the known side (one line: "rows of squares, that's why we multiply"); the box for a missing number in a multiplication, named from family 11 as the missing factor.

Read: "This rectangle covers 15 square cm. Find its missing side in cm." Figure: 5 cm on top, gold "?" on the right. "Covers" and "square cm" go gold: the inside is known; the answer is a plain length. At a glance: 15 squares in rows of 5 make fewer than 5 rows, since 5 rows would be 25; and the missing side is shorter than the 5 in the picture.

The example: 5 by 3 to scale. (1) Deal: fifteen squares drop into the rectangle in rows of five, the count ticking 5, 10, 15; three rows. (2) The number of rows is the missing side: the "?" becomes 3. (3) Board: 5 × □ = 15, so □ = 15 ÷ 5 = 3. "How many rows of five make fifteen: that is a division." (4) Answer: 3 cm. (5) Check: 5 × 3 = 15.

Fast: divide the area by the known side.
Sense: smaller than the area, and 15 ÷ 5 is a fact the learner knows.
Check: multiply back; the product is the area given.
Line: "How many rows? Divide."
Trap: 15 − 5 = 10, the perimeter type's subtraction carried over. The deal shows 15 squares making only 3 rows of 5; ten rows would need 50 squares. The gold "covers" and the square unit are what tell the two types apart, and the lesson says so.
Kit: `deal`. Nothing new.
Writer: a capable cheaper model.

**`border-or-cover-unit` — Which unit?** Foundations 1 and 4.

Misfiled first: this is 3.MD.C.5, the family's first idea, sitting in a grade 4 skill; it should open the family at grade 3. Its "cubic cm" choice is grade 5 vocabulary (5.MD.C.3), so the lesson defines it in passing with a cube on screen, no `term` card.

Standard: 3.MD.C.5a and b, "A square with side length 1 unit, called 'a unit square,' is said to have 'one square unit' of area," and "a plane figure which can be covered without gaps or overlaps by n unit squares is said to have an area of n square units"; the length unit for a trip around comes from 3.MD.D.8.

Foundations first: one centimeter drawn as a segment. Build a square on it: one square centimeter, the unit that covers. Build a cube on it: one cubic centimeter, the unit that fills. Three units from one length, each answering a different question: how long, how much it covers, how much it holds. Then a card: ribbon laid along its edge, pulled straight and measured against a ruler (a length, cm); paper laid on its face, tiled with unit squares (square cm); a box filled with cubes (cubic cm), one beat, since the choice exists.

Read: "Ribbon runs once around a card. Which unit measures the length of ribbon?" Choices cm | cubic cm | square cm. "Around" and "length" go gold. At a glance: nothing is being filled, so cubic cm is gone; a ribbon is a line.

The example: the card with the ribbon walking its edge as a rust string, then straightening into a line beside a ruler: it is a length, and lengths are measured in centimeters. Answer: cm. Quick second case (the other wording): "Paper covers the flat face of a card": "covers" gold; unit squares tile the face; square cm. Three beats.

Fast: around means cm; covers means square cm; fills means cubic cm.
Sense: say it in the sentence: "22 cm of ribbon" sounds right; "22 square cm of ribbon" does not, because a ribbon is a line.
Check: lay the unit on the thing: a segment along the ribbon, a square on the face, a cube in the box.
Rule out: cubic cm dims first in both problems, nothing is filled; then the gold word decides between the two left.
Line: "Square units cover; plain units go around."
Trap: picking square cm for anything about a flat card, because the card is a flat rectangle. The ribbon pulled straight against a ruler kills it: the unit belongs to the ribbon, not the card.
Kit: a card as `region` with `walk`; the string straightening to a ruler (`morph` or the app's ruler scene); a unit square and a unit cube (the app's cube solids) built on one segment. Nothing beyond the family's tools and the app's scenes.
Writer: a capable cheaper model: two short readings and a picture.

**`same-area-border-choice` — Same area, which is shorter around?** Foundation 6. The sibling of pilot lesson 3; both lessons share the opening below, each reshaping its own way.

Standard: 4.MD.A.3 (apply the perimeter formula) and 3.MD.D.8, "exhibiting rectangles ... with the same area and different perimeters."

Foundations first: shared opening, then the surprise, staged: 24 squares laid 1 by 24, string label 50; the squares rearrange to 2 by 12, label 28; 3 by 8, 22; 4 by 6, 20. Same squares every time, the trip shrinking as the shape gets squarer. "Equal inside does not mean equal edge. The squarer shape has less edge for its inside."

Read: "These rectangles have equal areas. Which needs less ribbon around its border?" Choices They are equal | B | A. Figures to one scale: A is 2 by 12, B is 4 by 6. "Less ribbon around" goes gold: perimeter, plain cm. At a glance: B is the squarer one, so B should be shorter around; and "They are equal" cannot be true of two different rectangles.

The example: (1) The areas, since the problem states them: 24 and 24, written inside each; the teal is the same amount in both. (2) Walk A: 12, 14, half the trip 14, doubled 28. Walk B: 4, 10, half the trip 10, doubled 20. Both strings stay on screen, B's visibly shorter. (3) 20 is less than 28: B. (4) Check: both areas are 24, both perimeters recomputed.

Fast: compute both half-trips and double. The squarer rectangle is the shorter one around, always, for equal areas; the lesson teaches it as the glance and proves it with the reshape, but the answer is boxed from the numbers.
Sense: a long thin strip has a lot of edge for little inside.
Check: both strings' labels; the shorter is the answer.
Rule out: "They are equal" dims at once, with the reason: equal areas make equal edges only when the rectangles are the same rectangle.
Line: "Squarer is shorter around."
Trap: choosing "They are equal" because the areas are equal; and recomputing areas (24, 24) instead of perimeters. The reshape kills the first; the gold word and the string the second.
Kit: `reshape` with `keep: "area"` (live string label and count), two regions at one scale, `dim`. Nothing new beyond the family's list.
Writer: the strongest writer for the first wave (the reshape must be staged, not described); a natural cast lesson, the student saying "same area, so same ribbon."

**`same-border-area-choice` — Same perimeter, which covers more?** Foundation 6. Pilot lesson 3.

Standard: 4.MD.A.3 (apply the area formula) and 3.MD.D.8, "exhibiting rectangles with the same perimeter and different areas."

Foundations first: shared opening, then the reshape with the fixed string: a 1 by 9 rectangle, string label 20, 9 squares inside; it morphs to 2 by 8, 16 squares; 3 by 7, 21; 4 by 6, 24; 5 by 5, 25. The label never moves; the count climbs. "The same string can go around a little or a lot. The squarer it is, the more it holds."

Read: "These rectangles have equal perimeters. Which covers more area?" Choices They are equal | A | B. Figures to one scale: A is 2 by 8, B is 4 by 6. "Covers more" goes gold: the inside, square units. At a glance: B is squarer, so B; "They are equal" is out.

The example: (1) The perimeters first, because the problem says they are equal: A, 2 + 8 = 10, doubled 20; B, 4 + 6 = 10, doubled 20; the same string. (2) Tile A: 2 rows of 8, 16. Tile B: 4 rows of 6, 24; written inside each. (3) 24 is more than 16: B. (4) Check: both perimeters are 20; the tiles are counted.

Fast: multiply each pair and compare. The squarer one wins, always, for equal perimeters; taught as the glance, proved by the reshape, boxed from the numbers.
Sense: the thin one is nearly all edge.
Check: the two strings are the same length; the counts inside differ.
Rule out: "They are equal" dims at once: equal edges do not make equal insides unless the rectangles are the same.
Line: "Squarer is bigger inside."
Trap: "the longer one covers more": it looks like more because it is longer. The reshape with the string fixed shows the long one holding the fewest squares. The cast version puts this line in the student's mouth and lets the string answer.
Kit: `reshape` with `keep: "perimeter"`, two regions at one scale, `dim`.
Writer: the strongest writer, as the pilot lesson that proves the family's central surprise.

**`rectangle-two-constraints-choice` — An area and a perimeter limit.** Foundations 5 and 6.

Standard: 4.MD.A.3, "apply the area and perimeter formulas for rectangles in ... mathematical problems"; 3.MD.C.7b's "represent whole-number products as rectangular areas" is what the factor pairs do.

Foundations first: shared opening. Then: every rectangle with 20 squares is a factor pair of 20, and every pair is a rectangle; the pairs are drawn to one scale, 1 by 20, 2 by 10, 4 by 5, each tiled with the same 20 squares. A table beside them, sides | perimeter, empty. "At most 18" defined: 18 or less; 18 itself is allowed.

Read: "Use positive whole-number sides. Area must be 20 square cm; perimeter at most 18 cm. Which rectangle works?" Choices 1 cm by 20 cm | 4 cm by 5 cm | 2 cm by 10 cm. Two conditions, both gold; a rectangle must pass both. At a glance: check area first: 1 × 20, 4 × 5, 2 × 10 are all 20, so the area throws nothing out; 4 by 5 is the squarest, so it has the shortest trip, and if any works it does.

The example: (1) Each choice's sides multiply to 20: the areas written inside the three drawn rectangles. (2) Walk each and fill the table on the word: 1 by 20, half-trip 21, doubled 42; 2 by 10, 12, 24; 4 by 5, 9, 18. (3) "At most 18": 42 and 24 are too long; 18 is allowed. (4) Answer: 4 cm by 5 cm. (5) Check: 4 × 5 = 20 and 2 × (4 + 5) = 18, which is at most 18.

Fast: area first (sides multiply to it); then compute every candidate's perimeter and test it against the limit. Try the squarest pair first: it has the smallest perimeter, so if it fails, none passes.
Sense: 20 squares need the least fence when they are closest to a square.
Check: both conditions re-read against the chosen rectangle, with "at most" said as "18 or less."
Rule out: a choice whose sides do not multiply to the area is gone before any walking (the generator may make one); then any perimeter over the limit dims, with the table as the reason.
Line: "Compute every candidate."
Trap: reading "at most 18" as "less than 18" and rejecting the only rectangle that works. The lesson draws the limit on a short number line with a filled dot at 18. A second one: choosing 1 by 20 because the 20 matches the area in the question; its 42-cm string on screen is longer than the other two put together.
Kit: `table` rows filled on the word (exists), three regions at one scale with `walk`, `dim`.
Writer: the strongest writer for the first wave, as the design says: the "squarest first" reasoning is a small proof and the same argument carries the existence type.

**`rectangle-existence-choice` — Does such a rectangle exist?** Foundation 6.

Badly worded first: the prompt asks "Which statement is true?" and offers rectangles. Reword the prompt ("Which rectangle has both?") or make the choices statements; the lesson reads it as "which rectangle, if any."

Standard: 4.MD.A.3, applying both formulas to one rectangle; the reasoning is 3.MD.D.8's "same area and different perimeters" turned into an argument.

Foundations first: shared opening. Then the complete list of rectangles with 12 squares: 1 by 12, 2 by 6, 3 by 4, drawn to one scale and tiled; the list is complete when the pairs would start repeating (4 by 3 is 3 by 4 turned). A table, sides | perimeter, empty.

Read: "Use positive whole-number sides. Area: 12 square cm. Perimeter: 12 cm. Which statement is true?" Choices 1 cm by 12 cm | 2 cm by 6 cm | No such rectangle. Both numbers are exact, both gold. At a glance: a 12-cm string has a half-trip of 6, so the two sides add to 6; the pairs 1 and 5, 2 and 4, 3 and 3 hold 5, 8 and 9 squares, never 12. That is the whole answer in one breath; the lesson earns it on the picture.

The example: (1) Fill the table by walking each rectangle: 1 by 12, 26; 2 by 6, 16; 3 by 4, 14. (2) Neither offered choice has perimeter 12: 1 by 12 and 2 by 6 dim together with their perimeters as the reason. (3) The pair the choices leave out, 3 by 4, is the squarest, so 14 is the shortest trip 12 squares can have; 12 is shorter than 14, so no rectangle of 12 squares has a 12-cm edge. Not just the two offered: any. (4) Answer: No such rectangle. (5) Check from the other side: a 12-cm string, reshaped, holds at most 9 squares (the 3 by 3); 12 squares is more than it can hold.

Fast: list all the factor pairs of the area with their perimeters; the asked perimeter must match one exactly; if it is smaller than the squarest pair's, no rectangle works.
Sense: the squarest shape has the least edge for its inside; anything shorter around cannot hold the squares.
Check: the string argument above, or recompute the chosen pair's area and perimeter when a pair is the answer.
Rule out: each offered pair dims with its perimeter; "No such rectangle" is never chosen because the others failed. It is proved by the pair the choices omit.
Line: "Squarest pair, smallest trip. If that one can't, none can."
Trap: matching numbers: 1 by 12 chosen because the perimeter is 12. The table shows its string at 26, more than twice what is asked. A second one, when the generator offers the squarest pair as a choice: picking "No such rectangle" without listing every pair; the complete list is built first, every time.
Kit: `table`, three regions at one scale with `walk`, `reshape` with `keep: "perimeter"` for the check, `dim`.
Writer: the strongest writer: a small proof with "which statement" choices, where the reasoning is the lesson.
