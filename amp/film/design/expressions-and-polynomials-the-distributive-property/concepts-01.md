# Expressions and polynomials, part 1: the ten concepts

Grade 5 numerical expressions and grade 6 variable expressions. Each stands on the family design's foundations 1 to 4 and uses the board's motions (box, name, collapse, drop in, copies). Written from the exact variants `facts.ts` produced.

---

## Grade 5: numerical-expressions (5.OA.A.1, 5.OA.A.2)

Every film in this skill opens with the same half-minute, drawn live: an **expression** is a recipe for a number; its **value** is the number it makes; to **evaluate** is to run the recipe. Parentheses make a **group**: "make this one number first." Then the why of the order: five times four is one number the way a word names one thing, so 4 + 5 × 4 is four and five fours. Then the three lines: groups first; then times and divide, left to right; then plus and minus, left to right. The scratch-paper sketch is the lesson's own picture: circle the group, write its value, rewrite the line shorter.

### `operation-order` — Find the value of an expression with a group.

Note first: the app's method says "then powers"; no variant has an exponent and grade 5 has none (exponents are 6.EE.A.1). The lesson does not teach powers.

**Standard.** 5.OA.A.1: "Use parentheses... in numerical expressions, and evaluate expressions with these symbols."

**Foundations first.** The opening above. Plus the why of × before +, with a picture: 6 × 4 as six cards each reading 4 in a row, bracket over them "6 × 4, one number: 24." You cannot add 4 to the first card and call it 6 × 4.

**Read.** "Evaluate" (gold): the answer is a single number. At a glance: the last thing done is the plus, so the expression is "four more than something"; the answer is bigger than 4.

**The example.** 4 + (5 + 1) × 4. (1) Box (5 + 1), name under it "sum": a group, so first. (2) Collapse: 4 + 6 × 4, the 6 gold, everything else standing directly under itself. (3) Name the whole: "four more than six fours"; the box goes on 6 × 4: times before plus. (4) Collapse: 4 + 24. (5) 28. The board shows four lines, each one shorter, exactly one thing changing per line.

**Quick second case** (what the group did): the same digits without it, 4 + 5 + 1 × 4. Box 1 × 4 → 4 + 5 + 4; two pluses left: left to right, 9, then 13. One sentence: "The parentheses are what made us add first." This is the standard's own point and shows left to right once.

**Fast.** The three lines. No shortcut.

**Sense.** Four more than 24; the product is most of the answer.

**Check.** Run it again in the same order and land on 28; or read the collapsed chain back: 28 is 4 more than 24, and 24 is six fours.

**Line.** "A group is one number. The last thing you do is what it is."

**Trap.** Left to right regardless: 4 + 6 = 10, 10 × 4 = 40. The box lands on 6 × 4 with the six cards: six fours is one number before anything is added to it. Shown on a `watch` card as what to do instead, not struck.

**Kit.** `box` and `step` (family 4.1); the copies picture from `grid` with a label per cell.

**Writer.** Cheaper model from this concept: one motion, one example, the arithmetic checked by `--dry`.

### `fl-equation-truth-grouped` — Which equation is true (or false)?

**Standard.** 5.OA.A.1 ("evaluate expressions with these symbols") with 5.NBT.B.7 ("Add, subtract... decimals to hundredths... strategies based on place value").

**Foundations first.** An equation is two expressions with an equals sign between; it is **true** when both sides make the same number (family 11's meaning, said in one line: "equals means the same amount, not 'the answer comes next'"). Each side is its own recipe; run each to one number; compare. The board keeps a vertical gap at the equals sign so the two sides never run together.

**Read.** "True" or "false" (gold) decides which one we are hunting; the work is the same either way. At a glance: compare sizes before computing anything.

**The example.** "Which equation is true?" with the four choices shown. (1) Estimate each in whole numbers, one beat per row, and dim the nonsense together: 1.4 = 7.4 + (2 × 2) says a number is equal to more than 7.4; 8.3 = 15.3 − 2 says taking 2 from fifteen leaves eight-something; gone. (2) Two remain; compute both sides of each. 15.9 + (4 × 2): box the group, 8; 15.9 + 8: the 8 is ones, so ones with ones: 23.9. Right side 28.9 − 4: 24.9. The two numbers stand side by side, not equal: dim, with the reason "off by one: estimation could not tell these apart, so we had to compute." (3) 11.1 + (2 × 6): 12; 23.1. Right side 28.1 − 5: 23.1. The same number on both sides, the equals sign goes gold: true.

**Quick second case** ("which is false"): the gold word flips. Estimate every row: 16 + 0.5 is about 16; 17.5 − 6 is about 11. The sides disagree at the whole-number level, so this is the false one; confirm 16.5 against 11.5. Three or four beats.

**Fast.** Estimate the sides in whole numbers first; compute only the rows the estimate cannot settle.

**Sense.** Adding makes a side bigger than its start; subtracting makes it smaller; a side that must be bigger than 7 cannot equal 1.4.

**Check.** Read the chosen equation aloud as a sentence with both values: "twenty-three point one is twenty-three point one."

**Rule out.** Size first: any equation whose two sides cannot be near each other dims in one beat. Then compute the survivors.

**Line.** "Each side is one number. Equal means the same number."

**Trap.** Adding a whole number into the tenths: 15.3 − 2 as 15.1, or 15.9 + 8 as 16.7. Say "the 8 is ones, so it meets the 5" and light the ones place; if the writer finds the eyes need it, one stack with headers for the first decimal sum only, never for all four.

**Kit.** `box`, `step` on an equation line; a gold equals sign when the two values match.

**Writer.** Cheaper model: one method, estimation then exact, every line machine-checkable.

### `fl-expression-description-match` — Match words to an expression, or an expression to words.

Note first: the variant (1.5 − 9) ÷ 6 has a negative difference; fine to read, not for a grade-5 worked example.

**Standard.** 5.OA.A.2: "Write simple expressions that record calculations with numbers... For example, express the calculation 'add 8 and 7, then multiply by 2' as 2 × (8 + 7)."

**Foundations first.** The four names said once: sum, difference, product, quotient. Then the two reading facts. "Subtract 9 from 1.5" starts with what you have: 1.5 first, take 9 away. "Then" splits a description into step one and step two; whatever is done first and is a plus or minus must go in parentheses, because without them the times or divide would jump ahead (foundation 2). "The difference" in step two is the result of step one, the group itself.

**The example** (words to expression). "Subtract 2 from 6, then divide the difference by 3," with its four choices. (1) Gold on "from 6": start at 6. Write 6 − 2, name it "difference". (2) Gold on "then": step two acts on the difference, so the difference is one number: box it, parentheses appear, "÷ 3" written after. (6 − 2) ÷ 3. (3) Match: the choice lights. (4) Why the parentheses: write 6 − 2 ÷ 3 beneath, name under it "six minus a quotient": the divide would happen first; the words said the difference first.

**Quick second case** (expression to words): 2 × (4.1 + 5). Box the group, name "sum"; the whole, name "a product: two times the sum", and "double" means times two. Choice two lights; "half" is divide by two, dims; "add 5 to double 4.1" has no group (2 × 4.1 + 5), dims; "add 2" has the wrong operation, dims.

**Fast.** List the steps of the expression inside out (group first); list the steps of the words in order; the lists match.

**Sense.** The same two operations in the same order with the same first number.

**Check.** Read the chosen pair against each other one operation at a time: start number, first operation, second operation.

**Rule out.** Wrong operation anywhere dims first (a plus where the words say times). Then wrong order: a choice whose group holds a different operation. Then wrong direction of a subtraction or division. Each decoy named as it dims.

**Line.** "'From' tells you where to start. 'Then' tells you what gets the parentheses."

**Trap.** 2 − 6 for "subtract 2 from 6": the drop-in writes the 6 first, on the word "from". And 6 − 2 ÷ 3 without the group: the name under it reads "six minus a quotient", visibly not the words.

**Kit.** `box` with `name`; nothing new.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: every decoy is a different misreading and nothing is computed, so nothing is checked by machine.

### `mcas-parenthesized-product-statement` — Describe a product of a sum without evaluating.

**Standard.** 5.OA.A.2's second sentence: "Recognize that 3 × (18932 + 921) is three times as large as 18932 + 921, without having to calculate the indicated sum or product."

**Foundations first.** The group is one number even if we never find it. "Times as large as" means copies: 6 × something is six of that something (family 10's "times as many", one line). The copies picture: a card reading (26 + 9), copied six times in a row, a bracket over all six.

**Read.** "Without evaluating" (gold): we are not allowed to add, and we do not need to. At a glance: the thing outside the group is × 6, so the answer says "6 times as large as"; the thing inside is a sum of 26 and 9.

**The example.** (26 + 9) × 6, four choices shown. (1) Box the group, name "the sum of 26 and 9". (2) Six cards, each reading (26 + 9), appear one per count; bracket: "6 times as large as the sum." (3) Choices with a different outside operation dim together: "divided into 6 equal parts" cuts the one card into six; "6 more than" puts a single 6 beside one card. (4) The remaining decoy, drawn: six cards reading 26 and one card reading 9. Beside the true picture, five 9s are missing. "Six times 26 with 9 added afterward" is not six copies of the sum. Dim. (5) The survivor lights. Said aloud: "We never had to add 26 and 9."

**Quick second case.** 5 × (11 − 7): the card reads a difference; five copies; the factor in front means the same thing. "5 times as large as the difference of 11 and 7." Three beats.

**Fast.** Name the inside by its operation; say what the outside does to it.

**Sense.** Six whole cards, every card with both numbers on it.

**Check.** Read the chosen statement against the expression: "the sum of 26 and 9" is the group; "6 times as large" is the × 6.

**Rule out.** Outside operation first: anything not "times" dims in one beat. Then the one that multiplies only one of the two numbers.

**Line.** "Times a group means copies of the whole group."

**Trap.** "6 times 26, with 9 added afterward": multiplying only the first number, the family's great trap in its grade-5 form. Six 26s and six 9s against six 26s and one 9, side by side.

**Kit.** `grid` with a label per cell for the copies; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: a reading type whose whole value is in the two pictures set beside each other and in a fifth grader's words.

### `staar-nested-grouping` — Brackets around parentheses; a product described in words.

Note first: two kinds under one name. Kind A is `operation-order` with a second layer of group. Kind B is a description-to-expression followed by a multidigit product (5.NBT.B.5), which is why that standard is linked. Both get a full example.

**Standard.** 5.OA.A.1 ("parentheses, brackets"), 5.OA.A.2 ("write simple expressions that record calculations"), 5.NBT.B.5 ("multiply multi-digit whole numbers").

**Foundations first.** Brackets are a bigger group around a group: the same meaning, drawn bigger so the eye can tell them apart. The innermost group, the one with nothing inside it, goes first; its value replaces it; then the next group out. Boxes nest: inner box drawn first.

**Example A.** 4 + [(9 × 8) − 42] ÷ 5. (1) Inner box on (9 × 8): 72. 4 + [72 − 42] ÷ 5. (2) Box the bracket: 30. 4 + 30 ÷ 5. (3) Name the whole: "four more than a quotient"; the ÷ 5 waited for the bracket and still comes before the plus. 30 ÷ 5 = 6. (4) 4 + 6 = 10.

**Quick case** (two groups at the same depth): [(4 + 5) × (12 − 6)] − 6 × 3. Both inner groups collapse, 9 × 6; the bracket, 54; then 6 × 3 = 18 before the minus; 36. One line: "Groups at the same depth do not touch each other."

**Example B.** "Find the product of 711 and the difference of 49 and 9." (1) Gold on "product of ___ and ___": two things multiplied. The first is 711. The second is "the difference of 49 and 9": written as the group (49 − 9), first-named number first. Expression: 711 × (49 − 9). (2) Group first: 40. (3) 711 × 40 is 711 × 4 tens: 711 × 4 = 2,844 stacked; 2,844 tens is 28,440 (family 2's shift, one line). (4) 28,440.

**Fast.** Innermost group first, then outward; for words, the group's name tells its operation and the first-named number comes first.

**Sense.** A: four plus a small quotient. B: about 700 × 40 = 28,000.

**Check.** A: the trap reading gives a non-whole answer (4 + 30 = 34, 34 ÷ 5 = 6.8), which is itself a warning. B: 28,440 ÷ 40 = 711, or the estimate.

**Line.** "Innermost first. Brackets are just a bigger group."

**Trap.** B: 711 × 49, then take away 9: multiplying before the difference is made. The box on "the difference" as one number, written before the times sign is touched.

**Kit.** Nested `box`; `stack` has no `×` operation (family 7 owns the multiplication algorithm), so this lesson multiplies by 4 then by ten with the `places`-style shift; note the `×` stack as a kit wish for family 7.

**Writer.** Cheaper model: both kinds are fixed motions and every line is checkable.

### `equal-expression-select` — Select the two expressions with the same value.

Note first: tagged 5.OA.A.1 but it tests properties of operations (division over a difference, the distributive property, reordering a sum), which is 6.EE.A.3 thinking; at grade 5 the sure method is "collapse each to a number", and the rectangle is the why. The variant 6 − 8 + 20 cannot be run left to right by a fifth grader (6 − 8 is negative); it should read 6 + 20 − 8, or move to grade 6.

**Standard.** 5.OA.A.1 as tagged; the thinking is 6.EE.A.3.

**Foundations first.** Two expressions have the same value when they make the same number. The sure way to know: run both. The way to see why, for a product of a difference: the rectangle, family 7's picture, drawn to scale.

**Read.** "Select the 2" (gold): exactly two, and both must equal the given expression's value. At a glance: compute the given first, then every choice must hit that number.

**The example.** 9 × (15 − 6), five choices shown. (1) Collapse the given: 9 × 9 = 81. Every right answer is 81. (2) Rule out by size in one beat: (15 − 6) + 9 adds instead of multiplying, 18; 9 × 15 + 9 × 6 is bigger than 9 × 15, so bigger than 81; both dim. (3) The three left, collapsed each: 9 × 15 − 6 = 129; 9 × 15 − 9 × 6 = 135 − 54 = 81; (15 − 6) × 9 = 81. (4) Why, on the rectangle: 9 tall, 15 wide, unit ticks; a rust strip 6 wide at the right end, "taken away"; what is left is 9 by 9. Nine fifteens minus nine sixes. The decoy took away one 6, not nine: a rust strip 1 wide by 6 tall is drawn beside it. (5) The rectangle turned a quarter turn is (15 − 6) × 9: the same rectangle.

**Quick second case** (division): (21 − 12) ÷ 3 = 3; 21 ÷ 3 − 12 ÷ 3 = 7 − 4 = 3, and (21 ÷ 3) − (12 ÷ 3) is the same thing with the parentheses the order already gave it. The bar: 21 long, 12 rust at the end, the 9 left cut into three shares; each share is a third of 21 less a third of 12. The addition variant is covered by the method; its one new idea is one line: "adding 6 to a thing or adding the thing to 6 is the same sum."

**Fast.** Collapse the given to a number; collapse each choice; match.

**Sense.** The given is 81, a bit less than 9 × 15; any choice larger than 135 or near 18 is gone.

**Check.** Exactly two selected; each equals 81.

**Rule out.** Size: too big or too small to be 81 dims together with the reason said.

**Line.** "Same value: run both. Taking away a group of six means taking away every six."

**Trap.** 9 × 15 − 6: the group broken, one 6 removed instead of nine. The rust strip 9 tall against the strip 1 tall.

**Kit.** `area` numeric grid with the rust `strip` (family 4.2); the `bar` with a rust part and `partition` into 3.

**Writer.** Strongest writer: five claims that fail or hold for five different reasons, each needing the right picture in two beats.

### `expression-after-first-step` — Do only the first step; find the first wrong line.

Note first: a procedure fragment; the mistake-finding half is the valuable one and is the main example.

**Standard.** 5.OA.A.1; 5.NBT.B.7 for the decimal arithmetic in the first-step kind.

**Foundations first.** One step is one operation collapsed; everything else is copied exactly. Inside a group the three-line rule still holds: times before plus even inside parentheses. The group is one number: a minus in front of it acts on the whole number, never on just its first part. The picture is the `step` motion itself: one chunk gold, all else standing under itself.

**Example A** (first wrong line). The student's four lines shown as the learner sees them, choices shown. (1) Rule out at once: run the problem yourself. 46 − (8 + 2 × 3): inner times first, 46 − (8 + 6); the group, 46 − 14; 32. The student ended at 44, not 32, so "no line has a mistake" dims. (2) Compare line by line. Line 2, 46 − (8 + 6): matches ours; correct, even though it is where many look first. (3) Line 3, 38 + 6: the student did 46 − 8 and left the 6 outside. The group was one number, 14; you take away fourteen, not eight. Line 3 goes rust. Answer: Line 3. (4) Said once: "find the first line that differs from yours; everything after it is built on the mistake."

**Example B** (first step only). 27.4 − (1.7 + 2.5 × 8), choices shown. (1) The group is first, and inside it the times is first: box 2.5 × 8. (2) 2.5 × 8 is two and a half eights: 20. (3) Collapse, everything else copied: 27.4 − (1.7 + 20). (4) Decoys named as they dim: 27.4 − 21.7 did two steps; 25.7 + 2.5 × 8 did the subtraction first and lost the group; 27.4 − (1.7 + 200) slipped a place, 2.5 × 8 is not 25 × 8.

**Fast.** Do the work yourself first; then compare.

**Sense.** A: 46 minus about 14 is about 32, so 44 is wrong somewhere. B: the new line has exactly one operation fewer than the old one.

**Check.** A: finish the correct work; the first line that differs is the answer. B: cover the gold chunk and the rest of the two lines match symbol for symbol.

**Rule out.** A: if your final value matches the student's, "no mistake" is likely; if not, it dims first. B: a choice with no parentheses while the group is unfinished, or with two operations gone, dims together.

**Line.** "One step: one thing changes. The group is one number, and the minus takes all of it."

**Trap.** 46 − (8 + 6) → 38 + 6, and inside a group adding before multiplying (Line 2 of the 45 − (7 + 2 × 2) variant, 45 − 9 × 2). The collapse always lands the inner box on the times, and the group is made one number before the minus touches it.

**Kit.** `box`, `step`; the problem card must show the student's lines and let one line be marked rust (`highlight` on a problem line with `"ink": "warm"`).

**Writer.** Strongest writer: the lesson is about judging someone else's reasoning line by line, and the decoys are each a different misstep.

## Grade 6: variable-expressions (6.EE.A.2)

### `expression-or-equation` — A phrase or a sentence?

Note first: no standard id; one-line vocabulary. Keep the film under a minute.

**Foundations first.** An **expression** is a phrase: it names one number (7a, 4x + 8, "the product of 7 and a number"). An **equation** is a sentence: two expressions with an equals sign between, saying they name the same number. In words the equals sign is "is" or "equals". An equation can be false and is still an equation.

**Read.** "Equation" or "expression" (gold): which one are we hunting. At a glance: look for = or "is".

**The example.** "Which phrase describes an equation?" Four phrases; the word "is" in "a number divided by 2 is 31" goes gold and an equals sign is written under it: a sentence. The other three have no "is": phrases, dim together. **Quick second case** (symbols): "Which is an expression?" with n + 7 = 22, 5 + 9 = 10, 7a, 7x + 5 = 39. The three equals signs light; 7a has none. One beat: 5 + 9 = 10 is false, and still an equation.

**Fast.** Find the equals sign or the word "is".

**Check.** Read it aloud: a sentence can be true or false; a phrase cannot.

**Line.** "An expression names a number. An equation says two are the same."

**Trap.** Thinking a false statement is not an equation, or that a letter makes something an equation. The gold equals sign is the only test.

**Kit.** Nothing new.

**Writer.** Cheaper model.

### `label-expression-parts` — Name terms, coefficients, exponents, constants; sum or product?

Note first: the answer is a slot card (letters a to f); the lesson must show the app's card and its exact labels. The 2(x + 5) answer key gives two slots the same letter ("sum": b, "count": b); confirm that is intended.

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.2b: "Identify parts of an expression using mathematical terms (sum, term, product, factor, quotient, coefficient); view one or more parts of an expression as a single entity... describe the expression 2 (8 + 7) as a product of two factors; view (8 + 7) as both a single entity and a sum of two terms."

**Foundations first.** The words, each with its picture. **Terms** are the parts added. A **coefficient** is the number in front of a letter: the count. A **constant term** has no letter. An exponent counts the factors: x² is x · x. **Factors** are the parts multiplied. The last operation names the whole: added last, a sum; multiplied last, a product. The tile chart (x² | x | 1) with the count under each pile makes "the coefficient is the count" visible.

**Example A** (a sum). 7x² + 5x + 9 with its slot card. (1) Tiles fly from the expression into piles: seven x²-tiles (each the square on x), five x-tiles, nine unit squares; counts under the piles. (2) Three piles, three terms, joined by plus: the whole is a sum. (3) Label each slot as the card asks: the coefficient of x² is 7, the count of squares; the exponent 2 means x times x, written beside one square tile; the coefficient of x is 5; the constant term is 9, the pile with no letter. One line for the 5x + 4y + 3 variant: a different letter is a different kind of tile, with its own count.

**Example B** (a product). 5(k + 8) with its slot card. (1) A rectangle 5 tall and (k + 8) wide, to scale (k an unticked length, 8 ticked), no cut yet. (2) Its two sides are the two **factors**: 5, and the group. The whole is a product, because the last thing done is the multiplying. (3) The width is one length and also two pieces: box the group, name "one factor"; then the plus inside, name "a sum of two terms, k and 8". The standard's double sight, said as the whole point.

**Fast.** Split at the pluses and minuses outside parentheses for terms; the number in front is the coefficient; no letter, constant; if the outside is a times, the whole is a product.

**Check.** Every number and letter in the expression has exactly one name on the card; the names say the same thing the tiles or the rectangle show.

**Line.** "The coefficient is the count. The last thing you do is what it is."

**Trap.** Calling the exponent a coefficient or the constant a coefficient; calling 5(k + 8) a sum because it has a plus inside. The count under the pile, and the rectangle whose outside is a times.

**Kit.** `tiles` mode of `blocks` (family 4.3); `area` with terms, uncut, to scale; the slot card rendered as the learner sees it.

**Writer.** Cheaper model: two fixed pictures and vocabulary placed on them.

### `substitute-linear-expression` — Find the value when x is a number.

Note first: x = −11/2, −2, −5 and −6x are 7.NS material in a grade-6 type (the app tags 7.NS.A.3 itself). The main example is on grade; the signed cases are quick cases that name family 3's rule in one line.

**Standard.** 6.EE.A.2c: "Evaluate expressions at specific values of their variables... in the conventional order"; 7.NS.A.3 for the signed and fractional values.

**Foundations first.** A letter is a slot for a number, and the recipe is the same for every number. 4x means four times x, the times sign hidden: with x = 2 it is four 2s, a row of four cards each reading 2, bracket "4x = 8"; never "forty-two". To evaluate: drop the number into every slot, in parentheses, then run the three-line rule.

**Read.** "Find the value... when x = 2" (gold on the value): the answer is a number. At a glance: 4x is four 2s, 8; the answer is 8 less 7.

**The example.** 4x − 7 when x = 2. (1) Every x lights; 2 drops in with parentheses: 4(2) − 7. (2) The four cards of 2: 4(2) is 8. Collapse: 8 − 7. (3) 1.

**Quick cases**, each a different case the learner meets. −x + 3 at x = −5: −x is the opposite of x, one rust x; drop in: −(−5) + 3; the opposite of negative five is five; 5 + 3 = 8. 4x + 1 at x = −11/2: 4(−11/2) + 1; four halves of eleven is 22, and a positive times a negative is negative (family 3, one line): −22 + 1 = −21. The base case 5x + 0 at x = 0 is one line: five zeros is zero.

**Fast.** Drop in with parentheses; multiply; then add or subtract.

**Sense.** Four 2s take 7 is a small positive; 4 times a negative is negative, and adding 1 leaves it negative, so −21 has the right sign.

**Check.** Undo: 1 + 7 = 8, and 8 is four 2s. Or run it again in the same order.

**Line.** "A letter is a slot. Drop the number in, in parentheses; the recipe stays."

**Trap.** 42 − 7 = 35, reading 4x with 2 as a two-digit number; dropping the sign of a negative input; −x at x = −5 as −5. The parentheses on every drop-in make 4(2) unreadable as forty-two, and the rust x is drawn before the number lands in it.

**Kit.** `substitute` (family 4.1); the copies picture from `grid`.

**Writer.** Cheaper model: one motion, cases fixed here, every line checked by `--dry`.
