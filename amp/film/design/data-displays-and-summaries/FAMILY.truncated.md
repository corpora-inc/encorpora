# Data displays and summaries, K to A2: supplementary design notes

These notes provide additional teaching examples and component proposals. Use [FAMILY.md](FAMILY.md) for the complete family plan.

Eighteen skills, 57 problem types, from sorting shapes to the normal curve. Planning snapshot: the catalog and templates snapshot 2026-11-09; every example named below is one the app generates. This design covers the idea, the pictures, how lessons go, the kit, the seam, what is misfiled, the pilot. Per-type concepts use these foundations and pictures.

Nothing here is a rule list. It is one idea, one picture that grows with the child, and the habits that turn every problem in the family into careful reading.

---

## 1. The idea underneath the whole family

Data is a pile of things, and each thing has something written on it: a kind (dog, oak, swimming) or a number (5 centimeters, 3 goals, 13.2 seconds). Every display in this family, from a kindergarten sorting mat to a normal curve, is that same pile arranged so one question can be answered by looking. Every summary (a total, "how many more", the mean, the median, the range, the standard deviation) is one number asked of the pile.

The child who owns this family can go both ways: take the things and make the display, and look at the display and see the things in it. Once that is true, every problem here is reading: what is it asking, and where in the picture is that? This applies from kindergarten to Algebra 2.

The foundations, in the order they are built. Each later lesson stands on the ones above and names them in one line.

1. **One mark is one thing.** (K to 1) A shape on the mat, a picture in a row, a tally stroke, a dot: each stands for exactly one thing. Count the marks and you have counted the things. All the marks together are all the things, so the groups add up to the total; the display never loses a thing or invents one.

2. **Sort, then count; line up, then compare.** (K to 1) Sorting puts things of one kind together, so counting a kind is counting a row. Rows that start on the same line can be compared before counting: the longer row has more. "How many more" is not the longer row; it is the *overhang*, the part of the longer row that sticks out past the shorter one. (From family 6: the compare bar; a difference is a gap.)

3. **A number line sorts numbers; a line plot is a number line with the things stacked on it.** (2) When the things are measurements, their kinds are numbers, and numbers have an order and a distance, so they live on a number line. Each measurement is one mark above its number. A stack answers two different questions: the number under it says *how big* (5 centimeters); the marks in it say *how many* (3 ribbons). Keeping those two apart is most of grade 2. (From P3, the number line.)

4. **A mark can stand for more than one thing, and the key says how many; a bar is read against its scale.** (3 to 4) Five students fuse into one symbol worth 5 (the same move as ten squares becoming a rod). A bar's height is read where its top meets the axis, never by counting squares; where the top falls between two lines, the step between lines is the scale divided by the lines between labels. (From family 2: unitizing, skip counting.)

5. **The line can be cut into halves, quarters and eighths, and the line does the arithmetic.** (3 to 5) A fraction line plot is the same number line with finer ticks. The difference between two marks is the hops between them (seven quarter-hops is 7/4, which is 1 3/4). Pooling everything and sharing it out equally is *leveling*, and the level is the number grade 6 will call the mean. (From family 9: fractions on the line, like-denominator sums; from family 7: fair shares.)

6. **A statistical question expects different answers, and the answers make a distribution.** (6) "How tall am I?" has one answer and makes one dot. "How tall are the students in my class?" makes a dot plot. The shape the dots make has a center, a spread and a shape, and the rest of the family is ways of saying each with one number.

7. **Center: three questions of one pile.** (6) The *mean* is the fair share: level the stacks, or find the point where the plot balances. The *median* is the middle one in order: pair off from both ends until one (or two) are left. The *mode* is the tallest stack. They are different questions and can give different answers for the same dots.

8. **Spread: how far from the middle.** (6 to 7) The *range* is the whole stretch, greatest minus least. The *interquartile range* is the stretch of the middle half, the box. The *mean absolute deviation* is the average length of the sticks from each dot to the mean. (From family 3: distance on the line is absolute value; subtracting a negative.)

9. **Grouping trades detail for shape.** (6) A frequency table counts each stack. A histogram pushes the dots into equal-width bins and keeps only the counts. A box plot keeps only five numbers. A stem-and-leaf keeps every value and still shows the shape; turned on its side it *is* a histogram with bins of ten. Each display answers some questions well and others not at all, so "which display" means "which questions".

10. **The mean follows the tail; the median stays.** (6 to A1) One far dot drags the balance point toward itself and leaves the middle dot where it was. So a skewed pile is summarized by median and IQR, a symmetric one by mean and standard deviation (the MAD's cousin: a typical distance from the mean, compared in this app, never computed).

11. **Two groups compare on one scale, and the gap between centers is measured in spreads.** (7 to A1) Two plots on one number line; the difference of the means laid off with the MAD as a ruler: "two MADs apart, so the plots barely overlap." Centers read from the medians, spreads from the boxes.

12. **The normal curve: the standard deviation is the ruler.** (A2) A smooth histogram with the mean at its center and a tick every standard deviation; 34, 13.5 and 2.35 percent in the bands on each side; a z-score counts rulers from the mean.

**Taken from other families**, named in a line when used and never re-taught: counting and the total (family 1); part-whole and the compare gap (family 6); unitizing and skip counting (family 2); the number line, its ticks and its step (P3); fractions on the line and like-denominator addition (family 9); division as fair sharing (family 7); percent of a quantity (family 10, for `categorical-summary`); ordering rationals and distance as absolute value (family 3); the missing-addend equation (family 11, for a value missing from a known mean). **Given to**: family 33 (a scatter dot is one thing with two numbers; a two-way table is two sorts at once) and family 34 (expected value is the leveled mean of the outcomes).

## 2. The visual language

One rule makes the whole system: **every mark is one thing, drawn the same size as every other, so the display is to scale by construction.** A stack of four is twice as tall as a stack of two; a row of six symbols is twice as long as a row of three; a bar is exactly its count against its axis. Every picture here is one a child can sketch on scratch paper: a line, ticks, marks.

### The shapes

| Picture | What it is | Grades | Scale and labels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The thing** | In K to 3, an icon of the actual object (dog, leaf, ribbon, apple, triangle), all the same size; on numerical plots, the mark the app's line plot uses (an X or a dot); from grade 4, a *chip* (a number in a small rounded card) when the data arrive as a list | K to A1 | No mark stands for more than one thing unless a key says so |
| **Category rows** (the picture graph) | Rows of icons on a shared left edge; the category name at the left; a live count at each row's end; the total above | K to 3 | Equal icons, equal gaps: a longer row is more |
| **Tally** | The same count as strokes in fives, the fifth crossing | 1 | Each stroke one thing |
| **The bar graph** | A box drawn around each row (or column) of icons; the icons fade; an axis beside the bars with a tick at every unit and a number every scale step | 1 to 4 | Bar length is count times unit, exactly; title, axis numbers, category names; the scale said on the axis ("each line is 10 points") |
| **The key** | One symbol = n things, "= 5 students" beside it; a half symbol | 3 | Made once, on screen, by fusing n icons into one |
| **The line plot / dot plot** | A number line (P3) with ticks and numbers; marks stacked straight above their ticks in equal columns; the unit under the line; a title | 2 to A1 | Tick spacing to scale (a half-inch tick halfway between inch ticks); every tick labeled as the problem labels it (1 2/4 when the problem writes 1 2/4) |
| **The frequency table** | Two columns, *value* and *frequency* (how many times), one row per value including zeros, a totals row; a third column, value × frequency, when a mean is wanted | 2 to 6 | The kit's `table` |
| **The histogram** | The dot plot with its dots pushed into bins of equal width; one bar per bin; bars touching, because the axis is a number line | 6 | Bar height is the count; bin bounds on the axis |
| **The stem-and-leaf** | Stems in a column, leaves in rows, leaves sorted, rows left-aligned, the key always shown | 6 | One leaf one thing; a longer row is more |
| **The box plot** | Whiskers, box, median line on the number line, drawn *above* the dot plot it summarizes whenever the dots exist | 6 to A1 | Positions to scale; the five numbers labeled; "about 25%" in each piece the first time |
| **The mean's marker** | A gold triangle (the fulcrum) under the line at the mean; or, when leveling, the gold level line across the value stacks | 5 to A1 | "mean = 5" |
| **Value stacks** | One bar of unit squares per thing, its height the thing's value, side by side (P2's squares; the reference `mean-data` picture); for liquid, beakers filled to their amounts | 5 to 6 | Only when values are small enough to draw |
| **Deviation sticks** | Horizontal gold sticks from each dot to the mean line, to scale | 6 to 7 | |
| **Two groups** | Two dot plots, or two box plots, stacked on one shared number line | 7 to A1 | One scale, or no comparison |
| **The normal curve** | The bell over an axis, the mean at the center, a tick every standard deviation, the bands labeled 34, 13.5, 2.35, 0.15 | A2 | Equal tick spacing: the ruler |

A sorted row of chips with the middle one circled is a picture too; it is what the learner writes on scratch paper for a median or a quartile.

### Color means one thing each

- **teal**: the data (every icon, mark, bar, leaf, chip) and the first of two groups.
- **rust**: the second group; a thing being added, removed or corrected.
- **gold**: the one thing being read now: the word in the question that says what is asked, the stack being counted, the overhang, the guide line from a bar's top to the axis, the "at least" region, the middle chip once found, the mean's fulcrum, the deviation sticks. One gold thing at a time.
- **blue**: the five-number summary, always: the median marker, the quartiles, the box. A child who met blue as "the middle one in order" in grade 6 reads an A1 box plot by color. When mean and median must both show, the median is blue and the fulcrum is gold.
- **muted**: things paired off and set aside; dots that have become a bar; bins outside the asked range; the part of a bar not asked about; ruled-out choices.

### The motions (each one proves a foundation)

- **Sort** (F1, F2): icons slide from a jumble into rows on a common left edge; each row's count ticks as an icon lands; the total label above never changes. Sorting rearranges; it does not change the count.
- **Outline** (F2): a box is drawn around each row; the icons fade; the box is the bar. The bar's length is the count.
- **Overhang** (F2): two bars aligned at the base; the part of the longer past the shorter goes gold with its count. "How many more" is the gap, not the longer bar.
- **Fuse** (F4): five icons slide together and become one symbol with "= 5" beside it; a row of symbols counts 5, 10, 15; a half symbol counts half. The lesson says once that this is the rod from place value.
- **Guide** (F4): a dashed gold line from a bar's top straight to the axis; the number there lights. If the top is between two lines, the axis zooms and the sub-steps are counted ("two lines between 50 and 100: each line is 25"). Read the scale, never the squares.
- **Measure** (F3): a ribbon laid against the app's ruler, read to the nearest unit (or half, or quarter), the reading written into a list. Honors 2.MD.D.9 and 3.MD.B.4 on screen even though the app never asks the learner to measure.
- **Drop** (F3): each value in the list goes gold in turn and its mark falls onto the line above its number and lands on the stack; a placed-count ticks; when the list is done it equals the list's length. The plot is the list rearranged. **Lift** is the reverse: a mark rises and its value is read off the tick below. Used for every "which list matches" and every total.
- **Shade** (F3): for "at least 3", the line from 3 rightward is shaded gold with a filled end at 3 and the marks inside are counted; "more than 3" starts at a hollow end. The same end marks as rounding's catchment.
- **Hop** (F5): from the shortest mark to the longest, arcs along the line, one per tick, counted in the plot's unit: "one fourth, two fourths ... seven fourths." The difference is read on the line as on a ruler.
- **Pour, or level** (F5, F7): each thing becomes a bar of its value (a beaker filled to its amount, or a stack of unit squares); water or squares move from the taller to the shorter until all are one height; the total label never changes; the level is the mean. When values are too large to draw as squares, the **balance** does the work: a gold triangle slides under the number line until the plot balances on it. The lesson says once that these are the same number, and shows why with sticks: the sticks to the left of the triangle have the same total length as the sticks to the right.
- **Pair off** (F7): on the marks (or the sorted chips), the leftmost and rightmost dim together, then the next pair, inward, until one is left (gold, then blue: the median) or two are left (a blue marker halfway between them). The middle in order, with as many on each side.
- **Halve** (F8): the marks left of the median pair off to their own middle, then those to the right: two blue quartiles. The box is drawn between them above the dots, whiskers out to the first and last mark. The box is the middle half; each piece holds about a quarter of the marks.
- **Stretch** (F8): a bracket from the first mark to the last, its length written on it. The range.
- **Sticks** (F8): a gold vertical at the mean; from each dot a horizontal stick to it; the sticks lift and lay end to end in a row with the total written; the row is cut into as many equal pieces as there are dots; one piece is the MAD. The average distance from the mean, and why "absolute": a stick has a length, not a direction.
- **Bin** (F9): vertical bin lines drop onto the dot plot at equal spacing; the dots in each bin slide together into a column that becomes a bar; the dots fade. The histogram counts dots in ranges and forgets their exact values.
- **Split and turn** (F9): a chip splits into stem and leaf; the leaf flies to its stem's row; when all have flown, each row's leaves slide into order and the key appears. Then the whole plot turns a quarter turn and each row stands up as a bar: a histogram with bins of ten that kept its values.
- **Pull** (F10): one dot slides far to the right; the gold fulcrum follows; the blue median does not move (or moves half a step). The mean follows the tail.
- **Align and rule** (F11): two plots slide onto one shared line; a stick the length of the MAD is laid from one mean toward the other and counted: "one, two: two MADs." A gap means something only in units of spread.
- **Band** (F12): the normal curve's bands shade one at a time from the mean outward, each with its percent; an asked region sums the bands it covers; a z-score steps the ruler from the mean to the value.

### Deliberately not in the picture system

Pies for anything a bar or a line can carry (the circle graph is only a named choice in `best-display-choice`); bars not drawn to their count; marks of unequal size; a display presented already built; a formula first for mean, median or MAD. The display is built in front of the learner, from the things, the first time it appears at a grade.

## 3. How lessons in this family should go

### Foundations first, built live

Every lesson opens by building the display it will read, from the things, in twenty to forty seconds: the sort, the drop, the outline, the bin, the pair-off. Only the foundation of the lesson's own grade is built; earlier ones are named in a line ("a line plot is a number line, the one you counted on"). The lesson says what the display is *for* before any question ("so we can see at once which length is most common"). Then the problem, read exactly as the app shows it, with every choice.

Plain words before the word: "the middle one when they are in order" before *median*; "how many times it appears" before *frequency*; "how far from the mean, on average" before *mean absolute deviation*. At most two `term` cards; the rest in passing. In K to 2 the things are real things (dogs, leaves, ribbons, apples), never "counters", and nothing is struck out.

### The habits, in this family's terms

1. **Read.** The word that says what is asked goes gold, and it tells the learner where in the picture to look:
   - "how many" plus a kind or a value: a count; find that row or stack.
   - "how many more / fewer": a difference of two counts; the overhang.
   - "altogether / in all / how many were measured": the total; every stack.
   - "at least / or more": that value and everything to its right; "more than": strictly right; "less than": left.
   - "longest / shortest / greatest / least": the end marks; a value, not a count.
   - "median": order first. "mean": share equally. "mode": the tallest stack. "range": the stretch. "interquartile range": the box.
   - "typical" when one dot is far from the rest: the median.
   - "which list / table / plot matches": check counts, not values.

   Then one beat says what the answer must look like: a count is a whole number no bigger than the total; a difference is smaller than the larger count; a value is one of the tick labels; a mean sits between the least and the greatest and need not be a data value; a median is a data value or halfway between two; a mode is a value, never a count; a range is never negative; a box-plot percent is 0, 25, 50, 75 or 100; a normal percent is a sum of 34, 13.5, 2.35 and 0.15 (or 50 plus or minus those).

2. **At a glance.** What is known with no work: the tallest stack; which row is longer; that the dots sit at only three values, so the mean is value times count; that one dot is far away, so the mean will be pulled; that the choices agree everywhere but one value, so only that stack need be counted.

3. **The sure fast way.** Taught as the method, with the picture that explains it, only where it always works:
   - Matching a list, table or plot to a display: find the one value where the choices differ and count that stack; check the total against the stated count. Always works, because the choices differ somewhere.
   - A scaled bar that ends between two lines: the step between lines is the scale divided by the lines between labels; read up from the lower label.
   - The mean of a dot plot or a frequency table: value times count for each stack, summed, divided by the total count. Always right, and the only way for a table.
   - A missing value: mean times count is the total; subtract the known values.
   - The median of a dot plot: pair off from the ends; no list needed. Of a frequency table: add frequencies from the bottom until half the total is passed.
   - A change in the mean when one value changes: the change in the total divided by the count. A change in the median: only if the middle mark moves; look at which side of the median the changed value sits.
   - The 68-95-99.7 rule: sketch the bell with seven ticks, write 34, 13.5, 2.35 in the bands, shade the asked region, add.

   A symmetric plot's mean is its center of symmetry; that is offered as a *sense check*, not a method, because symmetry judged by eye fails.

4. **Sense.** Before the answer is boxed: the right kind (a count, a length in the unit, a percent); the right size (a mean between the ends; a difference smaller than either count; a share smaller than the total); and it matches the picture (as many marks on each side of the median; the fulcrum under the heavy side).

5. **Check.** After: the category counts add to the total of things; the marks equal the list's length; mean times count returns the total; least plus range returns the greatest; Q1 < median < Q3; for a built plot, lift one mark and find it in the list; for a chosen statement, read each clause against the picture.

6. **Rule out.** Choices that cannot be right dim together, with the reason: by *total* (a list with the wrong number of values; a frequency table whose counts do not sum to the stated 11 students; a stem-and-leaf with the wrong number of leaves); by *kind* (a count offered where a value is asked); by *size* (a difference bigger than a count; a mean outside the data; a median that is neither a data value nor a midpoint); by *the key* (the choice that ignored the key is exactly the key times too small); by *the percent rule* (a box-plot share that is not a multiple of 25); by *shape* (a statement whose first clause is false dies whatever its second says). The decoys in these types are the misconceptions below, so the lesson names them.

For the reading-heavy types a cast helps: the student says the real first misread aloud ("the mode is 7") and the teacher untangles it on the picture, as the sinusoid reference does.

### The realistic misconceptions, and the picture that ends each

Each of these is what children actually write.

- **Answering the longer count for "how many more".** The overhang is the only gold thing on screen.
- **Counting symbols as one each under a key of 5; a half symbol as 1.** The fuse made the symbol in front of them; the row counts 5, 10, 15 aloud.
- **Reading a bar's height as grid squares when the axis counts by 10.** The guide line goes to the axis and the axis number lights; squares are never counted.
- **Confusing how many with how big on a line plot:** "5" (the length) for "how many ribbons are 5 cm"; the longest length for "how many were measured"; ticks counted as if they were marks. One stack carries both labels once, "3 ribbons" above and "5 cm" below, and the lesson taps each.
- **"At least 3" without the 3; "more than 3" with it.** The shaded stretch starts at a filled or a hollow end.
- **A median from the unordered list, or from the middle of the axis instead of the middle mark; for an even count, one of the two middles instead of halfway.** The chips slide into order first; pair off; the blue marker lands between two.
- **The mode given as a frequency ("the mode is 7" when 7 is how many read 6 books).** The tallest stack is gold; the answer is the number under it.
- **The mean of a frequency table by averaging the value column, or dividing by the number of rows.** The dots are shown under the table: 19 dots, not 5 rows; value times count is one stack's total.
- **The range reversed, or a negative least added instead of subtracted (−1.3 − (−8.3) is 7; children write −9.6 or 9.6).** The stretch bracket is a distance counted on the line.
- **A stem-and-leaf misread against its key (7|8 as 78 when the key says 7.8); a repeated leaf counted once; a stem with no leaves mishandled.** The key is read first, aloud, every time; leaves drop from the list one by one.
- **A histogram bar's height taken as a data value; "30 to 34" confused with "up to 35".** The bin motion shows the dots becoming the bar; the bounds are on the axis.
- **A longer piece of a box plot read as "more data there".** The box sits above the dots: each piece holds about a quarter; a long piece is a spread-out quarter.
- **The range read as the box's width; the IQR as the whiskers' span.** Stretch (gold) and box (blue) are drawn separately.
- **MAD without the absolute value (signed deviations sum to zero), or divided by the number of distinct values.** The sticks have length; they lay end to end; the row is cut into as many pieces as there are dots.
- **The median counted into both halves when the problem says to leave it out.** The lesson follows the problem's stated convention and says so once; the median chip steps aside before the halves pair off.
- **"Bigger numbers mean a bigger standard deviation."** A second case where the larger numbers are the tighter set; the gaps between chips are what the sticks measure.
- **The 68% applied to one side; "between 1 and 2 standard deviations" as 95%.** Bands shade one at a time from the mean, 34 then 13.5, on each side.
- **A fact question taken for a statistical one because its answer is a number ("How many students are in my class?").** One speech bubble, one dot. Different answers, a dot plot.

### The lines worth remembering

One per foundation, said once when it applies and put in the recap. "One dot is one thing." "Sort first; the longest row wins; how many more is the overhang." "Under the dot is how big; the stack is how many." "Read the key first; the bar's top meets the scale." "The line does the subtraction." "A statistical question makes a dot plot; a fact question makes one dot." "The mean is the fair share; the median is the middle one in order; the mode is the tallest stack." "The range is the whole stretch; the box is the middle half; the MAD is the average stick." "A histogram forgets the values; a stem-and-leaf keeps them." "The mean follows the tail; the median stays." "Same scale, or no comparison; measure the gap in MADs." "The standard deviation is the ruler: 34, 13.5, 2.35."

### Pacing

Patient pacing: one idea per beat; the motion finishes before the voice moves on; a pause after each key idea; anything written stays until read. Grade 6 is the dense year (six new words); each gets its own built picture and its own lesson opening, never a list of definitions. Every multiple-choice problem shows every choice; where the choices are pictures (four dot plots, four stem-and-leaf plots, four tables), they are drawn small, lettered, and dim together.

## 4. Tools to build

**What exists:** the board; `numberline` (specified: drop, arcs, half, catch, zoom); `grid`, `counters`, `count`, `label @n` (the machinery behind `mean-data`'s leveling); `table` with named cells, `show`, `highlight`, `given`; `bar` (the percent bar); `axes` with `y: false` (a bare number line); `dot`, `seg`, `dim` (bracket), `label`, `move`, `glide`, `turn`, `highlight`; the app's own displays through `scene` (bar and picture graphs, line plot, histogram, box plots, ruler) with `appear`, `highlight`, `count`, `move`; `dim` for choices (specified). The app's displays can be shown and lit; they cannot be built, sorted, paired, leveled or binned. This family needs a data kit (`film/data.ts`) whose components are built *from the things* and derive from one another.

### 4.1 `things`: objects that sort into a display (K to 3)

```json
{ "things": { "id": "t", "icons": ["dog", "cat", "bird"],
  "items": ["dog", "cat", "dog", "bird", "dog", "cat"], "layout": "pile", "total": true } }
```

- Icons from a small set (animals, fruit, leaves, shapes, ribbons, students) or a plain mark; `items` in the order they will sort; `layout` `pile` or `rows`.
- Actions: `sort` (into rows on a common left edge, one per `at` word or all on a `dur`; counts tick; `total` stays), `tally` (a row's count redrawn as strokes in fives), `outline` (each row boxed, icons fade: the part is now a `bargraph`), `fuse` (`n` icons into one symbol with "= n"), `overhang` (the extra of the longer row gold with its count), `count` (tick one icon at a time on words).
- Names: `t.row.dog`, `t.row.dog.3`, `t.count.dog`, `t.total`, `t.key`.

### 4.2 `bargraph`: categories against a scale (1 to 4)

```json
{ "bargraph": { "id": "g", "cats": ["Oak", "Pine", "Elm"], "values": [7, 9, 3],
  "scale": 1, "lines": 1, "unit": "trees", "orient": "vertical", "title": "Trees in the park" } }
```

- Bars exactly to `values`; a tick every `lines` units, a number every `scale`; the kit draws the axis from `scale`, so labels and scale cannot disagree. Picture-graph form: `"symbol": "student", "key": 5` draws rows of symbols with half symbols and the key card.
- Actions: `guide` (dashed gold line from a bar's top to the axis; the number lights), `zoom` (the axis between two labels enlarged with its sub-steps), `read` (the bar's value written at its top), `overhang`, `show`/`highlight` of any bar or label.
- Names: `g.bar.Pine`, `g.axis.50`, `g.label.Pine`, `g.key`.

### 4.3 `dotplot`: the number line with the things stacked (2 to A1)

```json
{ "dotplot": { "id": "p", "from": 1, "to": 3, "step": 0.25, "labels": "quarters",
  "data": [1.25, 1.5, 1.5, 2, 2.75], "unit": "in", "title": "Lengths of leaves", "list": true } }
```

- Built on `numberline`. Ticks every `step`; `labels` as the problem writes them (`whole`, `halves`, `quarters` as 1 2/4, `eighths`, `decimal`). Marks of one size stacked straight above their ticks. `list: true` draws the data as chips beside the plot for `drop`. Two groups: `rows: [{ "id": "a", "data": [...] }, { "id": "b", ... }]` on one line, teal and rust.
- Actions: `drop` (the next chip goes gold and its mark falls and lands; `"n": 3` or one per `at` word; a placed-count label), `lift` (a mark rises, its value reads below), `shade` (`from`, `to`, closed or open ends; marks inside gold; a count), `stretch` (bracket first to last, length written), `hop` (arcs from one mark to another counted in the unit), `pair` (dim the outer pair; repeat on words; `"all": true` runs inward; the median marked blue, midpoint when two remain), `halve` (each side pairs to its quartile), `box` (the box plot above the dots; `"percents": true` writes "about 25%" in each piece), `mean` (the gold fulcrum slides to the mean; `"line": true` adds the vertical), `sticks` (dot to mean line), `lay` (sticks fly to a row end to end, total written, cut into `n` pieces, one labeled), `bars` (each dot becomes a bar of its value in a `stacks` beside the plot), `bin` (`width`, `start`: bin lines drop, dots gather into bars, dots fade: the part is now a `histogram`), `pull` (one dot glides to a new value; fulcrum and blue median update live), `move` (one dot to a corrected value, rust while moving).
- Names: `p.dot.4` (fourth in data order), `p.at.2` (the stack at 2), `p.at.2.3`, `p.tick.2`, `p.chip.4`, `p.median`, `p.q1`, `p.q3`, `p.box`, `p.mean`, `p.stick.4`, `p.range`, `p.row.a`, `p.bin.2`.

### 4.4 `list`: chips that sort, pair and split (4 to A1)

```json
{ "list": { "id": "L", "values": [13.2, 11.7, 14.6, 13.6, 13.2, 10.5], "unit": "s" } }
```

Chips in a row in the figure (the board keeps the arithmetic). Actions: `sort` (chips slide into order, least first), `pair` (outer chips dim, inward), `middle` (the middle chip, or the gap between two with the midpoint written, gold then blue), `halve` (a cut at the median; each half's middle blue), `sum` (chips stack into a total). Names `L.chip.3`, `L.median`, `L.q1`, `L.q3`. The dotplot's `list: true` is this component.

### 4.5 `stacks`: value bars that level (5 to 6)

Regularizes the ad hoc `mean-data` picture: one bar of unit squares per value (or a beaker with liquid, fractional heights, for `fraction-plot-equal-share`), `level` moves squares from the tallest to the shortest until equal, a `total` label that never changes, the gold level line labeled "mean". Fed by `dotplot.bars`.

### 4.6 `stemleaf`, `histogram`, `boxplot`: the grouped displays (6 to A1)

- `stemleaf`: `data`, `stem` (`tens`, or `ones` with a decimal key), `key`. Actions: `split` (a chip splits; the leaf flies to its row), `sort` (a row's leaves into order), `key` (the card), `turn` (quarter turn into a histogram with bins of ten), `highlight`. Names `s.row.4`, `s.leaf.4.2`, `s.key`. Four small plots for a select type are four instances in one figure.
- `histogram`: standalone from `bins` and `counts` when no dot plot precedes it; `highlight` bins; `sum` over several bins; bars touch.
- `boxplot`: standalone from five numbers when no dots exist; two on one line; `quarters` (percents in the pieces), `iqr` (blue bracket under the box), `range` (gold bracket whisker to whisker), `median` (the line lit). Names `bx.min`, `bx.q1`, `bx.median`, `bx.q3`, `bx.max`, `bx.box`.

### 4.7 `normal`: the curve and the ruler (A2)

`mean`, `sd`; ticks at every sd with their values; `band` (shade the next band outward with its percent, `"side": "both"`), `region` (shade `from`/`to` or `above`/`below`, summing bands or showing a table readout), `z` (a ruler stepping from the mean to a value, counting). Names `nm.band.1r`, `nm.tick.2l`, `nm.region`.

### 4.8 Extensions to what exists

- `table`: a `frequency` form (`values`, `counts`, the column named as the problem names it, a totals row); a `products` column (value × frequency) that `fill`s one row at a time; `fill` from a `dotplot` (each stack's count drops into its cell); a `tally` cell type.
- The problem card: **picture choices**. A `problem` whose `choices` are small drawn displays (dot plots, stem-and-leaf plots, tables), lettered A to D in a row or grid, dimmable together. `line-plot-data-match`, `stem-leaf-match-select`, `stem-leaf-construct`, `frequency-table-build`, `choose-the-plot` and `table-fits-description` need it; the app's text descriptions of plots ("Marks: 1 has 1, ...") must never be the choice on screen.
- The app's ruler scene: a `measure` action that lays an object against the ruler and reads it to the nearest unit, half or quarter, writing the reading.
- `--dry`: a note when a `say` names a scale ("each line is 10") that differs from the `bargraph`'s `scale`.

### Build order

1. `dotplot` with `drop`, `lift`, `shade`, `stretch`, `pair`, `halve`, `box`, `mean`: the spine, used from grade 2 to A1 by about 25 of the 57 types. Stands on `numberline`.
2. `things` and `bargraph` with `sort`, `outline`, `fuse`, `guide`, `overhang`: K to 4, 11 types.
3. `table` frequency form and `fill`; picture choices in the problem card.
4. `list`, `stacks` (regularized), `dotplot.bars`, `hop`.
5. `stemleaf`, `histogram`, `boxplot` standalone and two-row.
6. `sticks`, `lay`, `pull`, two `rows` with the MAD ruler.
7. `normal`.

Each piece is built to the depth its pilot lesson asks and extended by the next; a lesson that needs a motion not here is a `KAIZEN.md` finding, never a reason to force an existing tool.

## 5. Who should write what (the seam)

This division of work depends on a strong concept, a kit that fits, then a cheaper writer, then a strong review. In this family the line falls on whether a lesson's worth lies in a *motion that defines*, in *reading*, or in a *procedure on one component*.

**Strongest writer.**
- Where a display or summary is built for the first time at its grade and the motion is the proof: `picture-graph-questions` (sort, outline, overhang), `line-plot-whole` (measure, drop, how many against how big), `scaled-picture-graph` (fuse), `fraction-line-plot` (the finer line), `fraction-plot-equal-share` (pour), `frequency-table-build` (the table from the dots), `histogram-read` (bin), `box-plot-read` (pair, halve, box), `dot-plot-mad` (sticks), `stem-leaf-read` (the key), `normal-model-rule` (bands). A weak author describes these motions instead of staging them.
- Mixed types that teach reading, where there is no recipe and the one example must be chosen to expose the trap: `tally-and-t-chart`, `table-fits-description`, `data-list-for-display`, `choose-the-plot` (three kinds under one name), `best-display-choice`, `measure-meaning-choice`, `mcas-statistical-question-select`, `statistical-question-choice`, `dot-plot-statement-select`, `frequency-table-center` (table, weights, changed list), `line-plot-center-range` (three measures), `dot-plot-arithmetic`, `typical-value-choice`, `outlier-center-shift`, `skewed-box-compare`, `compare-distributions-choice`, `standard-deviation-compare`. Their quality is in the reasoning, which no checker sees.

**A capable cheaper model, from a written concept, once the kit is complete.** One method, one component, the picture already defined by an earlier lesson in the family, `--dry` and the math check catching most slips: `sort-shapes-count`, `sort-shapes-most`, `bar-chart-total`, `bar-chart-difference`, `scaled-bar-graph`, `scaled-bar-grade4`, `staar-dot-plot-threshold`, `line-plot-build-slots`, `line-plot-data-match`, `fraction-plot-difference`, `mean-median-blanks`, `dot-plot-mean`, `mean-missing-value`, `mode-of-data`, `staar-stem-leaf-combine`, `stem-leaf-construct`, `stem-leaf-match-select`, `dot-plot-iqr`, `median-data`, `range-data`, `interquartile-range-data`, `quartile-list`, `means-in-mads`, `double-box-compare`, `median-mode-compare-blanks`, `frequency-table-grade4` (long but procedural: sort by the hundreds digit first), `categorical-summary`, `normal-probability` (z, table, sketch).

Order matters: a cheaper model's lesson comes *after* the strong-writer lesson that defines the picture at that grade, so its concept can say "the plot you saw built". Review stays with a model at least as strong as the writer, and an independent reviewer watches every lesson of the first wave.

## 6. What is misfiled, off grade or badly conceived; what the standards ask that the app never does

> Flagging a type means it is a weak choice for a main example or sits in the wrong skill, never that the app should drop it.

**Misfiled or off grade.**
- `categorical-summary` (mode, relative frequency as a percent, a count from a percent bar) is 6.RP.A.3 and 6.SP.A.3 filed under grade 1 `categorical-data`. Move to grade 6 (`percentages` for the percent forms, `mean-median` for the mode).
- `table-fits-description` is two problems: a one-way table that must sum to the stated total with the stated number of choices (1.MD.C.4, grades 1 to 2) and a boys/girls-by-grade table (a two-way table, 8.SP.A.4). Split; the second belongs with `two-way-tables` in family 33.
- `data-list-for-display` (grade 2) includes "a table of counts in ranges" with values to 46: binned data, grade 6 `distribution-plots`. Keep the dot-plot case at grade 2, and link it to a standard (it has none).
- `frequency-table-build` sits under grade 2 `picture-bar-graphs` (2.MD.D.10) with no picture or bar graph, the word "frequency", and equal-width intervals. It is the grade 4 to 6 frequency table. Move to `frequency-bar-graphs` or `distribution-plots`.
- `line-plot-center-range` (grade 4, 4.MD.B.4) asks for mode and median, which are 6.SP.A.3; only the range is honest 4.MD.B.4. Split, or mark those variants a grade 6 stretch; the lesson must define them from scratch either way.
- `frequency-bar-graphs` (grade 4) has no standard id, and `frequency-table-grade4` is judged to serve 2.OA.A.1, wrong on both counts: grouping 28 values into intervals is histogram preparation (6.SP.B.4). `scaled-bar-grade4` is 3.MD.B.3 with scales of 25, 50 and 100: honest grade 4 practice; say so.
- `dot-plot-statement-select` (grade 6, skill `mean-median`) has no measure of center in it; it is dot-plot reading and belongs in `distribution-plots` beside `staar-dot-plot-threshold`.
- `staar-stem-leaf-combine` (grade 6, `distribution-plots`) is reading plus one sum or difference; it is a variant of `stem-leaf-read` and should sit beside it.
- `frequency-table-center` holds a percent-weighted course grade (already flagged in the curriculum plan); that is a weighted mean of parts, a different idea, and should be its own type.
- `signed-data-summaries` (grade 7) is grade 6 statistics on grade 7 numbers, linked only to 6.SP.B.5. Its data have no context ("Find the median of these data: −9, −4, ..."), while 6.SP.B.5 is "in relation to their context"; temperatures, elevations and balances would make the negatives mean something.
- `median-mode-compare-blanks` (grade 7, 7.SP.B.4) is median and mode of two seven-value lists with one comparing line; 6.SP.A.3 does the work.
- `statistics-mean` and `mean-median` are two skills for one standard (noted in the curriculum plan); `mean-median-blanks` already does the median inside `statistics-mean`.
- `measure-meaning-choice` and `best-display-choice` are vocabulary types; keep their films short, one built picture per word.
- Catalog links: `scaled-picture-graph` is judged 3.OA.D.8 and not its home 3.MD.B.3; `line-plot-whole` is judged 2.MD.B.5 and 6.SP.B.5, not 2.MD.D.9; `staar-dot-plot-threshold` and `data-list-for-display` have no linked standard.

**Problem text and answer format.**
- `fraction-plot-difference` expects 7/4 and 25/4 for lengths in inches; a fourth grader writes 1 3/4 and 6 1/4. Accept both, or the lesson contradicts the checker. `mean-data` (86/5) and `dot-plot-mean` (25/8) should accept 17.2 and 3.125.
- `line-plot-data-match`, `stem-leaf-match-select`, `stem-leaf-construct`, `frequency-table-build` and `choose-the-plot` present plots as text ("Marks: 1 has 1, ...", "Plot A"). The choices must be drawn displays, in the app and in the film.
- `dot-plot-iqr` and `interquartile-range-data` state the leave-out-the-median convention; `quartile-list` uses even counts so it never arises; the box plots in `box-plot-read` and `double-box-compare` must be generated with the same convention or a learner who checks finds a contradiction.
- `fraction-line-plot` and `line-plot-build-slots` label quarters as 1 2/4; the lesson keeps the app's labels and says once that 2/4 is one half.
