# Addition and subtraction, part 2: ten concepts

Written from the family design and the facts output. Each stands on the family's foundations by number and uses the family's pictures; the habits (read, at a glance, the sure fast way, sense, check) are the same five moves as in place value. Two notes that run through all ten: grade 1 and 2 films are short and slow, numbers as words, nothing struck out; and every example's picture is to scale, labeled, and the one the child could sketch.

---

### Grade 1: counting-arithmetic (1.OA.C.5)

The two types are one picture seen from both sides: the grade 1 line, 0 to 20 with every number written, a hollow mark at the start, one arc per step. `count-on-back` gives the steps and asks where you land; `counted-steps` shows where you landed and asks how many steps. The second film is brief and leans on the first.

**`count-on-back` — Count on or back from a number.** Foundation 5.
First: both sampled variants count exactly 1. The standard's own example counts on 2. If the generator never goes past 1, widen it to 1 through 5; the lesson designs for that (the example counts 3, the quick case counts 1) so it is right either way.
Standard: 1.OA.C.5, "Relate counting to addition and subtraction (e.g., by counting on 2 to add 2)"; the "counting on" strategy of 1.OA.C.6.
Foundations first: the line 0 to 20, every number written: the counting numbers laid in a row. *Count on*: say the next numbers, one for each step, moving right. *Count back*: the numbers before, moving left. The *start* is the number you stand on; it is not a step, because you are already there, so it is not said. Then the two facts this lesson exists for: counting on three is adding three; counting back three is taking away three. Each step is an arc; arcs are numbered underneath; the dot where you stop is where you land.
Read: "Start at 9. Count on 3. What number do you land on?" Gold on "on": the direction. At a glance: on means right, so bigger than 9; three steps, so a little bigger; 9 is one short of ten, so the first step lands on 10.
Example: (1) A hollow mark at 9: "stand here. Don't say nine." (2) "Ten": an arc 9 to 10 drawn on the word, a 1 under it. "Eleven": arc, 2. "Twelve": arc, 3. Every arc one space long. (3) The dot lands on 12 and takes its number. (4) Board: 9 + 3 = 12. "Counting on three is adding three."
Quick second case, the other direction and one step (the generator's shape): "Start at 13. Count back 1." One arc left on "twelve"; land on 12; 13 − 1 = 12. Counting back one is the number just before.
Fast: for 1, say the next number (or the one before); no counting. For 2 to 5, say that many numbers, a finger up for each; the last one said is where you land.
Sense: on lands to the right, on a bigger number; back lands left, on a smaller one. A count-on that landed on a smaller number went the wrong way.
Check: count back the same steps from the landing: twelve, back three: eleven, ten, nine. Home. Or count the arcs.
Line: "Don't count the number you're standing on."
Trap: saying the start as the first step ("nine, ten, eleven": 11). The start is a hollow mark with no arc; only arcs are numbered, and the number of arcs is the count. Also "back" taken as right: each arc arrows the way it goes.
Kit: `numberline` with `count: true` (hollow start, numbered arcs) and an option to label every tick on the grade 1 line.
Writer: a capable cheaper model once `number-line-missing-hop` has approved the hop picture. The risk is voice (short sentences, numbers as words), which the blind test checks.

**`counted-steps` — How many were counted on or back.**
Standard: 1.OA.C.5, the same clause from the other side: the count of numbers said is the amount added or taken away.
Foundations first: the same line and words, and one idea more: every number said after the start is one step, so counting the numbers said counts the steps.
Read: "Count on from 11: 12, 13, 14, 15. How many were added to 11?" Gold: "added". At a glance: the answer is the number of numbers listed, a small number; it is also how far 15 is from 11.
Example: (1) The list on the board; a hollow mark at 11. (2) Each listed number is read and its arc drawn on the word: 12 (1), 13 (2), 14 (3), 15 (4); the dot lands on 15. (3) Four arcs: four were added. (4) Board: 11 + 4 = 15.
Quick second case: "Count back from 7: 6, 5, 4, 3, 2. How many were subtracted from 7?" Five arcs left; 7 − 5 = 2.
Fast: count the numbers said, not the start. Touch each listed number once.
Sense: the start and the landing are that far apart; 11 to 15 is a short way, so the answer is small.
Check: the two ends tell the hops: 15 − 11 = 4; 7 − 2 = 5.
Line: "Each number said is one step."
Trap: counting the start too (5 for "11: 12, 13, 14, 15"), or answering the landing number (15, or 2). The start has no arc; only arcs are numbered; the gold word "added" is not "where did you land".
Kit: as above. Writer: cheaper, as above.

### Grade 1: add-two-digit (1.NBT.C.4)

**`add-ones-regroup` — Add ones to a two-digit number.** Pilot 2. Foundations 9 and 7. Rename the skill `add-ones-to-two-digit` (design, section 6).
Standard: 1.NBT.C.4, "adding a two-digit number and a one-digit number ... one adds tens and tens, ones and ones; and sometimes it is necessary to compose a ten"; "relate the strategy to a written method".
Foundations first: 68 in the cubes chart: 6 rods, 8 cubes, the digit under each pile. One line from family 2: "a rod is ten cubes; the digit counts, the place says what." Define "add 9 ones": nine cubes, so they go to the cubes, not the rods. Define the trade: when a pile reaches ten cubes, they become one rod and move to the tens; nothing is added or lost. Beside the chart, the written form: 68 over + 9 with headers tens | ones, the 9 under the 8, because it is ones.
Read: "The blocks show 68. Add 9 ones. What is the new total?" Gold: "ones". At a glance: 8 and 9 is more than ten, so a trade is coming and the tens go up by one; the answer is in the seventies.
Example: (1) Nine rust cubes arrive in the ones; the count label ticks 8 to 17: seventeen ones. (2) "Seventeen ones is a ten and seven." Eight teal and two rust cubes slide together, fuse into a rod, slide into the tens and turn teal; the ones label reads 7, the tens label 60 to 70; in the same beat the stack writes 7 under the ones and a small 1 above the tens. (3) Tens: six rods and one more: seven. Written. (4) 77. (5) Said once on the same cubes: "sixty-eight and two make seventy; seven more is seventy-seven." The two that filled the rod are the two.
Fast: add the ones; if they reach ten, the tens digit goes up one and the ones digit is what is left over.
Sense: 68 and about ten is about 78; the answer is just under, in the seventies.
Check: read the chart: seven rods, seven cubes. Or take the nine back: 77 − 7 = 70, 70 − 2 = 68.
Line: "Tens with tens, ones with ones. Ten ones make a ten."
Trap: writing 17 whole (617), joining the 9 to the rods (158), or treating +9 as +10 (78). One digit under each pile; ten cubes must become a rod and have nowhere to go but the tens; the 9 is drawn as cubes, and seven of them are still in the ones at the end.
Kit: the cubes skin on `blocks`; `add` in rust; `trade` up with counts and digits updating; a shot holding chart and stack together so the carry is written on the fuse's word.
Writer: strongest. The first trade: the whole family's algorithm is this one beat.

### Grade 2: addition-within-100 (2.NBT.B.5)

**`add-two-digit-regroup` — Add two two-digit numbers with a carry.** Foundation 9.
Standard: 2.NBT.B.5, "Fluently add ... within 100 using strategies based on place value, properties of operations"; 1.NBT.C.4's "tens and tens, ones and ones".
Foundations first: two rows in one chart, 55 teal (5 rods, 5 cubes) and 29 rust (2 rods, 9 cubes); the stack beside them with headers: the stack is the chart written down, ones under ones. One line: "you know ten ones make a ten." Define the carry: the small 1 above the tens is the rod made from ten ones.
Read: "What is 55 + 29?" At a glance: 29 is one short of 30, and 55 and 30 is 85, so the answer is 84, in the eighties; 5 and 9 is 14, so a carry is coming.
Example: (1) Estimate beside the stack: about 55 + 30 = 85. (2) Ones: the nine rust cubes join the five: fourteen; ten fuse into a rod and slide to the tens as the stack writes 4 and the carry 1. (3) Tens: five rods, two rods, and the one carried: eight. Written. (4) 84. (5) "Or see it this way: 29 is thirty less one. Fifty-five and thirty is eighty-five; give one back: eighty-four." Said once; same answer.
Fast: tens with tens, ones with ones; a column of ten or more sends one ten left. When an addend ends in 9, add the round ten and give one back.
Sense: the estimate puts it in the eighties; 74 is wrong at a glance.
Check: 84 − 29 = 55; or the round-ten way lands on 84 again.
Line: "Tens with tens, ones with ones. The carry is a ten."
Trap: forgetting the carry (74), or writing 14 across two columns (714). The rod slides into the tens pile as the 1 is written above it, one beat; one digit per column.
Kit: nothing beyond 4.1 (`estimate`) and 4.2 (second row, `join`, `trade`).
Writer: cheaper, once `add-ones-regroup` is approved; `decimal-sum-difference` is the stack reference.

**`make-hundred` — The number that makes 100.** Foundations 6, 9, 10.
Standard: 1.OA.D.8, "Determine the unknown whole number in an addition ... equation" (45 + □ = 100); 2.NBT.B.5, "the relationship between addition and subtraction".
Foundations first: the box is a missing part. The bond: 100 above, 45 and "?" below; one line, "the whole hides a part." 100 is ten rods: a flat. Then the open line with 45 and 100, a bracket between them marked "?": the missing part is a hop.
Read: "45 + □ = 100. What number goes in the box?" Gold: the box. At a glance: 45 is less than half of 100, so the box is more than 50; 5 and the box's ones must make ten, so the box ends in 5.
Example: (1) On the line, a hop from 45 to the next ten, 50, labeled +5 on "five". (2) A hop from 50 to 100, +50 on "fifty". (3) The bracket resolves: 5 and 50 is 55. (4) Why 55 and not 65, on the cubes: 4 rods and 5 cubes; five rust cubes arrive and fuse with the five into a rod. "That rod is the fifth ten. So the tens only need five more." Five rust rods arrive; ten rods fuse into the flat: 100. The rust pieces are 5 rods and 5 cubes: 55. (5) Board: 45 + 55 = 100.
Quick second case, no ones: 70 + □ = 100. One hop, +30. Nothing to make ten with, so the tens make ten.
Fast: the ones make ten, the tens make ninety (ones digits sum to 10, tens digits to 9). A number ending in 0: the tens make ten.
Sense: 45 is a bit less than half, so its partner is a bit more than 50.
Check: stack 45 + 55: ones 10, carry one; tens 4 + 5 + 1 = 10: 100.
Line: "Ones make ten, tens make ninety."
Trap: 65 (the ones made to ten *and* the tens made to a hundred). The fuse shows the ones' ten is the fifth rod; nine rods are already there before the last one. 45 + 65 would be a flat and a rod: 110.
Kit: `numberline` hop and distance; `blocks` trade up to a flat; `bond` with "?".
Writer: strongest. The honest "why not 65" is the lesson.

### Grade 2: subtraction-within-100 (2.NBT.B.5)

**`subtract-two-digit-regroup` — Subtract within 100 with a trade.** Foundations 6, 9, 10. Two examples, because two true methods compete and the numbers decide; the lesson teaches the choice.
Standard: 2.NBT.B.5, "Fluently ... subtract within 100 using strategies based on place value, properties of operations, and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction."
Foundations first: two meanings on one bond: 100 − 86 is "what is left when 86 is taken from 100" and "86 and what make 100"; same bond, same empty circle. Define the trade down: when a pile has too few cubes to take from, open a rod into ten cubes; the pieces change, the amount does not. One line: estimate first.
Read: "What is 100 − 86?" At a glance: 86 is close to 100, so the answer is small, under 20; 6 and what make ten: it ends in 4. The top number is round and the two are close: count up.
Example A, counting up: (1) Open line, 86 and 100, bracket "?". (2) Hop 86 to 90, +4; hop 90 to 100, +10. (3) 4 and 10: 14. (4) Board: 86 + 14 = 100, so 100 − 86 = 14. (5) One beat: the stack could do it too (open the hundred into ten tens, a ten into ten ones), but when the top is round or the numbers are close, counting up is quicker.
Example B, the algorithm: "What is 73 − 48?" Not round, not close. (1) Estimate: about 70 − 50 = 20. (2) 7 rods, 3 cubes; the stack beside with headers. (3) Ones: "take eight from three. Only three here: can't take eight. Open a ten." A rod slides right and breaks into ten cubes: 6 rods, 13 cubes. In the same beat, in the same columns, the 7 is struck with 6 above it and 13 appears above the 3, read top to bottom. (4) Thirteen take away eight: five; eight cubes leave to a muted pile. (5) Tens: six take away four: two; four rods leave. (6) 25. (7) Add back: 25 + 48 = 73, the ones 5 + 8 = 13 fusing into the rod that was opened.
Fast: top round or numbers close: count up to the next ten, then by tens. Otherwise tens with tens, ones with ones, opening a ten when the ones run short.
Sense: 100 − 86 is small, under 20; 73 − 48 is about 20.
Check: add back, every time: 14 + 86 = 100; 25 + 48 = 73.
Line: "Can't take? Open a ten. Close numbers? Count up."
Trap: taking the smaller digit from the larger whichever is on top (100 − 86 → 186; 73 − 48 → 35). The pile has three cubes; eight cannot leave it; the only move is to open a rod. On the line, the hops from 86 land on 90 and 100; 186 is nowhere.
Kit: `stack` vertical borrow (struck digit, new digit above, in its own column); `blocks` split and `remove` to a muted pile; `numberline` distance.
Writer: strongest. Explain why each method fits, and stage the first vertical borrow clearly.

### Grade 2: addition-facts (2.OA.B.2)

**`make-ten-fact` — Add through ten.** Foundations 3, 7, 8. The grade 2 twin of grade 1's `add-within-20`: one concept, this film briefer and aimed at knowing the fact; the two films use different numbers so a child who sees both sees two cases.
Standard: 2.OA.B.2, "Fluently add ... within 20 using mental strategies. By end of Grade 2, know from memory all sums of two one-digit numbers"; the strategy is 1.OA.C.6's "making ten (e.g., 8 + 6 = 8 + 2 + 4 = 10 + 4 = 14)".
Foundations first: two ten frames side by side. A full frame is ten and is read, not counted. The empties of a frame are its partner to ten: eight filled leaves two. Said once: breaking a number never changes it; five is two and three, still five.
Read: "What is 8 + 5?" At a glance: more than ten, less than twenty; eight needs two, and the five has it, so the answer is ten and the rest of the five.
Example: (1) Eight teal counters in the first frame, five rust in the second; the two empties go gold: "eight needs two." (2) Before anything moves, the five is drawn as two and three: a gold ring around two of them; the label still reads 5. (3) The two slide into the empties; the frame's label flips to 10; three remain, labeled 3. (4) "Ten and three: thirteen." Board: 8 + 5 = 8 + 2 + 3 = 10 + 3 = 13.
Quick second case, the generator's: "What is 2 + 9?" Start with the bigger number: nine needs one; two is one and one; one slides in: ten and one, eleven. "Turn it around; the total is the same."
Fast: bigger number first; its partner to ten comes out of the smaller number; ten and what is left.
Sense: a teen number whose ones digit is the leftover.
Check: count on from eight five steps: nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen. Or 13 − 5 = 8.
Line: "Eight needs two. Fill the ten, then add the rest."
Trap: filling the ten and stopping (8 + 5 → 10), or breaking the five in the wrong place. The five is split in gold before any counter moves, its label stays 5 until the two have gone, and the three are still sitting in their frame when ten is said.
Kit: `grid` frame with `fa.empty` and `fa.full`; counters; cell moves. Nothing new.
Writer: cheaper, once the grade 1 frame lesson is approved; `make-ten-remainder` is the voice reference.

**`related-subtraction` — Subtract within 20 by thinking addition.** Foundations 6 and 7. Grade 2 twin of `subtract-within-20`.
Standard: 2.OA.B.2, "Fluently ... subtract within 20 using mental strategies"; the strategies are 1.OA.B.4, "subtract 10 − 8 by finding the number that makes 10 when added to 8", and 1.OA.C.6, "decomposing a number leading to a ten".
Foundations first: the bond: 15 above, 8 and "?" below. Subtraction is the missing part: "15 − 8" and "8 and what make 15" are the same bond with the same empty circle. Two frames show 15: a full frame (ten, read not counted) and five.
Read: "What is 15 − 8?" At a glance: the answer is smaller than 15; 8 is more than half of 15, so the other part is less than half; 8 needs two for ten, then the five: seven.
Example: (1) Eight counters of the first frame turn teal: that is the 8. (2) "Eight and what make fifteen?" The two empties of that frame go gold and fill rust: two to reach ten. (3) The five in the second frame, rust: five more. (4) Two and five: seven; the "?" fills. Board: 8 + 2 = 10, 10 + 5 = 15, so 15 − 8 = 7. (5) The same picture the other way, one beat: take the five to reach ten, three more from the frame: seven. The learner picks.
Quick second case, a small number taken, the generator's: "What is 11 − 2?" Count back two: ten, nine. Two counters leave, or two hops back. For taking one, two or three, counting back is fastest.
Fast: a small number taken: count back. A big number taken: ask what goes with it, through ten.
Sense: a part is smaller than the whole; taking a big part leaves a small one.
Check: the bond: 8 + 7 = 15; 9 + 2 = 11.
Line: "Subtract by asking: what goes with it?"
Trap: 15 − 8 → 13 (five from eight, keep the one). The eight counters are the start, and the question is what sits beside them; nothing is ever taken from the eight.
Kit: `bond` with ask and fill, beside the frames. Nothing new.
Writer: cheaper, once `subtract-through-ten` is approved.

**`related-subtraction-sentence` — Which subtraction does this fact help?** Multiple choice. Foundations 2 and 6.
First, as generated this item is badly conceived: the decoys are 15 + 5, 15 × 5 and 15 ÷ 5, so it is solved by spotting the minus sign, and two decoys use signs a grade 2 child has not met (3.OA); "n" is grade 3's letter (3.OA.D.8), grade 2 uses a box. Fix: decoys that are subtractions not from the fact (10 − 5 = □, 15 − 7 = □, 20 − 5 = □). The concept teaches the fact family so the lesson is right either way; until the fix lands, the rule-out beat says only "the question asks for a subtraction sentence: one choice has a minus sign" and does not teach the other signs.
Standard: 1.OA.C.6, "using the relationship between addition and subtraction (e.g., knowing that 8 + 4 = 12, one knows 12 − 8 = 4)"; 1.OA.B.4; 2.OA.B.2.
Foundations first: the bond for 5 + 10 = 15: parts 5 and 10, whole 15 above. "One bond, four facts": 5 + 10 = 15, 10 + 5 = 15, 15 − 5 = 10, 15 − 10 = 5 write themselves beside it in the parts' colors. Define: a subtraction starts with the whole and takes away a part; what is left is the other part. Define n in passing: "the box with a name; it stands for the number we don't know yet."
Read: "Here is an addition fact: 5 + 10 = 15. Which subtraction sentence can this fact help you solve?" Gold: "subtraction". At a glance: the sentence must use the fact's three numbers and no others, and 15 comes first.
Example: (1) The bond and its four facts. (2) The choices; 15 − 5 = n is the bond's third fact: whole 15, part 5 taken, the other part is n. (3) n = 10, read off the bond. Board: 15 − 5 = 10, because 5 + 10 = 15.
Rule out: as generated, the three without a minus sign dim together. With fixed decoys: 15 − 7 and 20 − 5 use a number that is not in the fact; 10 − 5 starts with a part, not the whole: all three dim together, "not this bond's numbers, or not starting from the whole."
Fast: the whole first, minus a part.
Check: solve the chosen sentence: 15 − 5 = 10, and 10 is in the fact.
Line: "Two parts make the whole; the whole hides a part."
Trap (with fixed decoys): picking 10 − 5 because it uses the two numbers you were given. The whole sits on top of the bond; every subtraction starts there.
Kit: `bond` with `facts`; `dim`.
Writer: strongest. The lesson is the fact family, not the minus sign, and the item's flaws have to be handled honestly on screen.

### Grade 2: multiple-addends (2.NBT.B.6)

**`add-several-two-digit` — Add three or four two-digit numbers.** Foundations 8, 9, 11.
Standard: 2.NBT.B.6, "Add up to four two-digit numbers using strategies based on place value and properties of operations."
Foundations first: the stack with headers takes any number of rows; ones under ones, tens under tens, the same chart. One line: "ten ones make a ten; twenty ones make two tens." Define the carry as whatever tens the column makes, read off the column's total, not always 1. Any order: pair ones that make ten first.
Read: "Find the total: 49 + 31 + 13 + 34." At a glance: the tens, 40 + 30 + 10 + 30, are 110, and the ones add about 17 more: around 125, past a hundred, so a hundreds digit will appear.
Example: (1) Stack of four rows, headers tens | ones, estimate ≈ 120 beside. (2) Ones: 9 and 1 make ten (the pair goes gold), 3 and 4 make seven: seventeen. "Seventeen ones is one ten and seven": write 7, carry 1. (3) Tens: 4 + 3 + 1 + 3 is eleven, and the carry: twelve. "Twelve tens is a hundred and two tens": write 2, and the 1 in a hundreds place that appears as it is said. (4) 127.
Quick second case, a bigger ones column: 48 + 27 + 35 + 19. Ones 8 + 7 + 5 + 9 = 29: two tens and nine; write 9, carry 2. Tens 4 + 2 + 3 + 1 + 2 = 12: 129.
Fast: pair ones to ten where you can; the ones total tells the carry (its tens digit); then the tens with the carry; a tens total of ten or more spills into the hundreds.
Sense: the estimate: near 120, past 100.
Check: add the ones column again from the bottom, pairing differently: seventeen again. Or 127 − 34 = 93 = 49 + 31 + 13.
Line: "The column's total tells the carry."
Trap: carrying 1 by habit when the column makes twenty or more (29 ones → 119), or losing the hundreds (27 for 127). The carry is written as the column total is said in tens ("two tens and nine"); the hundreds header appears when the tens column says twelve.
Kit: `stack` with up to four rows, a carry of 2, a new place appearing; `estimate`. Nothing beyond 4.1.
Writer: cheaper. One method, every digit computed by the kit and checked by `--dry`; after `add-two-digit-regroup` is approved.
