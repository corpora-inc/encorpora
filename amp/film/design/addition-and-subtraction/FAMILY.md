# Addition and subtraction, K to 4: family design

Eighteen skills, 30 problem types, kindergarten to grade 4. Planning snapshot: `src/curriculum/catalog.ts`, the engine templates and `film/facts.ts`, snapshot 2026-11-09. This is the family-level design: the idea, the pictures, how a lesson goes, the kit, the seam, what is misfiled, the pilot. The per-type concepts come in written from this.

---

## 1. The idea underneath the whole family

Addition and subtraction are one fact seen from two sides: **a whole and its parts.** Put the parts together and you have the whole. Know the whole and one part, and the other part is what subtraction finds, whether it was taken away, is still missing, or is the distance between two numbers. Every fact is three numbers (3, 4, 7); whoever knows two knows the third, so nothing is learned twice: 3 + 4, 4 + 3, 7 − 3 and 7 − 4 are one picture.

What makes it fast is one landmark: **ten.** Within ten a child learns the parts of every number by sight. Past ten, fill the ten first and add what is left. Past a hundred, do the same thing in every place, which is place value's trade (ten of these make one of those) written as a small 1 above a column or a struck digit. A child who owns part-whole and ten owns the family; the rest is length.

### The foundations, in the order they are built

1. **Joining and taking away are things you can watch.** (K) Two groups pushed together are one group: count it. Some taken out of a group: count what stays. Counting all is how a five-year-old adds, and it is right. The things can be anything, dogs, almonds, houses; a counter stands for one of them.
2. **A number is made of parts.** (K) Seven is 1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4. The number bond, a whole with two parts under it, is the picture. Once a child sees 7 as parts, "□ + 6 = 7" is not an equation; it is a bond with one circle empty. The equal sign is said once: *the same amount on both sides*.
3. **Ten is the landmark, and the frame shows its partner.** (K) Eight fills eight cells of a ten frame and leaves two empty: 8 and 2 make ten. Partners to ten are seen, not counted.
4. **Facts are known, not counted.** (K within 5; grade 1 within 10; grade 2 within 20, from memory.) A known fact is a bond you can see at once: doubles, partners to ten, five-and-some on the frame. The film's job here is the picture that makes a fact visible enough to stick.
5. **Count on from the bigger number; the number you stand on is not counted.** (1) To add 3, say the next three numbers. The number of hops is the amount added; the same backward is subtracting.
6. **Subtraction is the missing part.** (1) 13 − 8 asks "8 and what make 13?", the same bond with a different circle empty. Two meanings, one bond: what is left (take away) and how far apart (difference). The learner picks whichever is faster for the numbers in front of them; a lesson never forces take-away onto a "how many more" question.
7. **Go through ten.** (1, 2) 8 + 5: two of the 5 fill the ten, three are left: 13. 13 − 4: take 3 to reach ten, then 1 more: 9. The second number is broken where ten is, and breaking a number never changes it.
8. **Any order, any grouping.** (1) 6 + 2 + 8: 2 and 8 make a ten first. Turn the parts around; the whole stays.
9. **Tens with tens, ones with ones; ten ones make a ten.** (1, 2; from family 2.) 68 + 9: the 9 ones join the 8 ones; ten of them fuse into a rod that moves to the tens. The column algorithm is that trade written down. Taking away with nothing to take from: open a ten into ten ones. No tens to open: open a hundred into ten tens first.
10. **A number is a length; a hop is an amount.** (2) On a number line, back 30 is three long arcs and back 3 three short ones, to scale. The distance from 44 to 60 is a subtraction, found by counting up: 6 to 50, 10 to 60, 16. The missing hop is the missing part on a line.
11. **Estimate, run the same algorithm at any length, add back.** (3, 4) Round the front digits first so you know where the answer lands; the stack works the same with six digits as with two; the reverse operation proves it. A near-round addend gives the exact answer at a glance: 439 + 198 is 439 + 200, less 2.
12. **Read the whole story before any arithmetic.** (1, 3) Two jars poured together is a join; "set aside" is a take away; "how many more" is a distance. Draw what happens, and the operation falls out of the drawing. Family 8 owns the bar; here it is born from the bond.

### What this family takes from others, and gives

- From **family 1** (counting): the last number said is how many; counting on from any number; ten as a landmark.
- From **family 2** (place value): the digit counts and the place says what; ten of these make one of those and the amount never changes; zero holds a place; on a number line, find the step first.
- It says once what **family 11** will own: the equal sign means the same amount on both sides.
- It gives **family 8** the bond stretched into the part-whole bar, and gives every later family the habit *estimate, work, check by the reverse*.

A lesson never re-teaches a foundation it stands on; it names it in one line and shows the owned picture ("you know ten ones make a ten").

## 2. The visual language

One picture system from counters to the six-digit stack. Each picture is the previous one grown, so a grade 4 child still sees the bond inside the algorithm. Everything is to scale and labeled, and each picture is one a child can sketch on scratch paper; each lesson draws that sketch beside the kit's picture, once.

### The pictures

| Picture | Grades | Drawn as | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Things, then counters** | K to 1 | Real things first (five dogs, one more dog), each morphing into the app's counter: round teal for the first group, square rust for the second, exactly as the problem shows them | One counter is one thing; it can be anything you count |
| **Ten frame** | K to 2 | 5 × 2 cells to scale, filled in the app's order; empty cells faint; a full frame is labeled 10 | Ten as a landmark; the empties are its partner |
| **Number bond** | K to 3 | A whole circle above, two part circles below, two short links; counters or a numeral inside; "?" in the missing circle | A number is its parts; one bond, four facts |
| **Base-ten cubes in the chart** | 1 to 3 | 3D cubes: a unit cube, a rod of ten cubes, a flat of a hundred, to scale with cube lines visible, in family 2's chart (place names, digit row, live counts); two addends as two rows, teal and rust | Tens with tens, ones with ones; the trade |
| **The stack** | 1 to 4 | The standard algorithm with place headers; the carry small above its column; a borrow as the struck digit with its new digit directly above it, in its own column; the estimate beside | The trade in writing; the only picture at grades 3 and 4 |
| **Number line** | 1 to 3 | Grade 1: ticked 0 to 20. Grade 2: the open line, only the numbers landed on. Hops are arcs labeled with the signed amount (+10, −3), tens long and ones short, to scale; the landing dot gets its number | A number is a length; a hop is an amount; a distance is a subtraction |
| **Part-whole bar** | 1, 3 | The bond's two circles stretched into two bars end to end, lengths to scale, labels inside, a bracket over the whole, "?" for the unknown | The bond for big numbers and for stories |

The scratch-paper forms: dots and a bond (K); two rows of five, a line 0 to 20 (grade 1); a stick for a ten and a dot for a one, drawn beside the cubes and named once, and an open line with arcs (grade 2); the stack with its carries and an estimate (grades 3 and 4).

### Color means one thing each

- **teal**: the first group, the part you start with, what is there.
- **rust**: the second group, what joins or what leaves.
- **gold**: the one thing being looked at now: the empty cells of a frame, the ten being completed, the column being worked, the hop being counted, the word in the problem that says what happens. One gold thing at a time.
- **muted**: what has been taken away (counters that left stay as faint ghosts beside the group, so "what left plus what stayed" can be checked), ruled-out choices, placeholder zeros.
- **ink**: the whole, the total, the digit row.

No tinted answer backgrounds; nothing drawn at a size it is not.

### The motions, and what each proves

- **Join**: the second group slides against the first into one row or frame; the count label counts all. Addition is putting together (1).
- **Leave**: the taken counters slide out to a muted pile; what stays is counted. The two piles still make the start (1, 6).
- **Bond** (split and merge): a group's counters separate into the two part circles, or two parts merge up into the whole; the whole's label never changes. A number is its parts (2). **Ask**: the circle in question goes "?" gold; the four facts write themselves beside the bond in the parts' colors (6).
- **Fill**: the empties go gold and are counted; counters slide in from the second group until the frame is full and its label flips to 10; the rest of the second group stays, still visibly its own number. Make ten (3, 7).
- **Hop**: an arc from one number to the next, drawn on the counting word; the start is a hollow mark, never numbered; hops are numbered under their arcs. Count on and back (5).
- **Fuse and carry**: ten cubes snap into a rod and slide to the tens column as the small 1 is written above the tens on the stack, in the same beat. The carry is a trade (9).
- **Split and borrow**: a rod breaks into ten cubes and slides right as the digit is struck and the new digits appear above, in the same beat. Across a zero the chain runs in order, hundreds first: the hundred opens into ten tens, then one ten opens into ten ones. The borrow is a trade (9).
- **Distance**: a bracket with "?" between two points on the line; arcs counted up from the smaller fill it; the "?" becomes their sum. A difference is a missing hop (10).
- **Bar join and remove**: a second bar snaps end to end with the first under a bracket; a part slides out and the remainder stays. Stories (12).
- **Dim**: choices that cannot be right fade together in one beat, with the one reason said aloud.

## 3. How lessons in this family go

### The habits every film builds

Five moves, in this order, as in place value.

1. **Read.** The problem exactly as the app shows it, choices included. The word that says what happens goes gold: *altogether*, *take away*, *how many more*, *left*, *set aside*, *hop back*. One beat says what the answer must look like: a sum is bigger than both parts; what is left is smaller than the start; a missing part is smaller than the whole; a partner to ten is "ten minus"; a two-digit sum's tens digits tell its decade or the one above.
2. **At a glance.** What is known with no work: 8 is two short of ten; 29 is one short of 30; 198 is two short of 200; 86 is 14 short of 100; a hop of 30 lands three tens back; a story with "set aside" ends smaller than the two jars together. At grades 3 and 4 this beat is the estimate, said and written beside the stack.
3. **The sure fast way.** The shortest path that always works, shown on the picture. A shortcut is taught only if it never fails, and only with the picture that explains it: counting on from the bigger number; the partner to ten read off the empties; adding 9 or 29 or 198 by adding the round number and giving back the extra; counting up when the top number is round or the two numbers are close; *tens with tens, ones with ones*, which is the algorithm. Digit tricks that only work without a carry are never taught.
4. **Sense.** Before the answer is boxed: the estimate, or the picture that says the size is right ("still in the seventies", "less than the whole", "a bit more than 180").
5. **Check.** The reverse operation, every time: a subtraction by adding back (what stayed plus what left is the start), an addition by subtracting a part from the total, or by the bond (two parts, one whole). On the number line, count from the other end. For multiple choice, the ruled-out choices dim together with the reason, then the chosen one is proved.

### The sure fast ways, by grade

- **K.** See small parts without counting (five and some on the frame). Count on from the bigger group. Read the partner to ten off the empties. Take away by counting what stays, or by knowing the bond.
- **Grade 1.** Count on for +1, +2, +3 and back for −1, −2, −3, no further; beyond that, make ten. Doubles, then near doubles (6 + 7 is 6 + 6 and 1). Subtract by thinking addition through ten: 13 − 8 is 8 and 2 and 3. Ones added to a two-digit number join the ones; if they reach ten, a rod moves to the tens.
- **Grade 2.** Adding 9, 19, 29: add the ten and give one back. Making a hundred: the ones make the last ten, so the tens only need to make ninety (45 needs 55, not 65). Subtracting from 100 or between close numbers: count up. Several addends: pair ones that make ten in the column, then carry what the column total says (a column of 20 or more carries 2). Otherwise *tens with tens, ones with ones*, with a trade whenever a column needs one.
- **Grades 3 and 4.** Estimate from the front digits first. A near-round addend: add the round number, adjust. 1,000 − 863: count up 7, 30, 100. Numbers of different lengths line up on the right, never the left. Then the stack, the same at any length; then add back.

### Reading a story

The words point to the action; the picture decides. *Pours both together*, *altogether*, *in all* join parts into a whole. *Set aside*, *spends*, *takes away*, *left* remove a part. *How many more*, *how far*, *how long is the hop* ask the distance between two amounts. The lesson acts the story out (jars poured, marbles set aside, jumps on the line) and draws the bar or the hops before any number is written; the operation is read off the drawing. Never a key-word recipe recited as steps, and never one meaning of subtraction forced onto the other.

### Ruling out choices

Two types are multiple choice. *Which story matches the jumps*: a jump right is money coming in, a jump left is money going out, so the first jump's direction dims half the stories at once; the order of the two amounts dims the rest. *Which subtraction sentence does this fact help*: the sentence must use the fact's three numbers and nothing else; any sentence with another number, or another operation, dims together. Choices are never struck one by one.

### What children actually get wrong, and the picture that makes it impossible

- Counting the start as the first hop (9 + 2: "nine, ten" → 10), or counting all from one. The start is a hollow mark, never numbered; hops are numbered under their arcs.
- Answering the number taken ("6 take away 4" → 4). The taken counters sit faint outside the group; the count label counts what stayed.
- □ + 6 = 7 → 13, adding the two visible numbers; 8 = 6 + □ read as "8 + 6". The bond puts 7 above as the whole, so a part cannot be bigger than it; the equal sign is defined once as "the same amount on both sides". This is the place for a student voice to say "thirteen" and the teacher to point at the whole.
- Counting the filled cells, or answering 10, for "how many more fill the frame". Only the empties go gold.
- Making ten and losing the rest (8 + 5 → 10), or breaking the second number in the wrong place. The 5 is drawn as 2 and 3 before anything moves, and its label stays 5 until the 2 has joined the ten.
- 17 − 8 through ten: after taking 7 to reach ten, taking 8 again. The 8 is split into 7 and 1 first, and the 1 waits in gold.
- 68 + 9 → 617 or 158 (writing the whole 17), or treating +9 as +10. Ten cubes fuse into one rod that has to go somewhere: the tens column, where the 6 becomes 7.
- Forgetting the carry (55 + 29 → 74) or writing it in the ones (714). The rod moves and the 1 is written in the same beat.
- Taking the smaller digit from the larger regardless of which is on top (302 − 117 → 215, 100 − 86 → 186). The pile shows two cubes; you cannot take seven from two; the only move is to open a ten.
- Borrowing across a zero: writing 10 over the 0 without taking from the hundreds, or forgetting that the 10 becomes 9. The chain opens the hundred first, on its own word, then the ten.
- Making a hundred with both columns made to ten (45 + 65). The ones' ten *is* the tenth ten; the cubes show nine rods plus the fused one.
- Carrying 1 by habit when a column of several addends is 20 or more. The carry is read off the column total.
- Counting ticks or labels instead of hops (60 to 44 → 17), or subtracting digits (→ 24). Arcs 44 to 50 and 50 to 60 are labeled 6 and 10; the bracket reads 16.
- Matching a story by its two amounts and ignoring direction or order. The jumps are numbered 1 and 2 and arrow the way they go.
- Lining a five-digit number under a six-digit one at the left. Headers on the stack; the numbers slot from the right, as in family 2.
- Adding all three numbers in a two-step story, or subtracting from one jar instead of the total. The bar: two parts joined under the bracket first, then the set-aside part slides out of the whole.

### Lines worth remembering

"A counter can be anything you count." "Two parts make the whole; the whole hides a part." "Count on from the bigger number. Don't count the one you're standing on." "Eight needs two. Fill the ten, then add the rest." "Subtract by asking: what goes with it?" "Tens with tens, ones with ones. Ten ones make a ten." "Can't take? Open a ten. No tens? Open a hundred." "A hop's length is a subtraction: count up." "Line up on the right." "Estimate first. Add back to check." "Read the whole story, then draw it."

### Pace and voice

Kindergarten and grade 1 films are the shortest and slowest in the catalog: one idea, one picture, one example, one quick different case, about two minutes, short sentences, numbers as words, nothing struck out (say what to do instead). Motions finish before the voice moves on. A student voice is used where the real first wrong thought is worth hearing (13, 24, 65), never for chatter.

## 4. Tools to build

Exists and stays: the board; `stack` with headers and `column`'s `trade`/`write` timing; `grid`, `counters`, `@n` count labels, cell moves; the `blocks` chart with `split`, `cut`, `zoom`, `show`/`hide`/`highlight`; `axes` as a bare line; `scene` for the app's own figures (ten frames, base ten, number line); the mixed-lesson shape and the bar model of the reference two-step lesson.

### 4.1 `stack`: the borrow drawn vertically, unequal lengths, the estimate

Borrowing must read top to bottom in each column, not left to right. The struck digit and its new digit sit in the same column, the new digit directly above; the receiving column's new value ("12") sits above its digit; the carry is a small digit above its column. When a borrow crosses a zero, each step is its own `trade` word inside `column` beats, in order (hundreds 3 → 2; tens 0 → 10 → 9; ones 2 → 12), so nothing appears before the voice says why. For `"op": "-"` the top row may be longer than the bottom (540,046 − 71,719); rows right-align under the headers and the empty places stay empty, never zeros. Headers group thousands with the comma as the digit row does in `blocks`. `"estimate": "\\approx 200"` writes the estimate in muted ink beside the stack on its `at` word.

### 4.2 `blocks` in cubes, with the trade and a second row

Use 3D, symmetric, to-scale cubes. Build them as a skin on `blocks` with the same names and actions, so family 2's lessons re-render without edits: a unit cube, a rod of ten cubes, a flat of ten rods, isometric at one fixed angle, three tints of the row's ink on the three faces, cube lines visible, piles arranged as the app arranges them. Two additions this family needs: `trade` upward (`{"do":"trade","row":"n","from":"ones","n":10}`: ten pieces turn warm, slide together, fuse into one piece of the next place, slide left; counts and digits update; the total label does not), specified by place value but not yet in the kit; and a second `row` in one chart with `{"do":"join","from":"m","into":"n"}` sliding the rust pieces into the teal piles place by place, so an addition is watched before it is written. `remove` takes pieces away in rust. No thousand block is drawn: from four digits up, the stack is the picture.

### 4.3 `numberline`: hops, landing, distance

Place value's `numberline` (ends, ticks, step arcs, halfway, drop, zoom) plus: `"open": true` draws no ticks, only the numbers landed on; `{"do":"hop","from":68,"by":-30,"at":"back"}` draws one arc to scale, labeled with the signed amount, landing on a dot that writes its number; `"count": true` numbers the hops under their arcs and draws the start as a hollow mark so it is never counted; `{"do":"distance","from":44,"to":60}` draws the bracket with "?" and resolves it when the arcs beneath it are complete; two hops of opposite direction in one shot for the jump story, numbered 1 and 2. Labels never overlap: when two land close, the later takes a leader. Serves families 1 and 20 as well.

### 4.4 `bond`

```json
{ "id": "bd", "bond": "B", "whole": 7, "parts": [null, 6], "counters": true }
```

A whole circle above two part circles with links; `null` draws "?". `counters: true` fills a circle with dots to scale (up to ten, in two rows of five like the frame); otherwise the numeral. Parts: `bd.whole`, `bd.part.1`, `bd.part.2`. Actions: `split` (dots in the whole separate into the two parts; the whole's label stays), `merge` (the reverse), `fill` (a "?" resolves on the word), `ask` (a circle goes "?" gold), `facts` (the four facts appear beside the bond in the parts' colors, one per word), `stretch` (the circles morph into the part-whole bar of 4.6).

### 4.5 Frames, and counters that can be anything

`counters` gains `"thing"` (a small glyph set the size of a counter: dog, apple, star, house, almond, fish) and `{"do":"as","target":"a","shape":"round"}`, which morphs them into the app's counters on the word. `grid` gains `"frame": true` for the ten frame's heavier outline and two named targets: `fa.empty` (its empty cells, highlightable gold) and `fa.full` (a 10 label that appears when every cell is filled). `{"do":"fuse","target":"fa"}` turns a full frame into one rod beside the chart, used once where make ten meets place value.

### 4.6 The part-whole bar (first form of family 8's `bar`)

```json
{ "id": "pw", "partbar": "B", "parts": [{ "v": 209, "tex": "209" }, { "v": 145, "tex": "145" }], "total": "?" }
```

Two or three parts end to end at lengths proportional to their values, labels inside (a label that does not fit goes above with a leader), a bracket over the whole with its total or "?". `join` slides a part in; `remove` slides a part out and the bracket shrinks to what stays. This is not the existing percent `bar` (one whole, equal partitions, two scales); it is unequal labeled parts. Built now at this depth; family 8 adds comparison bars, copies and the change story.

### 4.7 `dim`

From the place-value spec: one beat, a set of choices, one reason.

### Build order

1. `stack` borrow and alignment: small, and the core of every grade 2 to 4 type.
2. `blocks` trade up, second row, cubes skin: unlocks `add-ones-regroup` and the regroup types, and re-renders family 2.
3. `bond`, frame targets, counters as things: the five kindergarten types and the fact types of grades 1 and 2.
4. `numberline` hops and distance: eight types here, plus families 1 and 20.
5. The part-whole bar: `bag-totals-chain`, `three-addend-story`, and the seed of family 8.

## 5. Who writes what

This division of work depends on a strong concept, a kit that fits, then a strong review and an independent sample review. Here it splits by whether a lesson's quality lives in the method or in the understanding.

**Strongest writer** (the understanding is the lesson):
- Every kindergarten type. The whole film is foundations said for the first time to a five-year-old; the voice, the pace and "a counter can be anything" are the hard part, and nothing can be assumed.
- The first lesson of each picture: `add-ones-regroup` (the first trade, cubes and stack in one beat), `subtract-with-regrouping` (the chain across zero), `number-line-missing-hop` (subtraction as a distance), `subtract-through-ten` (the second number broken before any taking).
- Types where two true methods compete and the lesson must choose honestly for the numbers: `subtract-two-digit-regroup` (100 − 86 by counting up, 73 − 48 by the algorithm), `make-hundred` (why 55 and not 65).
- The reading types: `bag-totals-chain`, `three-addend-story`, `number-line-jump-story`; and `related-subtraction-sentence`, whose point is the fact family, not the minus sign.

**A capable cheaper writer**, from the per-type concept, with the kit complete and the first lesson of its picture approved (one method, one component with named actions, arithmetic the kit computes and `--dry` checks):
- The algorithm types: `add-two-digit-regroup`, `add-several-two-digit`, `add-with-regrouping`, `add-three-digit`, `subtract-three-digit`, `add-multidigit`, `subtract-multidigit`. References: `decimal-sum-difference` and the reviewed first-trade lesson.
- The grade 1 and 2 fact types once the kindergarten picture has landed: `add-within-20`, `subtract-within-20`, `make-ten-fact`, `related-subtraction`.
- The number-line types once `missing-hop` is approved: `count-on-back`, `counted-steps`, `number-line-hops`.

The risk to test first is voice, not math: cheaper models rush and reach for big words at grades 1 and 2. The blind test runs per grade band, independent reviewers ranking films, before anything on the second list moves.

## 6. What in the problem types should change, and what the standards ask that no problem practices

> Base cases and trick questions (7 − 7, 8 − 0) are wanted in the app. Flagging one here means it is a weak choice for a lesson's example, never that the app should drop it.

### Problem types

- **Degenerate film seeds**: `take-away-within-10` (7 − 7), `add-within-20` (19 + 0), `facts-to-five` (0 + 3), `subtract-within-20` (8 − 0). Seed the lessons with something to see: 7 − 3; 8 + 7 and 15 − 7 (both crossing ten); 4 + 1 and 5 − 2.
- **`related-subtraction-sentence`**: the decoys are 15 + 5, 15 × 5 and 15 ÷ 5, so the item is solved by finding the minus sign, and two decoys use symbols a grade 2 child has not met (3.OA). Make the decoys subtractions that do not come from the fact (10 − 5 = n, 15 − 7 = n, 20 − 5 = n); then the item tests the fact family. "Use n for the unknown" is grade 3's letter (3.OA.D.8); grade 2 uses a box.
- **`count-on-back`**: both sampled variants count by 1. If that is all the type does, widen to 2 through 5; the standard's own example is "counting on 2 to add 2".
- **`make-ten-remainder`, `subtract-through-ten`**: procedure fragments (the answer is the leftover, not the sum or difference). Fine as scaffolds; their lessons finish the fact.
- **`make-ten-fact`, `related-subtraction`** (grade 2) pose the same problems as grade 1's `add-within-20` and `subtract-within-20`. Keep both skills (strategy, then fluency from memory), but one per-type concept serves each pair, the grade 2 lesson briefer and faster.
- **`number-line-jump-story`**: one choice is truncated in the facts output ("Student A has 46 dollars. She sp"); check the generator and the card. The stories are dollars under a skill linked to 2.MD.B.5, which is about lengths.
- **`add-multidigit`, `subtract-multidigit`**: answers print without commas (123417) while the prompts use them; the checker must accept both.
- **`bag-totals-chain`** is linked to 4.NBT.B.4 but stays within 1,000; its standards are 3.NBT.A.2 and 3.OA.D.8.
- **Naming**: the grade 1 skill `add-two-digit` and the grade 2 type `add-two-digit-regroup` are easily confused; rename the skill `add-ones-to-two-digit`.
- **Grade band**: nothing leaks up or down. `subtract-two-digit-regroup` reaching 100 on top is right for 2.NBT.B.5 and gives counting up its best case.

### Standard clauses no problem type practices

The lessons show these anyway; each is also a candidate for a new type.

- **K.OA.A.3** "decompose numbers less than or equal to 10 into pairs in more than one way": `number-bond-missing` asks one missing part; no type asks for two bonds of one number.
- **K.OA.A.2** "solve addition and subtraction word problems": the kindergarten prompts are counter sentences, not stories.
- **1.OA.B.3** the commutative example, "If 8 + 3 = 11 is known, then 3 + 8 = 11 is also known": no type turns a fact around; the associative example is practiced by `three-addend-story`.
- **1.OA.B.4** "subtraction as an unknown-addend problem": practiced only through `related-subtraction-sentence`'s matching; no type asks "10 − 8 by finding the number that makes 10 when added to 8" in its own form.
- **1.OA.C.6** "creating equivalent but easier or known sums (e.g., adding 6 + 7 by creating the known equivalent 6 + 6 + 1)": no doubles or near-doubles type.
- **1.OA.D.8** "the unknown whole number in an addition or subtraction equation ... 5 = □ – 3": only addends are hidden (`number-bond-missing`, `make-hundred`); an unknown start or unknown amount taken is never asked.
- **1.NBT.C.4** "adding a two-digit number and a multiple of 10": `add-ones-regroup` adds ones only, and family 2's `ten-more-or-less` adds exactly 10; 68 + 30 is asked nowhere at grade 1.
- **2.OA.A.1** "comparing, with unknowns in all positions": only `number-line-jump-story` serves this standard here, and it matches rather than solves. Family 8's `addition-stories` and `two-step-stories` must be checked for "how many more" with the unknown in every position.
- **2.MD.B.5** "word problems involving lengths that are given in the same units": every story in `number-line-arithmetic` is money. A length variant would make the link true.
- **2.MD.B.6** "represent whole numbers as lengths from 0": hops start at 20 or 68, never at 0; the lesson draws the length from 0 once.
- **2.NBT.B.9** "explain why addition and subtraction strategies work", and the "explain the reasoning used" and "relate the strategy to a written method" clauses of 1.NBT.C.4 and 2.NBT.B.7: this is what the films do; no problem type can ask for it.
- **3.OA.D.8** "equations with a letter standing for the unknown quantity" and "assess the reasonableness of answers using ... rounding": `bag-totals-chain` asks for a number only. The lesson estimates and writes the equation; the app could add a "which equation" variant.
