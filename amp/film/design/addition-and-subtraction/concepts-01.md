# Addition and subtraction, part 1: lesson concepts

Ten problem types, kindergarten and grade 1, written from the family design. None is multiple choice, so no concept has a **Rule out**. The five habits (read, at a glance, sure fast way, sense, check) run through every one; here the "at a glance" beat is a child's: "eight needs two", "seven is more than the five loose ones".

Two things true of the whole set, said once:

- **Every kindergarten film opens by making the picture out of things.** Dogs, birds, apples, blocks appear one at a time and each turns into the app's counter (round teal for the first group, square rust for the second, exactly as the problem draws them). Use ten to fifteen seconds to connect the real object to the counter before working the example.
- **Pace.** K films run about ninety seconds to two and a half minutes; grade 1 two to three. Numbers as words, one motion per beat, the motion finished before the next word. Nothing is struck out in K or grade 1; the lesson says what to do instead.

One correction to the family design: K.OA.A.2's word-problem clause *is* practiced at kindergarten, by `number-bond-missing`'s stories (ducks, apples, birds). It is `join-within-10` and `take-away-within-10` that stay counter sentences.

## Kindergarten

### `join-within-10` — How many altogether

**Standard.** K.OA.A.2: "add and subtract within 10, e.g., by using objects or drawings to represent the problem." K.OA.A.1: "Represent addition ... with objects ... drawings." The prompts are counter sentences, so the word-problem clause of K.OA.A.2 is not practiced here (alignment note, not a fault).

**Foundations first** (about 25 s). (1) A counter is one thing you count: five dogs trot in one at a time, each turns into a round teal counter; "a counter can be anything you count: dogs, apples, houses." (2) *Join*: two groups pushed together are one group; "altogether" means that one group. (3) From family 1, one line: the last number you say is how many.

**Read.** "There are 3 round counters and 5 square counters. How many counters altogether?" *Altogether* goes gold: push the groups together and count the one group. At a glance: the answer is more than 3 and more than 5; the counters are already two colors, nothing to sort.

**The example.** 3 round and 5 square: the bigger group comes second, so the fast way shows itself without naming any property. (1) Three teal rounds, label 3; five rust squares, label 5, as the app draws them. (2) Join: the squares slide against the rounds into one row sitting in a ten frame. Count all: as each counter is said, its numeral appears under it, one to eight; the row's label reads 8. (3) The faster way: start with the bigger group. The squares' label 5 goes gold: "five: we already know." Count on along the rounds: six, seven, eight, numerals under them. Same 8. (4) Eight.
Quick case: 1 round and 9 square. Stand on nine; one more: ten. The frame is full and its label flips to 10 ("a full frame is ten", family 2's line, one beat).

**Fast.** Hold the bigger group's number; count on along the smaller group.

**Sense.** Altogether is more than either group.

**Check.** Count on from the other group (three: four, five, six, seven, eight), or count all once.

**Line.** "A counter can be anything you count. Start on the bigger number and count on."

**Trap.** Saying the start again for the first new counter ("five, six, seven" → 7). The 5 is the group's label, not a counter; "six" is written under the first round counter. In the quick case, counting all ten from one and losing the place: the frame shows ten without counting.

**Kit.** Family 4.5 (counters as things, `as`, `frame`, `fa.full`). One addition: a numeral written under each counter on the word that counts it (`count` numbering the pieces, not only the group label), with `"from": 6` for counting on.

**Writer.** Strongest. The lesson depends on age-appropriate language and making the first picture meaningful.

### `take-away-within-10` — How many are left

**Standard.** K.OA.A.2: "add and subtract within 10, e.g., by using objects or drawings." K.OA.A.1: "acting out situations." One wording. The film seed 7 − 7 is a base case the app keeps and a poor main example; seed 7 take away 3.

**Foundations first** (about 20 s). (1) Seven birds land on a fence and become seven round teal counters in a row. (2) *Take away*: some leave the group. *Left* means the ones still here. (3) What leaves does not vanish: it waits beside the group, faint, so the start can be seen again.

**Read.** "There are 7 counters. Take away 3. How many are left?" *Left* gold: the counters still here, not the ones that went. At a glance: fewer than 7.

**The example.** 7 take away 3. (1) Seven teal counters, label 7. (2) Three turn rust and slide out together to a muted pile at the right, labeled 3 in muted ink. (3) Count what stayed: one, two, three, four; label 4. (4) Four. (5) Look at both piles: 4 here and 3 gone are the 7 we began with. A bond appears under the picture: 7 over 4 and 3. "Take away shows a number's parts."

**Fast.** Count what stays. If you already know 7 is 3 and 4, say it.

**Sense.** What is left is fewer than the start.

**Check.** What stayed and what left make the start: four and three make seven (the muted pile slides back for a beat, the label reads 7, then it slides out again).

**Line.** "What stayed and what left make the start."

**Trap.** Answering the number taken (3). "Left" is the gold word; the taken counters are faint and outside the group; the count label counts only the bright ones. A child who crosses out from both ends and recounts: here the leavers go in one slide, from one end.

**Kit.** Counters that leave to a muted pile (`move` plus muted ink, exists); `bond` under the picture (4.4).

**Writer.** Strongest (kindergarten voice).

### `number-bond-missing` — The missing part (pilot 1)

**Standard.** K.OA.A.3: "Decompose numbers less than or equal to 10 into pairs in more than one way ... record each decomposition by a drawing or equation (e.g., 5 = 2 + 3 and 5 = 4 + 1)." K.OA.A.2's word problems (ducks, apples, birds: a whole, one part, "the rest"). The box equations in both positions are 1.OA.D.8's "8 + ? = 11", a reach into grade 1 that is right to keep. Three forms of one bond: the equation with a box (box first, or whole first), the story, and the bond drawn with no words.

**Foundations first** (about 35 s). (1) A number is made of parts: seven apples in a bowl, four red, three green, become counters; they separate downward into two circles under a circle labeled 7. "This is a number bond: the whole on top, its parts under. Seven is four and three." (2) Merge back up; split another way: five and two. "Same whole, different parts." (3) The equal sign, once: "= means the same amount on both sides." 4 + 3 = 7 written in the circles' colors: two parts on one side, the whole on the other.

**Read.** Find the whole: the number alone on its side of =, or "all of them" in the story (3 ducks, 10 birds). The other number is a part. "The rest" means the other part. At a glance: the missing part is smaller than the whole; when the whole is ten, read the part off a ten frame.

**Example A** (the equation). □ + 6 = 7. (1) Read: the box plus six is the same as seven. Student: "Thirteen?" Teacher: seven stands alone on its side, so seven is the whole. The bond: 7 on top, six counters in one part, "?" gold in the other. (2) A part is never bigger than the whole; thirteen cannot go in. (3) Six, and how many more make seven? Count on: seven. One. A counter drops into the "?". (4) The box is 1. (5) 1 + 6 = 7 written under the bond, read aloud: one and six is the same as seven.
Quick B: 8 = 6 + □. Eight stands alone: the whole. Same bond, 8 on top, six in a part; count on: seven, eight: 2.
**Example C** (the story; a different act, words to picture). "10 birds sit on a fence. 6 are big and the rest are small. How many are small?" Ten birds become ten counters: the whole. Six slide into the "big" circle. "The rest" slide into the other circle: count them: four. On a ten frame beside: six filled, four empty; the partner of six. 4. Check: six and four make ten.
One beat for the wordless form: "Sometimes the app shows only the bond. The top circle is the whole."

**Fast.** Find the whole; count on from the known part to it. Whole of ten: the empties.

**Sense.** The missing part is smaller than the whole.

**Check.** Say the bond: part and part make the whole; read the equation with the number in the box.

**Line.** "Two parts make the whole; the whole hides a part. Equals means the same amount on both sides."

**Trap.** 13 for □ + 6 = 7 and 14 for 8 = 6 + □: adding the two numbers shown. The whole goes on top before anything else happens, and a part cannot be bigger than it. The student says "thirteen"; the teacher points at the top circle. This is the one place in the family where the cast earns its keep.

**Kit.** `bond` (split, merge, ask, fill) with counters in two rows of five to scale; the cast (exists); `frame`. Nothing beyond 4.4 and 4.5.

**Writer.** Strongest: the pilot lesson, the equal sign said for the first time, and the student voice.

### `ten-frame-complement` — How many more fill the frame

**Standard.** K.OA.A.4: "For any number from 1 to 9, find the number that makes 10 when added to the given number ... record the answer with a drawing or equation."

**Foundations first** (about 20 s). The ten frame drawn live: a row of five places, then a row of five under it; "five and five is ten." A full frame is ten. A counter takes a place; an empty place needs one.

**Read.** "How many more counters will fill this ten-frame?" *More* gold: the question is about the empty places, not the counters. At a glance: top row full means five are there, and the empties are all in the bottom row; the fuller the frame, the smaller the answer.

**The example.** Seven counters, the app's picture. (1) Read the frame without counting one by one: the top row is full, five; two more below: seven. (2) The empties go gold; count them: one, two, three. (3) Three rust counters slide in one at a time; the frame is full; its label flips to 10. (4) Three. (5) Record it: 7 + 3 = 10.
Quick case: two counters. The empties are a whole row, five, and three more: eight. 2 + 8 = 10. The empties can outnumber the counters.

**Fast.** Count the empty places.

**Sense.** Nearly full, a small answer; nearly empty, a big one.

**Check.** Counters and empties make ten: seven and three; the frame fills with none left over.

**Line.** "Seven needs three. Read the partner off the empties."

**Trap.** Answering 7 (the counters) or 10 (the full frame). Only the empties are gold, and "more" is the gold word.

**Kit.** `frame: true`, `fa.empty`, `fa.full` (4.5). Nothing else.

**Writer.** Strongest for the first kindergarten wave, as the family design says; but its one-move method makes it the K type to blind-test a cheaper writer on first.

### `facts-to-five` — Add and take away within 5

**Standard.** K.OA.A.5: "Fluently add and subtract within 5." The film seed 0 + 3 and the sampled 3 − 3 are base cases the app keeps; the lesson seeds 4 + 1 and 5 − 2 and gives the base case one beat.

**Foundations first** (about 30 s). (1) Five is a row: the top row of the ten frame, five places, the fingers of one hand. (2) The parts of five, seen not counted: five counters in the row split by a gold gap into 4 | 1, then 3 | 2, a small bond beside each. (3) The signs, once: + means put together; − means take away.

**Read.** The sign says what happens. Plus: the two numbers are parts, the answer is bigger than both. Minus: the first number is the whole, the answer is smaller. At a glance: within five, the answer is a part you can already see.

**Example A.** 4 + 1. Four teal in the row; one rust slides into the last place; the row is full: five. 4 + 1 = 5. No counting: four, and the row is full.
**Example B** (a different act). 5 − 2. Five fill the row; two turn rust and slide out faint; three stay: 5 − 2 = 3. The bond 5 over 3 and 2: take one part away and the other part stays.
Base case, one beat: 3 − 3: all three leave; nothing stays; the label reads 0. "Zero means none."

**Fast.** Know the parts of five. Plus: see which part is missing. Minus: take one part, the other stays.

**Sense.** Plus grows, minus shrinks; zero when everything is taken.

**Check.** The other fact of the same bond: 3 + 2 = 5 checks 5 − 2 = 3; 5 − 1 = 4 checks 4 + 1 = 5.

**Line.** "Within five, see it. Don't count it."

**Trap.** On a minus, answering the number taken (5 − 2 → 2), and 3 − 3 → 3 (nothing left to count, so the last number heard is said). The taken counters are faint and outside; the label counts only what stays and reads 0 when nothing does.

**Kit.** `grid [5,1]` with `frame`, `bond`. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Strongest (kindergarten voice); the shortest film in the family, about ninety seconds.

## Grade 1

### `add-within-20` — Add two numbers

**Standard.** 1.OA.C.6: "Add and subtract within 20 ... Use strategies such as counting on; making ten (e.g., 8 + 6 = 8 + 2 + 4 = 10 + 4 = 14) ... and creating equivalent but easier or known sums (e.g., adding 6 + 7 by creating the known equivalent 6 + 6 + 1)." The film seed 19 + 0 is a base case; seed 8 + 7. Two cases past ten: both addends under ten (make a ten) and one addend already a teen (the sampled 5 + 12: the ten is already made). Sums within ten are kindergarten's count on, named in one line.

**Foundations first** (about 30 s). (1) Two ten frames side by side. "A teen number is a full frame and some more": fifteen is a full frame and five (family 2's K line). (2) From kindergarten: eight fills eight places; the two empties are what it needs. (3) Breaking a number does not change it: seven drawn as 2 | 5 with a gold gap, label still 7.

**Read.** "What is 8 + 7?" At a glance: eight needs two, and seven has more than two, so the sum passes ten: a teen number.

**Example A.** 8 + 7. (1) Eight teal in the left frame, its two empties gold; seven rust in the right. (2) Break the seven where ten is: 2 | 5; its label stays 7. (3) Fill: the two slide into the empties; the left frame is full, label 10; the five stay, label 5. (4) Ten and five: fifteen. On the board, in the counters' colors: 8 + 7 = 8 + 2 + 5 = 10 + 5 = 15.
**Example B** (a different act). 5 + 12. (1) Twelve is a full frame and two: the ten is already made. (2) The five join the two: two and five is seven. (3) Ten and seven: seventeen. Nothing to break; the ten rides along.
One line for small sums: 6 + 3: stand on six; seven, eight, nine.

**Fast.** Both under ten and the sum passes ten: find the first number's partner to ten and break the second there. One number a teen: add the ones, keep the ten.

**Sense.** 8 + 7 is past 8 + 2 = 10 and under 10 + 10: "ten and some."

**Check.** Another sure way: the near double. 7 + 7 is 14, one more: 15. For B, count the counters in both frames: a full frame and seven.

**Line.** "Eight needs two. Fill the ten, then add the rest."

**Trap.** Making the ten and stopping (8 + 7 → 10), or breaking the seven in the wrong place. The seven is cut 2 | 5 before anything moves, its label stays 7 until the two have landed, and the five sit unmoved, visibly five. In B, counting on twelve hops from five and losing count: stand on the bigger number; a full frame is a ten you never count.

**Kit.** Two frames with `fa.empty` and `fa.full`; a gold gap (a short `seg`) inside a group; a group label that stays while the group splits. Nothing beyond 4.5.

**Writer.** Cheaper, once `ten-frame-complement` and `number-bond-missing` are approved and the frame kit exists; `make-ten-remainder` is the reference.

### `make-ten-remainder` — What is left after making ten

**Standard.** 1.OA.C.6, the making-ten clause: "making ten (e.g., 8 + 6 = 8 + 2 + 4 = 10 + 4 = 14)." A procedure fragment: the answer is the leftover piece of the second number, not the sum. Fine as a scaffold; the lesson finishes the fact. Use `make-ten-remainder` as the reference screenplay when applying this concept to the frame kit.

**Foundations first.** The same 30 s as `add-within-20`: a teen is a full frame and some; the partner to ten is read off the empties; breaking a number keeps it.

**Read.** "To add 9 + 6, move 1 from 6 to make ten. How much of 6 is left?" *Left* gold: the question is about the second number only, after the ten has taken its share. At a glance: one less than six; smaller than six, not the sum.

**The example.** 9 + 6. (1) Nine in the frame, one empty gold: nine needs one. (2) The six break 1 | 5, label still 6. (3) The one slides in; the frame is full, label 10. (4) The five stay, label 5. Five of the six are left. (5) Check: one moved and five left make six. Then, using the answer: ten and five: 9 + 6 = 15. The fragment is the middle of a fact the child finishes.

**Fast.** The leftover is the second number minus the first number's partner to ten.

**Sense.** Smaller than the second number by exactly what moved.

**Check.** Moved and left make the second number.

**Line.** "Nine needs one. The rest waits."

**Trap.** Answering 15 (the sum) or 1 (what moved). "Left" is gold; the five sit as their own group with their own label.

**Kit.** 4.5 frames.

**Writer.** Use the reference screenplay for a frame-kit adaptation.

### `subtract-within-20` — Take one number from another

**Standard.** 1.OA.C.6: "decomposing a number leading to a ten (e.g., 13 – 4 = 13 – 3 – 1 = 10 – 1 = 9); using the relationship between addition and subtraction (e.g., knowing that 8 + 4 = 12, one knows 12 – 8 = 4)." 1.OA.B.4: "Understand subtraction as an unknown-addend problem." The sampled 8 − 0 is a base case; seed 15 − 7.

**Foundations first** (about 35 s). (1) Fifteen: a full frame and five loose. (2) The bond: 15 − 7 asks "fifteen is seven and what?": 15 on top, 7 in one part, "?" gold in the other. (3) Breaking the number you take does not change it.

**Read.** "What is 15 − 7?" At a glance: seven is more than the five loose ones, so the answer is under ten; it is the other part of fifteen.

**The example.** 15 − 7, taking away through ten. (1) The seven to take, rust, broken where ten is: 5 | 2, label 7. (2) The five loose ones leave, faint: ten left. (3) Two more leave the frame: eight. 15 − 7 = 15 − 5 − 2 = 10 − 2 = 8. (4) Eight. Check: eight and seven make fifteen; the ghosts slide back and the label reads 15.
Quick case, the same bond read the other way: 13 − 9. Ask "nine and what make thirteen?" Nine needs one to reach ten, then three: four. When the number taken is near ten, counting up is the shorter road; both roads are one bond. One line for the base case: 8 − 0 takes nothing, so eight stay.

**Fast.** Is the number taken more than the loose ones? Take to ten, then the rest. Or ask "what goes with it?" and count up through ten. Pick the shorter.

**Sense.** Under ten when the number taken passes the loose ones; a part, so smaller than the start.

**Check.** Add back: the answer and the number taken make the start.

**Line.** "Take to ten, then the rest. Or ask: what goes with it?"

**Trap.** 17 − 8 → 2: after taking seven to reach ten, taking the whole eight again. The eight is broken 7 | 1 first; the one waits in gold and is all that is left to take. Also counting back with the start as the first number ("fifteen, fourteen..."): the start is a label, not a counter.

**Kit.** Frames, `bond`, the ghost pile, the gold gap. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Cheaper, once `subtract-through-ten` is approved. The only place a cheaper model may muddle is the two-ways beat, so the concept fixes it: through ten for 15 − 7, count up for 13 − 9, and say why.

### `subtract-through-ten` — How many more to take

**Standard.** 1.OA.C.6: "decomposing a number leading to a ten (e.g., 13 – 4 = 13 – 3 – 1 = 10 – 1 = 9)." A fragment: the answer is the second step (1), not the difference. Fine as a scaffold; the lesson finishes the fact after the answer.

**Foundations first** (about 30 s). (1) Seventeen: a full frame and seven loose. (2) "Reach ten": the loose ones leave and the full frame stays. (3) The number to take, eight, is one amount in two pieces. It is broken where ten is, 7 | 1, before anything moves; its label stays 8.

**Read.** "To find 17 − 8, first take away 7 to reach ten. How many more must you take away?" *More* gold. Eight is the whole amount to take; seven of it is gone; the question asks for the rest of the eight. At a glance: eight take seven: one.

**The example.** (1) Seventeen drawn: full frame, seven loose. (2) The eight in rust beside it, broken 7 | 1. (3) The seven loose ones leave, and the seven of the rust pile fade with them: ten left. (4) The one waits, gold: one more must come out of the frame. (5) One. Check: seven taken and one more make eight; all of the eight is taken. Then, using the answer: that one leaves the frame; nine. 17 − 8 = 9.

**Fast.** The rest of the number taken: 8 − 7.

**Sense.** Small: the loose ones took most of the eight.

**Check.** Taken so far and still to take make the number taken.

**Line.** "Break the number you take where ten is. Take the first piece, then the rest."

**Trap.** Taking eight again (answering 8, or ending at 2), or answering 9 (the difference) or 10 (what was reached). The eight is broken before anything moves, and the one is the only rust counter left standing.

**Kit.** Frames, ghost pile, gold gap. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Strongest: the first lesson of "break the second number before any taking", the picture `subtract-within-20` inherits.

### `three-addend-story` — Three groups, one total

**Standard.** 1.OA.A.2: "Solve word problems that call for addition of three whole numbers whose sum is less than or equal to 20." 1.OA.B.3: "To add 2 + 6 + 4, the second two numbers can be added to make a ten." 1.OA.C.6 making ten. Two cases: a pair makes ten (most variants; the pair is not always next to each other: 3 + 7 + 7, 6 + 4 + 7) and no pair makes ten (3 + 1 + 5; 6 + 3 + 1). The bare form 6 + 2 + 8 reads exactly like a story.

**Foundations first** (about 35 s). (1) Three groups are a whole with three parts: a bond with three circles under one. (2) Order: the three groups' counters merge up into the whole in one order, then in another; the whole's label never changes. "Add in any order. The whole stays." (3) A pair that makes ten fills a frame.

**Read.** "A box holds 3 red blocks, 7 blue blocks, and 7 green blocks. How many blocks?" *How many blocks* asks for all of them: the whole. At a glance: look for a pair that makes ten: three and seven. A ten inside means a teen; the answer is more than any two groups together.

**The example.** (1) Three groups of blocks become counters: 3 teal, 7 rust, 7 blue, each labeled; the bond with "?" gold on top and three parts. (2) Look for ten: the 3 and the 7 go gold and slide into a frame: full, 10. (3) The seven green join: ten and seven, seventeen; the "?" becomes 17. (4) Written in the counters' colors: 3 + 7 + 7 = 10 + 7 = 17. Check by another order: 7 + 7 = 14, and three more: 17. Same whole.
Quick case: "A team scored 3 points, then 1 point, then 5 points." No pair makes ten. Add any two: three and one is four; count on five: nine. Under ten, nothing to fill. One beat: "then" means more points came; all three land in one box.

**Fast.** Find a pair that makes ten and add it first; then the third. No pair: add two, count on the third.

**Sense.** More than any two groups together; a ten inside means a teen number.

**Check.** Add in a different order. The whole is the same.

**Line.** "Look for ten first. Any order; the whole stays."

**Trap.** Stopping at the pair (3 + 7 = 10) or adding only two groups. The "?" on top stays gold until all three groups have joined, and the third group sits apart with its own label until it moves.

**Kit.** `bond` with three parts (4.4 specifies two; extend). A third ink for the third group (blue, exists). Counters as things (blocks draw well; the writer picks a story whose things can be drawn, not pages).

**Writer.** Strongest: a reading type, and the lesson must show the whole bond before any adding so that order is a choice the child sees, not a rule they are told.

## Kit beyond the family's tools, collected

- A numeral written under each counter on the word that counts it, with a `from` for counting on (`join-within-10`, `add-within-20`).
- `bond` with three parts (`three-addend-story`).
- A `thing` glyph set that covers the stories the app generates (dogs, birds, ducks, apples, blocks, fish); the writer may change a story's things to ones that draw.

Everything else is in sections 4.4 and 4.5 of the family design.
