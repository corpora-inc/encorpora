# Addition and subtraction, part 3: regrouping to hundreds, the number line, thousands, multidigit

Ten problem types in four skills, grade 2 to grade 4. Before the concepts, four things to say about the types themselves.

- **Two pairs are one problem each.** `add-with-regrouping` (grade 2, 257 + 256) and `add-three-digit` (grade 3, 439 + 198) are the same case: two carries. `subtract-with-regrouping` (302 − 117) and `subtract-three-digit` (1,000 − 863) are both a borrow across zero. That is right by the standards (2.NBT.B.7 is "concrete models ... relate the strategy to a written method"; 3.NBT.A.2 is fluency with "strategies and algorithms"), so one concept serves each pair with two renderings: at grade 2 the cubes stand beside the stack and every trade is watched before it is written; at grade 3 the stack is the whole picture and the new things are the estimate first, the near-round shortcut, counting up from a round number, and the check by the reverse operation. Written below as four concepts, but the grade 3 ones are the grade 2 ones grown, and say so.
- **`bag-totals-chain`** is linked to 4.NBT.B.4; it serves 3.OA.D.8 and 3.NBT.A.2 and nothing of grade 4. One variant's total (1,004 beads) steps over the "within 1000" of 3.NBT.A.2; harmless, but the lesson's examples stay under 1,000.
- **`number-line-jump-story`** has a truncated choice in the facts output, is a money story under a lengths standard (2.MD.B.5), and matches rather than solves (2.OA.A.1 asks to solve). Its real standard is 2.MD.B.6, "represent whole-number sums and differences within 100 on a number line diagram".
- **`add-multidigit`, `subtract-multidigit`**: the prompts print commas, the answers do not (123417). The checker must accept both, or the lesson, which writes 123,417, contradicts it.

Pictures for this part, from the family design: the cubes chart with two rows and the trade (grade 2 only); the stack with headers, the carry small above its column, the borrow struck with its new digit directly above it in the same column, the estimate in muted ink beside it (grades 2 to 4); the open number line with hops to scale and the distance bracket (grade 2 and 1,000 − 863); the part-whole bar (grade 3 stories). Scratch paper, said once per lesson: a stick for a ten and a dot for a one beside the cubes (grade 2); the stack with its carries and the estimate (grades 3 and 4); a line with arcs (number-line types).

## Regroup hundreds (grade 2)

### `add-with-regrouping` — Add three-digit numbers, trading twice

**Standard.** 2.NBT.B.7: "Add and subtract within 1000, using concrete models or drawings and strategies based on place value ... relate the strategy to a written method. Understand that in adding ... three-digit numbers, one adds ... hundreds and hundreds, tens and tens, ones and ones; and sometimes it is necessary to compose ... tens or hundreds." 2.NBT.B.9 is what the film does (explains why the carry works); no problem can ask it.

**Foundations first.** One line from family 2: ten of these make one of those, and the amount never changes. Then the chart with two rows: 257 in teal cubes (2 flats, 5 rods, 7 cubes), 256 in rust, digits under the piles. Beside it the stack with headers, hundreds | tens | ones, and the lesson says the stack *is* the chart written down: each column of the stack is a column of the chart. Define the carry before it happens: when a column makes ten, those ten fuse into one piece of the next column, and we write it there as a small 1, because it now lives there. "Tens with tens, ones with ones. Ten ones make a ten."

**Read.** "Add: 257 + 256." The plus goes gold. The answer is bigger than both; two hundreds and two hundreds are four, and the tens will fill another, so it lands in the five hundreds.

**The example.** 257 + 256, the given variant: it carries twice, into the tens and into the hundreds, so one problem shows both trades. (1) Ones: the 6 rust cubes slide into the teal ones pile: 13 cubes; the count label reads 13. Ten of them turn warm, fuse into a rod, and slide left into the tens pile; in the same beat the stack writes 3 under the ones and the small 1 above the tens. "Thirteen is one ten and three. The ten goes with the tens." (2) Tens: 5 rods join 5 rods and the carried rod: 11; ten fuse into a flat and slide to the hundreds; the stack writes 1 and the small 1 above the hundreds. (3) Hundreds: 2, 2, and the carried flat: 5. (4) The piles read 5 flats, 1 rod, 3 cubes: 513; the stack reads 513 under its headers. Scratch paper: two rows of sticks and dots, with the fuse drawn as a ring around ten dots.

**Fast.** Right to left, each column its own sum; write the ones digit of the column, carry the ten on the word. No digit trick is taught; "add the digits" is only true when nothing carries.

**Sense.** The five hundreds, as the glance said.

**Check.** Count the finished piles: five hundreds, one ten, three ones, 513. Then add the stack the other way round (256 + 257): same digits, same carries, same answer. (Subtracting a part back, 513 − 256, is grade 3's check.)

**Line.** "Tens with tens, ones with ones. Ten ones make a ten, and the ten goes with the tens."

**Trap.** Writing the whole 13 under the ones (51113) or forgetting a carry (413, 503). The ten cubes fuse into one rod that visibly has to go somewhere, and the 1 is written in the tens in the same beat the rod lands there. Never a carry written in the ones.

**Kit.** `blocks` with a second row and `join`, `trade` upward (4.2); `stack` with headers (exists). Nothing beyond the family's tools.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, once `add-ones-regroup` (the first trade, pilot 2) is approved: same picture, one place longer, every digit computed by the kit.

### `subtract-with-regrouping` — Subtract across a zero (pilot 3)

**Standard.** 2.NBT.B.7, the "decompose tens or hundreds" clause: "sometimes it is necessary to compose or decompose tens or hundreds"; "relate the strategy to a written method."

**Foundations first.** Define *open* on the cubes: a rod opens into ten cubes; a flat opens into ten rods; the amount never changes (family 2's trade, run downward). Then the two sentences the whole lesson stands on: "Can't take? Open a ten. No tens? Open a hundred." The stack with headers beside the chart; the lesson says the struck digit and the new digit above it are the opening, written down, and they stay in their own column.

**Read.** "Subtract: 302 − 117." The minus goes gold. What is left is smaller than 302. About three hundred take away about one hundred is about two hundred, and we take a little more than a hundred, so a little under 200.

**The example.** 302 − 117, the given variant: the ones need a ten and there are no tens, so the whole chain shows. (1) 302 as cubes: 3 flats, no rods, 2 cubes; the empty tens pile is drawn empty. (2) Ones: take 7 from 2. The pile shows two cubes; you cannot take seven from two. Open a ten: there are no rods. (3) Open a hundred, on its own word: one flat slides right and opens into ten rods in the tens pile; the stack strikes the 3, writes 2 above it, and writes 10 above the 0 of the tens. (4) Now open a ten: one rod slides right and opens into ten cubes: 12 cubes; the stack strikes the 10, writes 9 above it, and 12 above the 2. Nine rods stand in the tens pile; the stack's 9 is those rods. (5) Now take: seven cubes turn rust and leave, 5 stay; one rod leaves, 8 stay; one flat leaves, 1 stays. The stack writes 5, 8, 1, right to left. (6) 185.

**Fast.** Right to left; look at the column before you subtract: if the top is smaller, open the next place; if the next place is 0, open the one past it first, and the 0 becomes 9 because it got ten and gave one away.

**Sense.** A little under 200.

**Check.** Add back: 185 + 117 in the stack: 5 + 7 = 12, carry; 8 + 1 + 1 = 10, carry; 1 + 1 + 1 = 3: 302. The carries land exactly where the openings were. The piles agree: 1 flat, 8 rods, 5 cubes.

**Line.** "Can't take? Open a ten. No tens? Open a hundred first."

**Trap.** Three real ones. Taking the smaller digit from the larger whichever is on top (7 − 2 → 215): two cubes cannot lose seven. Writing 10 over the 0 without taking from the hundreds (295): the ten rods have to come from somewhere, and the flat visibly leaves. Forgetting the 10 becomes 9 (195): nine rods stand in the pile after one opens.

**Kit.** `stack` borrow drawn vertically with each trade on its own `trade` word, in order (4.1); `blocks` split downward two levels and `remove` in rust (4.2, `split` exists).

**Writer.** The strongest writer: the first lesson of the chain across zero; every later borrow lesson is written from it.

## Number line arithmetic (grade 2)

### `number-line-hops` — Hop tens, then ones

**Standard.** 2.MD.B.6: "Represent whole numbers as lengths from 0 on a number line diagram ... and represent whole-number sums and differences within 100 on a number line diagram." 2.NBT.B.5 (strategies based on place value).

**Foundations first.** What a number is on a line: a length from 0. Draw 0 and 20, and the arc from 0 to 20 labeled 20: twenty is that far. Then the open line: only the numbers you land on are written. Define a hop: an arc from one number to the next, labeled with how much and which way (+10, −1); a hop of ten is long and a hop of one is short, to scale. Define forward (to the right, more) and back (to the left, less). The start is a hollow mark: you stand on it, you do not count it.

**Read.** "Start at 68. Hop back 30, then back 3." "Back" goes gold: the landing is smaller than 68. At a glance: three tens back changes the tens digit, 68 to 38; then the ones.

**The example.** 68 back 30, back 3. (1) Hollow mark at 68. (2) Three long arcs back, each −10, drawn on the counting words "fifty-eight, forty-eight, thirty-eight", each landing dot writing its number; the hops are numbered 1, 2, 3 under their arcs. (3) Three short arcs back, each −1: 37, 36, 35, numbered 1, 2, 3. (4) The landing dot, 35, goes solid. Scratch paper: a line with 68 at the right, three long arcs and three short ones.

Quick second case, the other direction and the length from 0: "Start at 20. Hop forward 20, then forward 1." The length 0 to 20 is already drawn; two long arcs forward, 30, 40; one short, 41. Three or four beats; it is here because both directions are in the variants and "forward" must be seen to land bigger.

**Fast.** Hop the tens first, counting by tens aloud; then the ones. Back: tens digit down; forward: up.

**Sense.** Back lands smaller; 68 back three tens is about 38, and a few less.

**Check.** Hop back the other way: from 35, forward 3 is 38, forward 30 is 68.

**Line.** "Don't count the one you're standing on. Tens are long hops, ones are short."

**Trap.** Counting the start as the first hop ("sixty-eight, fifty-eight, forty-eight" → lands 48 for three tens). The start is hollow and unnumbered; only arcs carry numbers.

**Kit.** `numberline` with `open`, `hop`, `count` (4.3).

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, once `number-line-missing-hop` (the first lesson of this picture) is approved.

### `number-line-missing-hop` — How long is the hop? (pilot 4)

**Standard.** 2.MD.B.6, "represent whole-number ... differences within 100 on a number line diagram"; 2.NBT.B.5, "the relationship between addition and subtraction".

**Foundations first.** A hop has two ends and a length. Its length is how far apart the two numbers are, and that is a subtraction: 60 − 44 asks the same thing as "how far from 44 to 60?" The length is the same whichever way the hop goes, so we always measure it from the smaller number up. Define the distance bracket: a bracket between the two ends with "?" above it. The way to fill it: count up from the smaller number to the next ten, then by tens, then the ones left; the pieces add to the length.

**Read.** "A hop goes from 60 to 44. How long is the hop?" "How long" goes gold: the answer is a length, not a landing. At a glance: 44 is past 40, so the hop is less than 20; 44 to 54 is already ten, so it is more than ten.

**The example.** (1) The open line with 44 and 60; the bracket with "?" between them. (2) From 44, a short arc to 50, labeled +6: "forty-four needs six to reach fifty." (3) A long arc 50 to 60, labeled +10. (4) The pieces: 6 and 10 are 16; the "?" becomes 16. (5) On the board: 60 − 44 = 16, "the hop's length is a subtraction." Scratch paper: the line, two arcs, their labels added.

**Fast.** Count up: to the next ten, then by tens, then ones; add the pieces. It always works, and it is fastest when the big number is round or the two are close.

**Sense.** Between 10 and 20, as the glance said.

**Check.** Hop it the way the problem said: from 60, back 10 is 50, back 6 is 44. Or 44 + 16: 6 to 50, 10 to 60.

**Line.** "A hop's length is a subtraction: count up."

**Trap.** 24, from taking each digit's smaller from larger (6 − 4, 4 − 0), and 17, from counting the numbers on the line including both ends. The two arcs are labeled 6 and 10; the bracket can only read their sum.

**Kit.** `numberline` `hop` and `distance` (4.3).

**Writer.** The strongest writer: subtraction as a distance is new here, and `make-hundred`, `subtract-two-digit-regroup` (100 − 86) and 1,000 − 863 all stand on this lesson.

### `number-line-jump-story` — Which story matches the jumps? (multiple choice)

**Standard.** 2.MD.B.6 (sums and differences on a number line diagram). It is filed under 2.OA.A.1, which asks the learner to *solve* two-step problems; this type matches a picture to a story, so it practices the reading half of that standard only. The stories are money, not lengths, so the 2.MD.B.5 link is false; a length variant would make it true. One choice is cut off in the facts output: fix the generator or the card.

**Foundations first.** The number line can tell a story. A jump to the right is more: money earned, things added. A jump to the left is less: money spent, things taken away. The length of the jump is the amount. Jumps happen in order, so they are numbered 1 and 2 and arrow the way they go. Read a line the way you read a story: where it starts, what happens first, what happens next.

**Read.** "The number line shows two jumps that start at 46. Which story matches the jumps?" Read the line before the choices: jump 1 goes right, 46 to 62, so first she gets money; jump 2 goes left, 62 to 49, so then she spends. At a glance, before any amounts: the story must begin with earning and end with spending.

**The example.** The given line: start 46, jump 1 right to 62, jump 2 left to 49. (1) The line is read as a story in plain words: "She had forty-six dollars. Then sixteen more came in. Then thirteen went out." (2) Jump 1 is measured: 46 to 62 is 16 (4 to 50, 12 to 62, counted up as the missing hop taught). Jump 2: 62 to 49 is 13. (3) The four choices appear exactly as the app shows them. Rule out, then prove the one left by acting it on the line: 46 + 16 lands on 62, 62 − 13 lands on 49, the same landing dot.

**Rule out.** Jump 1 goes right, so "spends 13, then earns 16" dims. Jump 2 goes left, so "earns 16, then earns 13" dims. Both in one beat: "the first jump is up and the second is down: these two are gone." Two remain, with the same numbers in different places: jump 1 is 16 long, so "earns 13, then spends 16" dims. One left.

**Fast.** Direction first (it dims half), then the first jump's length.

**Check.** Read the chosen story aloud and walk it on the line; it must land where the line lands.

**Line.** "Right is more, left is less. Read the jumps in order."

**Trap.** Matching by the two amounts and ignoring order or direction (the decoy "earns 13, then spends 16" has the same numbers). The jumps are numbered 1 and 2 and arrow their way, and the lesson reads direction before it measures anything.

**Kit.** `numberline` with two opposite hops numbered and arrowed in one shot (4.3); `dim` (4.7).

**Writer.** The strongest writer: a reading lesson, short, where the voice telling the line as a story is the whole point.

## Add and subtract within thousands (grade 3)

### `add-three-digit` — Add, with an estimate first

**Standard.** 3.NBT.A.2: "Fluently add and subtract within 1000 using strategies and algorithms based on place value, properties of operations, and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction." The same case as grade 2's `add-with-regrouping`; this is that concept with the cubes gone and the grade 3 habits added.

**Foundations first.** Name what is already owned in one line: tens with tens, ones with ones; a column that makes ten carries one to the next. The stack with headers is the picture, drawn alone. Define *estimate*: round the front digits and add them, so you know where the answer lands before you work; it is written beside the stack in muted ink and stays there.

**Read.** "What is 439 + 198?" Bigger than both. Estimate: 400 and 200 is 600, a bit more: about 640. At a glance, the exact answer: 198 is two short of 200; 439 + 200 is 639; give the two back: 637. The stack now has something to agree with.

**The example.** 439 + 198. (1) Headers; the two numbers; the estimate "≈ 640" beside them. (2) Ones: 9 + 8 = 17; write 7, carry 1 above the tens on the word. (3) Tens: 3 + 9 + 1 = 13; write 3, carry 1 above the hundreds. (4) Hundreds: 4 + 1 + 1 = 6. (5) 637, and the near-round line on the board agrees: 439 + 200 − 2 = 637.

**Fast.** When an addend is a few short of a round number, add the round number and give back the extra: exact every time, and no stack needed. Otherwise the stack, right to left, carry on the word.

**Sense.** About 640.

**Check.** Subtract a part back: 637 − 198 = 439 (or 637 − 200 + 2). Two different roads to one number is the check.

**Line.** "Estimate first. A number near a round one: add the round one, give back the rest."

**Trap.** Forgetting a carry (527, 627) or writing a column's whole sum (41317). The carry is written the moment the column says "seventeen"; the estimate catches a missing hundred before the answer is boxed.

**Kit.** `stack` with `estimate` (4.1).

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, from this concept, once `add-with-regrouping` is approved.

### `subtract-three-digit` — From a round number, count up; otherwise the stack

**Standard.** 3.NBT.A.2, the "strategies and algorithms ... and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction" clause. The two variants are two genuinely different cases: 1,000 − 863 (every top digit smaller, two zeros to cross; the round top makes counting up the honest fastest way) and 974 − 843 (no trade anywhere; look first and you know).

**Foundations first.** Two things a subtraction can mean, both known: what is left, and how far apart (grade 2's missing hop). From a round number, "how far apart" is faster: count up from the smaller number to the next ten, the next hundred, then to the top. Then the stack, named from grade 2: can't take, open a ten; no tens, open a hundred; and across zeros, each 0 becomes 9 because it gets ten and gives one. Estimate first, as in addition.

**Read.** "What is 1,000 − 863?" Smaller than 1,000; 863 is in the eight hundreds, so the gap is between 100 and 200. Estimate: 1,000 − 900 is 100, a bit more.

**The example.** 1,000 − 863, by counting up. (1) The open line with 863 and 1,000 and a bracket "?". (2) 863 to 870: +7. (3) 870 to 900: +30. (4) 900 to 1,000: +100. (5) 7 + 30 + 100 = 137; the bracket reads 137; on the board, 1,000 − 863 = 137. (6) One short road beside it, because tests also show it in a stack: top row 1,000, bottom 863; the ones need a ten, the tens are 0, the hundreds are 0, so open the thousand: it becomes 0, the hundreds 10 then 9, the tens 10 then 9, the ones 10; then 10 − 3 = 7, 9 − 6 = 3, 9 − 8 = 1: 137. "Every zero you pass becomes a nine. Same answer, longer road."

Quick second case, 974 − 843: look down each column first: 4 is enough for 3, 7 for 4, 9 for 8; no trading. Right to left: 1, 3, 1: 131. Estimate 970 − 840 ≈ 130; check 131 + 843 = 974. Three or four beats; it is here because the learner must see that the first thing to do is look, and that most columns need nothing.

**Fast.** From a round number, or when the two numbers are close: count up. Otherwise look down each column, then run the stack. Both are sure; the lesson chooses for the numbers and says why.

**Sense.** Between 100 and 200; near 140.

**Check.** Add back: 137 + 863, stacked: 7 + 3 = 10, carry; 3 + 6 + 1 = 10, carry; 1 + 8 + 1 = 10: 1,000, every column filling to ten.

**Line.** "From a round number, count up. Across zeros, every zero becomes a nine."

**Trap.** Smaller from larger in each column (1,863: bigger than the start, which the estimate kills at once), and writing 10 over every zero without passing the one along (147). The count-up picture never writes a zero; the stack beat opens the thousand on its own word and shows each nine.

**Kit.** `numberline` open with arcs of 7, 30 and 100 to scale on one line (4.3); `stack` with the chain across two zeros (4.1).

**Writer.** The strongest writer until `subtract-two-digit-regroup` (the same choice between counting up and the algorithm, at two digits) is approved; after that a capable cheaper model from this concept.

### `bag-totals-chain` — Two-step stories: join then set aside; a whole with three parts (pilot 5)

**Standard.** 3.OA.D.8: "Solve two-step word problems ... Represent these problems using equations with a letter standing for the unknown quantity. Assess the reasonableness of answers using mental computation and estimation strategies including rounding." 3.NBT.A.2 for the arithmetic. Not 4.NBT.B.4.

**Foundations first.** Read the whole story before any arithmetic. The number bond for big numbers is the part-whole bar: parts drawn end to end at their true lengths, a bracket over the whole; the "?" is whichever piece the story hides. The words that point: *pours both together* joins parts into a whole; *sets aside* takes a part out; *the rest* is the part that is left after the known ones. Grade 3's letter: the unknown gets a name, m, and the story is written as an equation. Estimate by rounding before the stack.

**Read.** Story 1: "One of the jars holds 209 marbles and another holds 145 marbles. Student B pours both jars out together and sets aside 232 of the marbles that are broken. How many marbles are not set aside?" "Pours both together", "sets aside" and "not set aside" go gold in turn. The answer is smaller than the two jars together, and you cannot get it from one jar: 232 is more than 145. Estimate: 200 and 150 is 350; take away about 230: about 120.

**The example.** (1) A teal bar 209 and a rust bar 145 snap end to end under a bracket "?": that is "pours both together." Stack: 209 + 145; 9 + 5 = 14, write 4 carry 1; 0 + 4 + 1 = 5; 2 + 1 = 3: 354. The bracket reads 354. (2) "Sets aside 232": a part of length 232 slides out of the whole and goes muted; the piece that stays is m. Equation: 354 − 232 = m. Stack: 4 − 2, 5 − 3, 3 − 2: no trade: 122. (3) Sense: about 120. (4) Check: add back, 122 + 232 = 354, and 354 is the two jars.

Second example, fully worked, because the story's shape is different: "A collection has 503 stickers. 248 are star and 156 are heart. The rest are moon. How many stickers are moon?" Here the whole is given and two parts are known. (1) One bracket 503 over three parts: 248 teal, 156 rust, m with "?". Estimate: 250 and 150 is 400; 500 take away 400 is about 100, and a little under, since the parts are a little over 400. (2) Equation: 248 + 156 + m = 503. The two known parts join: 248 + 156 = 404 (8 + 6 = 14, write 4 carry 1; 4 + 5 + 1 = 10, write 0 carry 1; 2 + 1 + 1 = 4). (3) 503 − 404: the ones cannot take 4 from 3 and the tens are 0, so the hundreds open: 5 becomes 4, the 0 becomes 10 then 9, the 3 becomes 13; 13 − 4 = 9, 9 − 0 = 9, 4 − 4 = 0: 99. (4) Sense: a little under 100. (5) Check: 99 + 404 = 503.

**Fast.** Draw the bar before any number: if two amounts join and one leaves, add then subtract; if the whole is given and parts are named, add the known parts and take them from the whole. The estimate before the stack says where the answer lands.

**Sense.** Join then set aside: less than the two together, more than nothing. A whole with parts: the rest is less than the whole, and about the whole minus the known parts rounded.

**Check.** Add back: the answer plus what was set aside equals the total that was poured; the three parts add to the whole.

**Line.** "Read the whole story, then draw it. The bar tells you what to add and what to take away."

**Trap.** Adding all three numbers (209 + 145 + 232 = 586, more than the jars together); taking the 232 from one jar; in the second shape, subtracting only one part (503 − 248 = 255). The bar makes the whole first and the set-aside part leaves it; three parts sit under one bracket of 503, so two must go before the third shows.

**Kit.** `partbar` with two or three parts, a "?" part, `join` and `remove` (4.6); `stack` with `estimate` (4.1).

**Writer.** The strongest writer: the lesson is the reading and the drawing, and the two story shapes must be told, not recited.

## Whole-number add and subtract (grade 4)

### `add-multidigit` — The same stack, six digits long

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.4: "Fluently add and subtract multi-digit whole numbers using the standard algorithm."

**Foundations first.** The stack is the same at any length: the places go on to the left, named by the headers, grouped in threes with the comma (family 2's periods: ones, thousands). Numbers line up on the right, ones under ones. The estimate from the front digits comes first and stays beside the work. New here: a carry past the last column is not lost; it becomes a new first digit.

**Read.** "What is 50,113 + 73,304?" Bigger than both. Estimate: 50 thousand and 73 thousand is 123 thousand, so the answer has six digits and starts 12 or 13.

**The example.** 50,113 + 73,304. (1) Headers through hundred thousands with the comma; the two numbers slot from the right; "≈ 123,000" beside. (2) Ones 3 + 4 = 7; tens 1 + 0 = 1; hundreds 1 + 3 = 4; thousands 0 + 3 = 3: four columns, no carry, each lit as it is said. (3) Ten thousands: 5 + 7 = 12 ten thousands; write 2, carry 1 into the hundred thousands, a column that was empty above both numbers. (4) Hundred thousands: nothing plus nothing plus the carry: 1. (5) 123,417, the comma after the thousands.

**Fast.** Estimate the front digits; then the stack, right to left; a carry out of the last column is the answer's first digit.

**Sense.** About 123,000, six digits.

**Check.** Subtract a part back: 123,417 − 73,304 = 50,113 (the stack runs again with one trade at the ten thousands). Or add in the other order; same carry, same answer.

**Line.** "Line up on the right. The stack is the same at any length."

**Trap.** Leaving the last carry unwritten (23,417): the estimate says six digits before the work starts, and the lesson writes the carry as a digit in its own column, not as a small mark.

**Kit.** `stack` headers to hundred thousands with the comma grouping (4.1).

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, from this concept and `add-three-digit`.

### `subtract-multidigit` — Unequal lengths, and the chain across zeros

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.4, "using the standard algorithm."

**Foundations first.** Two numbers of different lengths line up on the right, under the headers; the place above the shorter number stays empty, never a zero. The borrow, named from grades 2 and 3 in one line: can't take, open the next place; a zero on the way gets ten and gives one, so it becomes nine. The borrow is drawn in its column: the struck digit and the new digit directly above it. Estimate first.

**Read.** "What is 540,046 − 71,719?" Smaller than 540,046. Estimate: 540 thousand take away about 70 thousand: about 470 thousand, a little less.

**The example.** 540,046 − 71,719. (1) Headers; 540,046 on top; 71,719 slotted from the right so its 7 sits under the 4 of the ten thousands and the hundred thousands above it stays empty; "≈ 468,000" beside. (2) Ones: 6 − 9 cannot; open a ten: 4 becomes 3, 6 becomes 16; 16 − 9 = 7. (3) Tens: 3 − 1 = 2. (4) Hundreds: 0 − 7 cannot; the thousands are 0; the ten thousands are 4. The chain, in order, each on its own word: 4 becomes 3; the thousands' 0 becomes 10, gives one, becomes 9; the hundreds' 0 becomes 10. 10 − 7 = 3. (5) Thousands: 9 − 1 = 8. (6) Ten thousands: 3 − 7 cannot; open from the hundred thousands: 5 becomes 4, 3 becomes 13; 13 − 7 = 6. (7) Hundred thousands: 4 with nothing under it: 4. (8) 468,327.

**Fast.** Right-align, estimate, look down each column; where the top is smaller, open the next place, and where that is zero, go left to the first digit that is not zero and let every zero on the way become nine.

**Sense.** About 468,000.

**Check.** Add back: 468,327 + 71,719 = 540,046, the carries landing where the openings were.

**Line.** "Line up on the right. Can't take? Open the next place; a zero on the way becomes nine."
