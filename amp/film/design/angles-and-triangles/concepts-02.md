# Angles and triangles, part 2: the concepts for ten problem types

Written from the family design. Every number below was recomputed from the generated variants; where a variant's arithmetic is shown, it is the exact one the learner will see. The skill openings (section 3 of the design) are assumed: each concept says what its film adds to its skill's opening, not the opening again.

Two corrections to the family design, to apply in these concepts:

- **Complementary angles are practiced.** `angle-equation` has a variant with "∠AOC is a right angle" (whole 90). The concept below gives 7.G.B.5's complementary-angle case its own screen.
- **The 8.G.A.5 types are misfiled**, as the family design explains: `triangle-angle-sum`, `triangle-exterior-angle`, `parallel-lines-angle`, `angle-chase-chain` are catalogued under 8.EE.C.7 only, yet half their variants have no x. Each concept below names 8.G.A.5 as primary and 8.EE.C.7 for the expression variants.

## Triangle construction (grade 7, 7.G.A.2)

### `triangle-conditions` — How many triangles fit the conditions?

**First:** this is a mixed type. Five different givens live under one question, and the answer depends entirely on *what kind of thing* was given. The lesson teaches the sort, then one motion per kind. It overlaps `triangle-uniqueness` (the swinging-side case is the one kind missing here); the two should share their opening.

**Standard:** 7.G.A.2, "constructing triangles from three measures of angles or sides, noticing when the conditions determine a unique triangle, more than one triangle, or no triangle." The three-angle case is the other face of 8.G.A.5's angle-angle criterion; the lesson names it in one line.

**Foundations first.** The skill opening (the hinge and the compass arcs: "two sides must reach past the third", foundation 6). Then, built live, the one idea this film owns, foundation 7: *sides lock, angles only shape.* Say what "different triangles" means the way the problem does: a triangle turned over or turned around is the same triangle; a bigger copy is a different one. Then the sort, drawn as a small table on the board as it is discovered, one row per kind: three sides; three angles; two angles; two sides and the angle between; two angles and a side between. The last column, "how many", is filled during the examples.

**Read.** "How many different triangles meet these conditions" with "same size and shape count as one" gold. At a glance, before any arithmetic: what was given? **All angles:** the answer can never be "exactly one" (dim it now). **All sides:** the answer can never be "more than one" (dim it now). **Sides and an angle mixed:** it is one, unless the angles already break 180.

**The example.** 44°, 40°, 96°. (1) Add: 44 + 40 + 96 = 180, so a triangle exists. (2) Draw it true: a small triangle with its three arcs and numbers. (3) **Scale up:** the triangle grows from one corner; every angle's readout holds at 44, 40, 96 while every side's length readout climbs. Stop at a copy twice the size: same angles, a different triangle. "Three angles fix the shape. They say nothing about the size." (4) More than one triangle. (5) The sort's row fills: three angles → more than one if they add to 180, none if they do not.

**The other kinds, each on its own short screen** (each is a different motion and a different answer, so none can be skipped):
- 2 mm, 10 mm, 5 mm: the longest lies flat; arcs of 2 and 5 swing from its ends and **miss**, the 3 mm gap drawn: 2 + 5 = 7, less than 10. No triangle.
- 6 cm, 10 cm, 10 cm: arcs meet; 6 + 10 is more than 10: one crossing above (its mirror below is the same triangle). Exactly one.
- 4 m and 12 m with 110° between: the angle is drawn, the two sides laid along its rays, the third side dashed between the ends. Nothing can move: **the hinge is locked.** Exactly one.
- 62°, 77° and a 15 m side between: the side is drawn, a 62° ray from one end, a 77° ray from the other; they cross in one place. Exactly one. (62 + 77 = 139 is under 180, so they do cross.) Two angles alone (66°, 56°): the third is 58°, and the scale-up again: more than one.

**Fast.** Sort first. Three angles: add; 180 means many, anything else none. Two angles: add; under 180 means many, 180 or more none. Three sides: two shorter against the longest. A side with angles on it, or an angle with sides on it: exactly one, no arithmetic (two angles must still add to less than 180).

**Sense.** Everything given as a length pins a size; anything given only as angles leaves the size free.

**Check.** For "many", show two sizes with the same angles. For "none", the sum that fails. For "one", the arcs or rays that meet in one place.

**Rule out.** Three angles: "exactly one" dims at once. Three sides: "more than one" dims at once. Mixed: "no triangle" dims unless two angles reach 180.

**Line.** "Three sides lock a triangle. Three angles only fix its shape."

**Trap.** "Three angles are given, so exactly one": everything feels pinned. The scale-up answers it without words. Second: 6, 10, 10 read as "no triangle because two sides are equal"; the arcs meet.

**Kit.** Family tools 4.4 (arcs, `meet`, length readouts) and `view`/`move` for the scale-up. Add: the board's `table` part used as the sort, with its "how many" cells filled on the word. Nothing else.

**Writer.** Strongest. Five situations, five motions, and the whole lesson is the sort; a weak writer collapses it into a recipe.

### `triangle-uniqueness` — The swinging side

**First:** off grade as generated. Every direct variant puts the given angle at 30° or 150°, because judging where a swinging side lands needs a fact about distances. At 30° the straight-across distance is half the slant (Geometry's special triangle); at 150° the nearest point of the far ray is the corner itself. Both can be carried without trigonometry (the fold and the picture below), and the lesson should say the 30° fact is borrowed. If any variant uses another angle, the type belongs in Geometry. Also: "exactly two" is never the answer to a direct variant seen, but it is the truth of two distractor sets (∠A = 30°, AC = 14, BC = 8; ∠X = 30°, XZ = 16, YZ = 12), so the lesson must teach it.

**Standard:** 7.G.A.2, the same clause as above, the "more than one" and "no triangle" outcomes.

**Foundations first.** The sort from `triangle-conditions`, recalled in one beat (three sides lock; three angles only shape). Then the one new thing: *two sides and an angle not between them.* Read it from the letters: ∠A with AC and BC; AC touches A, BC does not, so the angle is not between the sides, and BC is the side that swings ("Side BC is opposite ∠A" says the same). Draw: the 30° angle at A, AC = 10 cm laid along the upper ray, and from C the side BC swinging on a muted arc toward the lower ray, "like a gate hinged at C". Where the arc meets the ray is B. Then the borrowed fact, proved by the **fold**: drop the straight-across dashed segment from C to the ray; flip the whole picture over the ray; the two copies of AC make a triangle with 60° at A and two sides of 10, so every side is 10 (ticked), and the straight-across segment is half of it: 5. "At thirty degrees, the straight-across distance is half the slant."

**Read.** "How many different triangles" (gold). At a glance: compare the swinging side with half the slant. Shorter than half: it cannot reach the ray. Exactly half: it touches once. Between half and the whole slant: it cuts twice. The slant or longer: once (the other landing is at A or behind it). With an obtuse given angle: the far ray leans away, so the swinging side must be longer than the fixed side or it never reaches; and it can only reach once.

**The example.** ∠A = 30°, AC = 10 cm, BC = 3 cm. (1) Draw the sentence as above; half the slant is 5, written on the dashed segment. (2) The arc of radius 3 swings from C and stops short of the ray; the gap of 2 is drawn. (3) No triangle. (4) Then the arc **grows**, with a crossing counter: at 5 it touches (one triangle, a right angle marked at B); at 8 it cuts the ray twice (two triangles, both drawn, one dashed); at 10 and beyond, once. The four answers appear as the radius passes each landmark.

**Quick second case, different act:** ∠P = 150°, PR = 6, QR = 2. The 150° angle is drawn; the far ray leans back behind P; the arc of radius 2 from R cannot even reach P, which is the nearest point of that ray. No triangle. "Across from the biggest angle sits the longest side."

**The select variant** ("which set determines exactly one"): the sort does it. Three angles (70, 60, 50; 40, 40, 100; 60, 60, 60): many, dim. Two angles over 180 (108 + 95; 117 + 147): none, dim. Three sides that do not reach (2, 6, 11; 4, 6, 11): none, dim. Two sides with the angle *not* between (∠A = 30°, AC = 14, BC = 8: 8 is between 7 and 14, so two): dim. What is left: two sides with the angle between them (PQ = 5, ∠P = 40°, PR = 4): the hinge is locked. One.

**Fast.** Letters first: does the side not touching the angle swing? If so, compare it with half the slant (at 30°) or with the fixed side (obtuse). Otherwise, the sort.

**Sense.** A side shorter than the straight-across distance cannot touch the ray no matter how it swings.

**Check.** Draw the arc; count the crossings; a crossing at A does not count.

**Rule out.** "Infinitely many" dims whenever a length is given. In the select variant, three angles and over-180 pairs dim in one beat.

**Line.** "The side across from the angle swings like a gate. Count where it lands."

**Trap.** "All three measures are given, so exactly one": the growing arc shows two landings. And reading "AB = 8, ∠A = 30°, AC = 7" as a swinging case: both sides touch A, so the angle is between them and nothing swings; the letters decide.

**Kit.** 4.4 arcs with `meet` and a radius that grows (`"r"` animated from 3 to 11) with a crossing count; the fold is the existing `flip`. Nothing else.

**Writer.** Strongest. Four outcomes from one growing arc, a borrowed fact proved by a fold, and a select variant that is the sort: the staging is the lesson.

## Angle relationships (grade 7, 7.G.B.5)

### `angle-pair-measure` — The unknown angle at a point, on a line, or across a crossing

**First:** the catalog description names a bigger angle and a vertical pair, but the generator produces three kinds: three angles around a point (360), a ray on a line (180), and a crossing (equal). One method, three totals, so three screens. The right angle (90) does not appear; if the generator ever produces it, the method covers it.

**Standard:** 7.G.B.5, "use facts about supplementary... vertical, and adjacent angles... for an unknown angle in a figure"; with 4.MD.C.7, "the angle measure of the whole is the sum of the angle measures of the parts."

**Foundations first.** The skill opening: two lines cross; the half-turn lands each angle on its opposite ("across: equal"); two neighbors fill the straight angle ("side by side: one eighty"); the ring for "all the way around: three sixty." Then the letters rule, said once and used on every screen: the middle letter is the corner; two angles that share a ray letter sit side by side; two that share only the corner letter sit across.

**Read.** "What is the measure of ∠GOE" (gold on the name; the middle letter is the corner). Draw the sentence: three rays from O, in the order the names give, with the two known angles true to size and a teal `?` in the third. At a glance, name the total: three rays "divide the full turn", so 360; and 62 + 162 is already 224, more than 180, so the total cannot be a line.

**The example.** Rays OE, OF, OG; ∠EOF = 62°, ∠FOG = 162°; find ∠GOE. (1) Draw OE, then OF turned 62° from it, then OG turned a further 162°, each arc in its color; the third angle, teal, is what is left before OE comes round again. (2) Name the total: the ring appears, 360. (3) Board: 62 + 162 = 224, then 360 − 224 = 136, each operation in the margin; on the figure the two known wedges fit against the ring and the teal gap reads 136. (4) 136.

**Quick second case:** L, O, J on a line, ray OK, ∠JOK = 46°: the straight-angle half-disc is the total, 180 − 46 = 134. **Quick third:** lines RT and SU cross at P, ∠UPR = 145°, find ∠SPT. Letters: U, P, R and S, P, T share only P: across. The half-turn lands one on the other: 145, no arithmetic.

**Fast.** Name the total, add the known parts, subtract; across a crossing, copy.

**Sense.** The gap looks a little smaller than the 162 wedge and much bigger than the 62 one; 136 fits. An across angle looks the same because it is.

**Check.** 62 + 162 + 136 = 360, written on the board.

**Line.** "Across: equal. Side by side on a line: one eighty. All the way around: three sixty."

**Trap.** 180 as the total around a point (the arithmetic then fails, since 224 is already past it): the ring is on screen before any subtraction. And for the crossing, 180 − 145 = 35, the neighbor instead of the opposite: the letters rule and the half-turn choose first.

**Kit.** Family tools: `wedge`, `ring` (4.1), `turn`. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Cheaper. One method, three totals, the concept names every ray and every wedge.

### `angle-equation` — Write the equation the figure says, then solve

**Standard:** 7.G.B.5, "write and solve simple equations for an unknown angle in a figure," covering supplementary, complementary (the right-angle variant), vertical and adjacent angles; 8.EE.C.7b for variants with x on both sides.

**Foundations first.** The skill opening (half-turn, straight angle, ring, letters rule). Then this film's own point, said once: *the relation is a sentence, and the sentence is the equation.* "Across: equal" writes `=` between two expressions. "Side by side: they fill the whole" writes `+` between them and `=` the whole. The balance (family 11) solves it; the margin notes carry the operations.

**Read.** Gold goes on the asked thing: "value of x" or "measure of ∠GVH"; the board writes "Asked: ∠GVH" at the top and keeps it. Draw the sentence: two lines with their end letters, the crossing dotted, each expression written in its arc. At a glance: the letters decide the relation before anything is written. G, V, H and E, V, F share only V: across, so equal. S, V, T and R, V, S share VS: side by side, so they add to the whole (180 because T, V, R are on a line).

**The example.** Lines EG and FH cross at V; ∠GVH = (3x + 48)°, ∠EVF = (4x + 30)°; find ∠GVH. (1) Draw the crossing true (the solved angles, 102 and 78, so the picture is honest). (2) Letters: across. The half-turn lands the GVH wedge on the EVF wedge: equal. (3) Board: `3x + 48 = 4x + 30`; `− 3x` in the margin: `48 = x + 30`; `− 30`: `x = 18`. (4) "Find x, then answer the question": ∠GVH = 3(18) + 48 = 54 + 48 = 102. (5) 102.

**Second example, a different act:** T, V, R on a line, ray VS; ∠SVT = (5x + 31)°, ∠RVS = (4x − 40)°; find x. The straight-angle half-disc is the whole. `(5x + 31) + (4x − 40) = 180`; collect like terms, said as "five x and four x are nine x; thirty-one take away forty is negative nine": `9x − 9 = 180`; `+ 9`: `9x = 189`; `÷ 9`: `x = 21`. Asked for x: box 21. On the figure the two wedges read 136 and 44 and fill the line.

**Quick third case, same act, a whole children forget to use:** ∠AOC is a right angle, OB inside; (4x + 19) + (4x − 1) = 90; `8x + 18 = 90`; x = 9; ∠BOC = 55. The square corner on the figure is the 90.

**Fast.** Letters pick the relation; across writes `=`; side by side writes `+ … = whole`; collect, solve in the margin, substitute for the asked angle.

**Sense.** Every expression must come out a positive angle (4x − 40 forces x past 10; 21 is fine). Across: both expressions give the same number (102 and 102). Side by side: the two numbers add to the whole (136 + 44 = 180).

**Check.** Substitute x into every expression and add, or compare, on the board.

**Line.** "Across: equal. Side by side: they fill the whole. Find x, then answer the question."

**Trap.** Setting side-by-side angles equal, or across angles to 180 (x + 75 = 2x + 72 for a linear pair, or the reverse). The letters rule and the half-turn choose the relation before the equation exists. Second trap: boxing x when the angle is asked. "Asked:" stands at the top of the board from the start.

**Kit.** `wedge`, `turn`; the board as it is. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Strongest: the first lesson of the skill, and the equal-versus-180 confusion is the family's highest-stakes error.

### `angle-claim-reasoning` — What could, what cannot, which is bigger

**First:** three kinds under one name: a crossing's possible sums (an expression's form), a comparison of two angles, and the possible size of a triangle's angle. The third uses the triangle angle sum, which 8.G.A.5 owns; in grade 7 it is borrowed, and the film shows corners-to-a-line in one beat and says so. Mixed type: teach reading, one screen per kind.

**Standard:** 7.G.B.5 (vertical and supplementary facts); 7.EE.A.1, "add, subtract... linear expressions" (doubling an expression, subtracting one from 180).

**Foundations first.** The skill opening recalled in one beat. Then this film's idea: *name every angle in the figure first, then the question is reading.* At a crossing with one angle (2x + 4), the across angle is the same; each neighbor is 180 − (2x + 4) = 176 − 2x (said as "take the whole expression from one eighty"). Four angles, two sizes. And the polarity words: "could", "cannot", "true" go gold, because the wrong answer here is usually the right answer to the opposite question.

**The example, kind A.** Two lines, one angle (2x + 4)°. Which cannot be the sum of two of the four angles? (1) Draw a crossing (for one value of x; the arithmetic holds for all) and label all four: (2x + 4), (2x + 4), (176 − 2x), (176 − 2x), two colors. (2) Every way to pick two: two across: (2x + 4) + (2x + 4) = 4x + 8; two neighbors: 180; the other across pair: (176 − 2x) + (176 − 2x) = 352 − 4x. Three forms, written as a list. (3) Match the choices: 4x + 8 is on the list; −4x + 352 is on the list; 180 is on the list; 2x + 94 is not. (4) 2x + 94 cannot be.

**Kind B, one screen.** ∠X and ∠Y side by side on a line, ∠Y = 134°. Draw true: Y is obtuse, X is what is left, 46. Y is greater. The glance: on a line, the angle over 90 is the bigger one; across a crossing, they are always equal (48 and 48), so "equal" is the statement.

**Kind C, one screen.** Triangle ABC, ∠A = 126°. Which could be ∠B? (1) Corners to a line, one beat: the three wedges fill 180. (2) 180 − 126 = 54 is left for B and C together, drawn as one 54 wedge cut by a sliding ray: B's readout runs from near 0 to near 54 while C's runs the other way; when the ray reaches the edge, C is 0 and the triangle is flat. (3) So B is more than 0 and strictly less than 54. (4) Choices 74, 54, 56 dim together; 14 could be. The "cannot" variant (∠A = 115, left 65): 7, 58, 43 are inside, 104 cannot.

**Fast.** Crossing sums: double the given, 180, double its supplement. Line pair: the obtuse one is bigger; vertical: equal. Triangle: the two unknowns share 180 minus the given, each strictly between 0 and that.

**Sense.** A sum of two angles at a crossing is never less than the smaller angle doubled nor more than the larger doubled; 180 is always possible.

**Check.** Pick a value of x and compute the chosen sum against the four real angles; for the triangle, add the chosen B to A and see that a positive C remains.

**Rule out.** Kind A: 180 and the doubled given are always possible, dim at once. Kind C: anything at or above the leftover dims together ("then the third corner is nothing").

**Line.** "Name every angle first. Then the question is reading."

**Trap.** 54 for "could be ∠B" when 54 is left over: it feels like the answer because it is the number computed. The sliding ray shows C shrinking to zero. Second: picking a "could" when "cannot" was asked. The gold polarity word and the recap's repeat.

**Kit.** `wedge`; an angle with `tex: "@deg"` (4.1) whose second ray glides for the sliding beat. Nothing else.

**Writer.** Strongest. Three kinds, one about the form of an expression; the quality is in the reasoning.

## Triangle angle theorems (grade 8, 8.G.A.5)

### `triangle-angle-sum` — The three corners make a straight line

**Standard:** 8.G.A.5, "informal arguments to establish facts about the angle sum... of triangles... arrange three copies... so that the sum of the three angles appears to form a line, and give an argument in terms of transversals why this is so" (primary, taught by the opening even though never tested); 8.EE.C.7b, "collecting like terms," for the expression variants. The catalog's 8.EE.C.7-only link should be corrected.

**Foundations first.** This film builds the skill's opening for every later film to recall. (1) The seeing: a triangle's three wedges, three colors, lift off and slide together onto a straight angle, filling it exactly: 180. (2) The reason: the muted parallel to the base through the top corner, arrow marks on both; each base wedge copies and slides up its own side (the side is the transversal) and lands beside the top wedge, because parallel lines make the same angles with any line that crosses them (foundation 8, said in one line). The three sit on the parallel: a straight line. Said once as the point of the moment: "The corners of a triangle are a straight line taken apart."

**Read.** "What is the measure of ∠D" (gold) or "value of x". Draw the triangle true from the given numbers, each known angle in its arc and color, teal `?` or `x` in the asked one. At a glance: the two known already add to 123, so the third is 57-ish: acute, and smaller than F.

**The example.** Triangle DEF, ∠E = 31°, ∠F = 92°; find ∠D. (1) Draw true: 31 at E (rust), 92 at F (blue), D teal. (2) Name the total: the three fill a line, 180. (3) Board: `31 + 92 = 123`, `180 − 123 = 57`, margins carrying the operations; on the figure, the two known wedges slide onto the straight angle and the teal gap reads 57. (4) 57.

**Second example, a different act.** Triangle PQR, ∠P = (2x + 12)°, ∠Q = (3x − 35)°, ∠R = (x + 29)°; find x. Draw true (70, 52, 58). Total: 180. `(2x + 12) + (3x − 35) + (x + 29) = 180`; collect: "two x, three x and x are six x; twelve, minus thirty-five, plus twenty-nine is six": `6x + 6 = 180`; `− 6`: `6x = 174`; `÷ 6`: `x = 29`. Asked for x: box 29. Then the check that uses it: 70 + 52 + 58 = 180.

**Fast.** Numbers: 180 minus the two known. Expressions: add all three, set equal to 180, collect, solve.

**Sense.** D is drawn acute; 57 is acute. x must make 3x − 35 positive (x past 12), and 29 does. The three angles, with x in, add to 180.

**Check.** Add the three angles on the board.

**Line.** "A triangle's corners make a straight line."

**Trap.** Stopping at the sum of the two known (123) or subtracting only one (180 − 92 = 88). "Asked: ∠D" on the board and the figure's acute D make 123 and 88 impossible to believe. For expressions: adding the x terms and forgetting the constants, or boxing an angle when x is asked.

**Kit.** `wedge` with `copy` and `fit` (4.3); parallel `arrows` and `extend` exist. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Strongest: the family's central argument is staged here once for every film after it.

### `triangle-exterior-angle` — The outside angle is the two far corners together

**Standard:** 8.G.A.5, "informal arguments to establish facts about the... exterior angle of triangles" (primary); 8.EE.C.7b for the expression variant.

**Foundations first.** Recall corners-to-a-line in one beat. Then build this film's fact: extend a side past a corner as a muted line; the outside angle appears, teal. The same muted parallel through that corner (parallel to the far side) cuts the outside angle into two pieces: one is a copy of the far corner on the same side (slid along the extended side), one a copy of the other far corner (slid along the slanted side). Then the seeing: the two far wedges lift and **fill the outside** angle with nothing left over. Said once: "The outside angle is the two far corners put together." The words: *exterior angle*, the angle outside one corner; *remote* corners, the two it does not touch.

**Read.** "Side PQ is extended past Q to point S": S lies beyond Q on line PQ, so the exterior angle at Q is ∠RQS and the far corners are P and R. Gold on what is asked: ∠RQS, or ∠F, or x. Draw the sentence: the triangle true, the extension muted, the teal exterior arc. At a glance: the exterior angle is obtuse when the near corner is acute; it is bigger than either far corner alone.

**The example.** Triangle PQR, PQ extended past Q to S; ∠P = 56°, ∠R = 65°; find ∠RQS. (1) Draw true: 56 at P (rust), 65 at R (blue), Q comes out 59; the extension; the teal exterior arc. (2) The two far wedges slide into the exterior angle and fill it. (3) Board: `56 + 65 = 121`. (4) 121. (5) Agreement, one beat: the inside corner at Q is 180 − 121 = 59, and 59 fills the straight line with 121; both routes say the same thing.

**Quick second case, the other direction.** DE extended past E to G; ∠FEG = 65°, ∠D = 43°; find ∠F. The exterior angle is the whole; one far corner is known; the part is the whole minus the other part: 65 − 43 = 22. On the figure, the D wedge leaves the exterior angle and the gap reads 22.

**Second example, a different act.** PQ extended to S; ∠P = (2x + 3)°, ∠R = (3x − 25)°, ∠RQS = (4x + 1)°; find x. The sentence as an equation: `(2x + 3) + (3x − 25) = 4x + 1`; collect: `5x − 22 = 4x + 1`; `− 4x`: `x − 22 = 1`; `+ 22`: `x = 23`. Box 23. Check with x in: 49 + 44 = 93 and the exterior reads 93.

**Fast.** Exterior asked: add the two far corners. A far corner asked: exterior minus the other far corner. Expressions: far + far = exterior, collect, solve.

**Sense.** The exterior is bigger than each far corner and makes a straight line with the near one.

**Check.** 121 + 59 = 180; or substitute x into all three.

**Line.** "The outside angle is the two far corners put together."

**Trap.** Taking 180 minus one far corner (180 − 56 = 124) or adding the near corner into the sum. The fill motion: only the two far wedges fit the outside angle, and the near one fits the line beside it, not inside it.

**Kit.** `wedge` copy and fit; `extend`; parallel `arrows`. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Cheaper. One fact, three asks, every wedge named here.

### `parallel-lines-angle` — Two sizes, and they add to 180

**Standard:** 8.G.A.5, "the angles created when parallel lines are cut by a transversal" (primary); 8.EE.C.7 for the expression variants. These variants carry the app's own figure ("marked angles"), so the film works over that figure.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 8, built live: two parallel lines with arrow marks, a transversal, eight angles. A wedge copies at the upper crossing and **slides along** the transversal to the lower crossing and lands exactly on the matching angle: corresponding angles are equal, because parallel lines point the same way. The half-turn at a crossing makes across angles equal. So every angle in the figure is one of two sizes, drawn in two colors, and the two sizes are neighbors on a line: they add to 180. The names, said in passing when they apply: corresponding (same position at each crossing), alternate (across from each other between the lines), same-side (together on one side between the lines).

**Read.** "One marked angle measures 101°. What is the value of x?" Here x is itself the measure of the marked angle, so the answer is a number of degrees. At a glance, from the figure drawn true: is the x angle the same look as the 101 (both obtuse) or the other look (acute)? Same look, same size; different look, the two sizes together make 180.

**The example.** 101° marked at one crossing, x at the other, in a position where x is acute (same-side interior or a corresponding angle's neighbor; read from the app's figure). (1) Color every angle in the figure one of two colors as the slide and the half-turn fill them in: all the 101s rust, all the others teal. (2) x is teal: the other size. (3) Board: `x + 101 = 180`, `− 101`: `x = 79`. (4) 79. Where x is in a rust position (the 72° variant), the slide lands the 72 wedge on x: x = 72, one beat.

**Second example, a different act.** Marked angles (2x + 55)° and (3x + 35)°, in corresponding positions: the slide lands one on the other, so `2x + 55 = 3x + 35`; `− 2x`: `55 = x + 35`; `− 35`: `x = 20`. Both read 95. If a variant marks two same-side angles, the same opening writes `+ … = 180` instead; the position decides, not the expressions.

**Fast.** Two sizes. Same position or across: equal. Side by side or same-side between the lines: 180 together. Name the pair for the reason; use the two colors for speed.

**Sense.** On a figure drawn true, an acute x cannot equal an obtuse 101; 79 is acute. 95 and 95 look the same because they are.

**Check.** 101 + 79 = 180, and every angle in the figure now reads 101 or 79.

**Line.** "Parallel lines: two sizes, and they add to one eighty."

**Trap.** "All eight are equal": x = 101. The two colors are on the figure before any equation. The reverse: writing x + 72 = 180 for a corresponding pair. The slide lands one on the other first.

**Kit.** `wedge` copies sliding over the app's parallel-lines figure through `scene`: the kit must let wedges and colors be laid on a `scene` figure's named angles, or draw the figure natively with the same geometry. That is the one gap.

**Writer.** Cheaper, once the scene gap is closed. One method; the concept names the positions.

### `angle-chase-chain` — Carry an angle across the figure, one fact a step

**First:** two kinds under one name, each a chain: a parallel inside a triangle, and vertical angles at one crossing of parallel lines carried to the other. In kind A the asked angle is always the exterior angle of the small triangle, so the chain has a long route and a one-step route; the film shows both.

**Standard:** 8.G.A.5 (angle sum, exterior angle, parallels, and the angle-angle criterion named in kind A); 7.G.B.5 (vertical angles, a straight line); 8.EE.C.7 for kind B's equation.

**Foundations first.** Recall the skill opening in one beat: corners make a line; a wedge slides along a transversal onto its match. Then this film's idea: *a chain is one fact per step; write what each fact gives before using the next.* And the reading for kind B: "the first angle lies between ℓ and m" means inside the parallels; "on the same side of t" means the pair is same-side interior, which adds to 180.

**The example, kind A.** Triangle ABC, ∠A = 52°, ∠B = 80°; D on AB, E on BC, DE ∥ AC; find the obtuse ∠DEC. (1) Draw the sentence: the triangle true (C comes out 48), D partway along AB, E along BC, DE drawn with arrow marks matching AC, the exterior-looking angle at E toward C marked teal. "Obtuse" gold: ∠DEC is the big angle on line BC at E, not the small one. (2) Long route, one fact a step: corners make a line, so ∠C = 180 − 52 − 80 = 48. DE ∥ AC with BC as transversal: slide the C wedge up to E; ∠BED = 48 (corresponding). B, E, C on a line: ∠DEC = 180 − 48 = 132. (3) Short route: the small triangle DBE has the same angles as ABC (the A wedge slides along AB onto D: 52; B is shared): same shape, the angle-angle idea, named once. ∠DEC is its exterior angle at E: the two far corners, 52 + 80 = 132. Both routes agree. (4) 132.

**Second example, kind B.** At the crossing of t with ℓ, vertical angles (3x + 16)° and (x + 56)°, the first between the lines; find the angle between ℓ and m on the same side of t at m. (1) Draw the sentence: two parallels, the transversal, the two expressions in across arcs at ℓ. (2) Vertical: equal. `3x + 16 = x + 56`; `− x`: `2x + 16 = 56`; `− 16`: `2x = 40`; `÷ 2`: `x = 20`. The angle: 3(20) + 16 = 76. (3) Carry it: the asked angle is at m, between the lines, on the same side: same-side interior, so the two fill 180 together: 180 − 76 = 104. (4) 104. "Asked:" stood at the top of the board, so 20 and 76 are steps.

**Fast.** Kind A: the asked angle is the small triangle's exterior angle: add the two given corners. Kind B: solve the vertical pair, find the angle, then 180 minus it.

**Sense.** "Obtuse" was asked, so 48 cannot be the answer; 132 is. In kind B the asked angle is the other size, so it is 180 minus the first, never equal to it.

**Check.** 48 + 132 = 180 on the line at E; 76 + 104 = 180 between the parallels.

**Line.** "One fact a step. Write what it gives before the next."

**Trap.** Kind A: answering 48 (∠BED) or the acute angle at E. Kind B: setting the vertical angles to add to 180 (3x + 16 + x + 56 = 180) because "angles at a crossing make 180", or stopping at 76. "Across: equal" from the opening, and "Asked:" on the board.

**Kit.** Points at a fraction along a segment (`{ "along": ["B","A"], "frac": 0.45 }`, a small extension of 4.4's `along`); `wedge` copy and slide; parallel marks. Nothing else.

**Writer.** Strongest. Choosing the route is the lesson, and the two kinds share nothing but the habit.

### `geo-isosceles-exterior-angle` — Equal sides, equal angles, then the outside angle

**First:** borrows Geometry's base-angle theorem (HSG.CO.C.10) in grade 8. Acceptable with the fold shown as the reason; the catalog should say it borrows. Two cases, exterior at the apex and at a base corner, both generated, both needed.

**Standard:** 8.EE.C.7b (the equation) and 8.G.A.5 (exterior angle, angle sum); 7.G.B.5 for the straight line at a base corner.

**Foundations first.** Recall fill-the-outside in one beat. Then build the borrowed fact: an isosceles triangle with ticks on AB and AC; **fold** along the line from A to the middle of BC; the two ticked sides land on each other, so the base angles land on each other: equal. Said once: "Equal sides, equal angles." The words: *base angles*, the two at the ends of the unequal side; *apex*, the corner between the equal sides.

**Read.** "Exterior angle at A, formed by extending BA beyond A": the extension runs past A, the outside angle sits between that extension and AC, and the far corners are B and C, the two base angles. "At B, extending AB beyond B": far corners A and C. Gold on "exterior angle at A". At a glance: the two base expressions must come out equal, and at the apex the exterior angle is simply twice a base angle.

**The example.** AB = AC, ∠B = (4x − 3)°, ∠C = (5x − 16)°; exterior angle at A by extending BA beyond A. (1) Draw the sentence: the triangle with ticks, the two expressions in the base arcs, BA extended past A as a muted line, the teal exterior arc at A. (2) Equal sides, equal angles: `4x − 3 = 5x − 16`; `− 4x`: `−3 = x − 16`; `+ 16`: `x = 13`. (3) A base angle: 4(13) − 3 = 49; both read 49 on the figure (drawn true now: apex 82). (4) Fill the outside: the two base wedges slide into the exterior angle at A and fill it: 49 + 49 = 98. (5) Asked: the exterior angle. 98. (The agreement, one beat: the apex is 180 − 98 = 82, and 82 + 49 + 49 = 180.)

**Second example, a different corner.** ∠B = (2x + 5)°, ∠C = (3x − 15)°; exterior at B by extending AB beyond B. `2x + 5 = 3x − 15`; x = 20; base angle 45. The exterior at B sits on the straight line AB extended, beside the 45: 180 − 45 = 135. Or the far corners: apex 180 − 90 = 90, plus the other base angle 45: 135. Same number, shown both ways in one beat.

**Fast.** Set the base angles equal, solve, find the base angle. Apex exterior: double it. Base-corner exterior: 180 minus it.

**Sense.** A base angle must be acute (two of them fit in 180). The exterior angle is obtuse when its corner is acute, and makes a line with it.

**Check.** 98 + 82 = 180 at A; or 45 + 135 = 180 at B; and both base expressions give the same number with x in.

**Line.** "Equal sides, equal angles. Find x, then answer the question."

**Trap.** Stopping at x = 13 or at the base angle 49; or giving 180 − 49 = 131 at the apex, treating A as a base corner. "Asked: exterior angle at A" at the top of the board, and the fill shows the two base wedges, not a straight line, filling the apex's outside.

**Kit.** `flip` for the fold, `seg` ticks, `extend`, `wedge` fit. Nothing new.

**Writer.** Cheaper. One method, two corners, every wedge and every line named here.
