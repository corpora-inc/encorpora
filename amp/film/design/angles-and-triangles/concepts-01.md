# Angles and triangles, part 1: concepts for ten problem types

Angle measure (grade 4, seven types) and triangle construction (grade 7, three types). Each concept below stands on the family design: the turn, the ring, the protractor, the wedge, the hinge. Two shared openings carry the skills, as the decimal films share the cut of the flat:

- **Angle measure, about twenty seconds, built in full by `protractor-reading` and recalled in one beat by the other six:** a ray sweeps away from another and an arc grows behind it with a readout counting; the arms stretch and shrink while the readout holds ("an angle is a turn; the arms can be any length"); the ring of turn fades in around the vertex, 360 ticks, and the landmarks shade: a quarter turn is a square corner, 90; a half turn is a straight line, 180.
- **Triangle construction, about fifteen seconds, built in full by `rg-triangle-third-side-test` and recalled by the other two:** two sides hinged at a corner swing toward the far end of a base; when they reach past it they cross and there is a triangle; when they only touch, the hinge lies flat on the base and there is nothing inside.

Where the app has its own figure (every protractor type), the film uses that figure through `scene`; the kit's protractor must match it to the tick, so the lesson's protractor is the problem's protractor.

## `protractor-reading` — Read an angle on a protractor

**Standard.** 4.MD.C.6: "Measure angles in whole-number degrees using a protractor." With 4.MD.C.5b underneath it: "An angle that turns through n one-degree angles is said to have an angle measure of n degrees."

**Foundations first.** This is the first film of the skill, so it builds the picture everyone else recalls. (1) Two rays from one endpoint, the *vertex*: an angle. The sweep: ray QR starts on ray QP and turns away; the arc grows behind it; a readout counts. (2) The arms lengthen and shorten; the readout does not move: an angle is a turn, not a length. (3) The ring: a full turn around Q is cut into 360 equal one-degree turns; the landmarks shade and are named: a quarter turn is a square corner, 90; a half turn is a straight line, 180. (4) The *protractor*: half of that ring, with the one-degree ticks drawn, a center mark, and a zero line. "A protractor is a ruler for turns." (5) It has two rows of numbers running opposite ways, because you may put either ray on zero; the two rows always add to 180.

**Read.** "What is the measure of angle PQR, in degrees?" The middle letter, Q, is the corner (gold as it is read). The answer is a whole number of degrees between 0 and 180. At a glance, before any number: is the opening more or less than a square corner? Here ray QR leans past the square corner, so the answer is over 90.

**The example.** Angle PQR, measure 100: the reading where 80 looks just as plausible. The figure is the app's protractor with Q at the center mark and QP along the zero line. (1) The place habit, shown even though the figure arrives placed: a protractor slides in off its mark, its center lands on Q, it rotates until zero lies on QP; "center on the corner, zero on a ray." (2) Find the zero that is on QP; that row of numbers is yours. (3) The gold arc runs from that 0 along the row, the readout counting 10, 20, 30 ... until ray QR crosses it; the number 100 glows. (4) At the crossing both rows' numbers are shown: 100 and 80. 100 + 80 = 180; they are the two ways round the half turn. The look decides: QR is past the square corner, so 80 is out and dims with that reason. (5) 100 degrees.

Quick second case, 170 (three beats): zero on the ray, follow that row, 170; the other row says 10, but the arms are almost a straight line, so 10 is nonsense. Adds the look check at the other end of the scale.

**Fast.** Find the zero on your ray; follow that row to the other ray. **Sense.** More than a square corner means more than 90; nearly a straight line means nearly 180. **Check.** Read the other row; the two readings make 180. **Line.** "Center on the corner, zero on a ray, read where the other ray crosses. The two rows add to one eighty: take the one that started at zero." **Trap.** Reading the row that did not start at zero on the ray (80 for 100, 10 for 170). The gold arc starts at the zero that sits on the ray, and the look check is said before the number is read. **Kit.** 4.1 `sweep`, `stretch`, `ring`; 4.2 `protractor` with `place` and `read` (`"scale":"both"`), matching the app's drawing. **Writer.** Strongest: it is the skill's foundation film, and the sweep-to-protractor argument is the design.

## `adjacent-angles` — Angles side by side

**Standard.** 4.MD.C.7: "Recognize angle measure as additive. When an angle is decomposed into non-overlapping parts, the angle measure of the whole is the sum of the angle measures of the parts."

A note on the type: the goal says "find the whole angle or the missing part", but every generated wording asks for a part. The addition direction is still taught, because it is the check (the two parts fit back into the whole); if a "find the whole" variant exists, it is a three-beat quick case on the same figure.

**Foundations first.** One-beat recall: a turn, measured in degrees. Then the fit: two wedges, rust and teal, slide together to share a ray; their arcs join into one arc and a readout shows the sum. Reverse it: a ray cuts a wedge, and the two pieces' numbers add to the whole. "Angles add. The part is the whole minus the other part." Then reading names: the middle letter is the corner; every name here has corner X; the whole is the angle with the largest measure, and the one letter missing from the whole's name (Y) is the ray that cuts it.

**Read.** "Angle WXZ measures 110. Angle YXZ measures 50. What is the measure of angle WXY?" Three angles, one corner X. 110 is the whole; Y is the cutting ray; YXZ is the known part, WXY the part asked (gold). At a glance: a part is smaller than its whole, so the answer is less than 110, and less than the other part's complement, 60 is the only kind of size that fits.

**The example.** Draw the sentence to scale: ray XZ along the base, ray XW turned 110 from it with a muted outer arc labeled 110; ray XY inside, 50 from XZ, the rust wedge YXZ labeled 50; the teal wedge WXY labeled "?". (1) What must these add up to? The whole, 110: `50 + ? = 110` on the board, in the colors. (2) The rust wedge lifts out of the whole; what remains is the teal wedge, and its arc is visibly the shorter piece. (3) `110 − 50 = 60`, note "take the known part from the whole". (4) 60 degrees. (5) Check: the rust wedge slides back; the two arcs join; the readout reads 110.

Quick second case, the straight angle (three beats): angle JKM is 180, a line with a dot on it, drawn with the half-disc arc: "a straight line is an angle too, a half turn." LKM is 115; JKL is 180 − 115 = 65. Adds the one new idea the learner will meet: the whole can be a line.

**Fast.** Whole minus the known part. **Sense.** The answer is smaller than the whole, and the drawn teal arc is the smaller piece. **Check.** Add the parts: 60 + 50 = 110. **Line.** "The middle letter is the corner. The part is the whole minus the other part." **Trap.** Adding: 110 + 50 = 160, because "angles add". The lesson asks "what must these add up to?" before any arithmetic, and the lifted wedge leaves a visibly smaller piece behind. **Kit.** 4.3 `wedge`, `copy`, `fit`; `angle` with a second radius for the whole's muted outer arc. **Writer.** Cheaper model: one method, the fit specified to the part, dry-checkable board; the concept names the figure exactly.

## `turn-degrees` — A circle cut into equal angles

**Standard.** 4.MD.C.5a: "An angle is measured with reference to a circle with its center at the common endpoint of the rays, by considering the fraction of the circular arc between the points where the two rays intersect the circle."

**Foundations first.** One-beat recall, then the ring built live: a full turn around the center is 360; cut it in half and each half is a straight line, 180; cut each half again and a quarter is a square corner, 90. The ring is labeled at 0, 90, 180, 270, 360. "A circle cut into equal angles: each one is 360 divided by how many."

**Read.** Two wordings. "The circle is cut into 8 equal angles that meet at its center. How many degrees is the shaded angle?" and "The shaded angle is 3/4 of the full circle." Both ask for a fraction of 360. At a glance: one of eight is half a quarter, so under 90; three quarters is past a straight line, so over 180 (and the shaded angle is drawn the long way round, a reflex angle: "an angle can be bigger than a straight line").

**The example.** Eight equal angles, one shaded. (1) The ring with eight radii. (2) The whole turn is 360; eight equal parts: `360 ÷ 8 = 45`. (3) The sectors fill one at a time as the count runs 45, 90, 135, 180, 225, 270, 315, 360; the second sector lands exactly on the quarter mark, so two of them are a square corner, which is the sense check said aloud. (4) 45 degrees. If more than one sector is shaded in a variant, multiply: k sectors is k × 45; the film says so once over the counted sectors.

Quick second case, 3/4 of the circle (three beats): quarters are 90 each; three of them shade in: 90, 180, 270. 270 degrees. Adds the fraction wording and the reflex angle.

**Fast.** 360 divided by the number of equal angles; times the number shaded. A quarter is 90 with no division. **Sense.** Under 90 if the piece is smaller than a quarter; over 180 if it covers more than half. **Check.** The pieces counted around come back to 360: 8 × 45 = 360; 270 + 90 = 360. **Line.** "A full turn is three sixty." **Trap.** Dividing 180 by the number of pieces, because the protractor the child just learned stops at 180. The ring is labeled 360 at the top and the sectors are counted all the way around to it. **Kit.** 4.1 `ring` with `cuts`, `shaded` and a running sector label. **Writer.** Cheaper model: one method, one picture, two short cases.

## `protractor-ray-choice` — Which ray makes the angle

**Standard.** 4.MD.C.6: "Measure angles in whole-number degrees using a protractor." It is the multiple-choice shadow of the unpracticed clause "Sketch angles of specified measure": the learner finds where a given measure lands.

**Foundations first.** One-beat recall of the protractor and its two rows, then the fact this type turns on: the two rows always add to 180, so the number 155 appears twice on the protractor, once on each row, on two different rays. Only one of them is 155 from your zero ray.

**Read.** "Use ray BA as zero. Which ray is 155° along the protractor?" B is the center; find where ray BA lies and the row that reads 0 there; the answer is a letter. At a glance: 155 is far more than a square corner, so the ray is on the far side of the square corner, close to the other end of the baseline.

**The example.** Rays BC, BD, BE, BF fan out from B on the app's protractor; ray BA on the baseline at the outer 0 (whichever row reads 0 there is "yours"); ray BE crosses at outer 155 (inner 25); one decoy ray crosses at outer 25 (inner 155). (1) Zero on BA: that row is ours. (2) The look: 155 is past the square corner. Every ray on the near side of 90 dims together, with that reason; two remain. (3) The gold arc runs along our row from 0, counting, and stops at 155: ray BE sits there. (4) E. (5) The decoy: the ray at 25 on our row reads 155 on the other row. It is 25 from BA, not 155: a small opening, which the eye can see.

Quick second case, 10° (three beats): a tiny turn, almost on top of the zero ray; the ray crossing at 10 on our row is E; the ray at 170 reads 10 on the other row but is almost a straight line. Adds the look check at the small end.

**Fast.** Side of the square corner first; then follow the row that starts at zero on the given ray. **Sense.** Over 90 means a wide opening, under 90 a narrow one; the chosen ray must look it. **Check.** The chosen ray's other-row number plus 155 is 180. **Line.** "Take the row that started at zero on your ray." **Trap.** Following the row that did not start at zero on BA and choosing the ray that reads 155 there (really 25). The at-a-glance dim removes it before the number is ever followed, and the arc is drawn from the zero that lies on BA. **Kit.** 4.2 `protractor.read` with the crossed number glowing; board `dim`. **Writer.** Cheaper model: one method, the rule-out and the decoy named in the concept.

## `adjacent-angle-equation` — Choose the equation for the missing part

**Standard.** 4.MD.C.7: "Solve addition and subtraction problems to find unknown angles on a diagram in real world and mathematical problems, e.g., by using an equation with a symbol for the unknown angle measure."

**Foundations first.** One-beat recall of the fit: parts add to the whole. Then what an equation is here: the sentence "the two parts make the whole", written in numbers with a letter standing for the number we do not know. Nothing is computed to answer the question; the equation is chosen, not solved. "x is a number of degrees, so it is more than zero and less than the whole."

**Read.** "The roof rises from the top of the wall at angle JKM, which measures 35°. The brace splits it into angle JKL of 15° and angle LKM of x degrees. Which equation finds x?" Corner K. The whole is JKM, 35; the known part 15; the unknown part x. At a glance: x is a part, so x is less than 35, and x is positive.

**The example.** Draw the sentence: ray KJ along the wall top, ray KM the roof turned 35 from it, the muted outer arc 35; the brace KL inside, the rust wedge JKL at 15 and the teal wedge LKM labeled x. Choices on the card: `15 − 35 = x`, `35 − 15 = x`, `35 + 15 = x`, `x = 35`. (1) What must these add up to? The whole: `15 + x = 35`, written in the figure's colors. (2) Said another way, the rust wedge lifts out: what is left is x: `35 − 15 = x`. The two lines are one sentence read two ways. (3) Rule out, in one beat: `15 − 35 = x` is less than zero, not an angle; `35 + 15 = x` makes the part bigger than the whole; `x = 35` says the part is the whole. All three dim with those reasons. (4) `35 − 15 = x`. (5) Check: x would be 20, and 20 + 15 = 35; the wedge slides back and the arcs join.

Quick second case (three beats): ABD is 105, ABC is 25, CBD is x, and the choices hold only the addition form `x + 25 = 105`, no subtraction form. Same sentence, the other way round; the learner must recognize both. Adds the one thing that trips a child who has memorized "whole minus part".

**Fast.** Write "part plus x equals whole"; the subtraction form is the same line rearranged; pick whichever is offered. **Sense.** x is a positive number smaller than the whole; any equation that breaks that is wrong before any arithmetic. **Check.** Solve it in your head and add the parts back. **Rule out.** Negative, bigger than the whole, equal to the whole: three decoys, one beat. **Line.** "Part plus part is the whole; x is the part we don't know." **Trap.** `35 + 15 = x`, because "angles add"; and `15 − 35 = x`, written in the order the numbers appear in the story. The question "what must these add up to?" fixes the first; "x is a number of degrees, so it is more than zero" fixes the second. **Kit.** 4.3 `wedge`; board `dim`. **Writer.** Cheaper model: one method, decoys named with their reasons.

## `angle-benchmark-estimate` — Nearest benchmark

**Standard.** The catalog gives none. File it under 4.MD.C.6 ("Measure angles in whole-number degrees") as the estimate that precedes and checks every measurement; it is the sense check the protractor lessons lean on. Condition: the kite, gate, ladder, scissors and table figures must be drawn to the true degree, or the type is unanswerable; the film must check each generated figure.

**Foundations first.** One-beat recall, then the benchmarks built as pictures the child can hold up against any angle: the square corner, 90 (the corner of a sheet of paper); fold the square corner in half: 45; the straight line, 180 (a half turn); a square corner cut in three equal turns: 30 is one of them, 60 is two. Each is a translucent wedge the film can lay on a vertex.

**Read.** "Student A flies a kite. Which benchmark best matches the angle the string makes with the ground?" The angle is between the ground line and the string, at Student A's hand. The answer is one of the listed benchmarks, the nearest one. At a glance: first question always: is it a square corner, a straight line, or in between?

**The example.** The kite figure, true to degree: the string rises at 60 from the ground. (1) Lay the square-corner wedge on the vertex: the string is inside it, so less than 90; 90 dims. (2) Lay the 45 wedge (half the corner): the string is past it, so more than 45; 30 dims with it. (3) The 60 wedge fits the string. (4) 60 degrees. (5) Sense: two thirds of a square corner, steeper than halfway.

Quick second case, the folding table fully open (three beats): the two halves make a straight line, so the angle between them is 180, a half turn. Adds the case children miss: flat is an angle.

**Fast.** Compare with the square corner first; then with its half, 45; then whether it is a bit more (60) or a bit less (30). **Sense.** The estimate matches the look: steep is near 90, flat is 180. **Check.** Lay the chosen wedge on the figure; the arms should nearly coincide. **Rule out.** Everything on the wrong side of the square corner dims in one beat; 180 dims unless the arms are a line. **Line.** "Square corner first. Then half of it." **Trap.** Judging the angle by the length of the arms (a long string looks like a "big" angle). The opening recall holds the readout while the arms stretch, and the wedges are laid at the vertex only. **Kit.** 4.3 `wedge` as translucent benchmark tiles that `fit` onto a vertex; the app's story figures drawn true to degree. **Writer.** Cheaper model, with the strongest reviewer confirming the figures are true to degree.

## `protractor-angles-select` — Several rays, one protractor

**Standard.** 4.MD.C.6 ("Measure angles in whole-number degrees using a protractor") and 4.MD.C.7 ("the angle measure of the whole is the sum of the angle measures of the parts"): the angle between two rays is a difference of two readings.

**Foundations first.** One-beat recall of the protractor and "the row that starts at zero on A". Then the new idea: with several rays, read each one once on that row and write the readings down; every angle in the figure is then a subtraction, because the angle from A to C is the angle from A to B plus the angle from B to C (the fit, recalled in one beat as two wedges joining).

**Read.** "Rays A, B and C from vertex O are drawn on one protractor. Select the 3 true statements." Five statements, each an angle and a number; exactly three are true. At a glance: two statements name the same angle with different numbers (AOC = 130, AOC = 120), so one of each pair is false, and that already accounts for two of the two falsehoods. The count asked ("3") goes gold.

**The example.** A on the zero, B crossing at 30, C at 120 on A's row. (1) Read each ray once on A's row; a small table fills beside the figure: A 0, B 30, C 120. (2) ∠AOB: from 0 to 30: 30. ∠AOC: 0 to 120: 120. ∠BOC: the teal wedge from B to C; 120 − 30 = 90, and the look agrees: a square corner. (3) Each statement against the table: AOB = 30 true; AOC = 130 false; AOC = 120 true; BOC = 90 true; AOB = 40 false. The false pair dims together. (4) Three selected; the count matches.

Second worked example, because the question changes polarity: "Select the 4 angles whose measure is not 30°." Rays A, B, C, D; readings A 0, B 30, C 60, D 100 (the figure's own numbers). The six angles as differences: AOB 30, AOC 60, AOD 100, BOC 30, BOD 70, COD 40. "Not thirty" gold: the two that are 30 dim; the other four are the answer; count four. The trap here is answering the opposite question.

**Fast.** Read every ray once on one row; every angle is the bigger reading minus the smaller. **Sense.** A difference between two rays matches the look between them (a square corner reads about 90). **Check.** Partial angles add back: 30 + 90 = 120; the count of selections equals the number asked. **Rule out.** Two statements about one angle: at most one is true. **Line.** "Read each ray once. Between two rays: the bigger reading minus the smaller." **Trap.** Reading one ray on the outer row and another on the inner, then subtracting; and selecting the angles that are 30 when the question says "not". One row, named once, for the whole table; the polarity word is gold and said again at the recap. **Kit.** 4.2 `protractor.read` per ray with the readings staying lit in a small `table` beside the figure; 4.3 `wedge` between two non-zero rays; board `dim`. **Writer.** Strongest: two question kinds, a polarity, and the reasoning is in choosing one row and turning every statement into a subtraction.

## `rg-triangle-third-side-test` — Could, or could not, be the third side

**Standard.** 7.G.A.2: "constructing triangles from three measures of angles or sides, noticing when the conditions determine a unique triangle, more than one triangle, or no triangle."

**Foundations first.** The skill's foundation, built here in full. (1) A triangle is three sides that close. Lay the longest side flat as the base, 14, drawn to scale. (2) The compass arcs: from each end of the base a muted arc swings with the other side's length as its radius. Where the arcs cross is the third corner. (3) Shorten one side until the arcs only touch: the touching point is on the base line, the hinge lies flat, there is nothing inside: not a triangle. Shorten more: the arcs miss, and the gap is drawn. "Two sides must reach past the third." (4) The hinge: sides 6 and 14 joined at one end, the third side dashed between the free ends with a live length. Folded, the third side is 14 − 6 = 8; flat, it is 14 + 6 = 20; both are not triangles. Beneath, a number line from 8 to 20 with hollow dots at both ends: the third side lives strictly inside.

**Read.** "Two sides of a triangle have lengths 6 and 14. Which length could not be the third side?" The polarity "could not" goes gold. At a glance: the third side must be more than 8 and less than 20; three of the four choices (14, 19, 11) are inside, and one is a boundary.

**The example.** Choices 14, 19, 11, 8. (1) The window: difference 8, sum 20, hollow ends. (2) 14, 19, 11 drop onto the line inside the window and dim together: each one could be. (3) 8 drops onto the hollow dot. The arcs of 6 and 8 swing from the ends of the 14 base and touch on the base line: flat. (4) 8 could not.

Second worked example, "could": sides 9 and 12, choices 22, 20, 3, 21. Window 3 to 21, hollow. 3 and 21 land on the hollow dots (flat); 22 lands outside (the arcs miss); 20 lands inside; the arcs of 9 and 20 cross. 20. The polarity flips and both boundaries appear among the choices, so it is a different case.

**Fast.** Difference and sum; the third side is strictly between. **Sense.** The chosen length on the number line: inside for "could", on or outside for "could not". **Check.** Add the two shorter and compare with the longest: 6 + 8 = 14, equal, flat. **Rule out.** For "could not", everything strictly inside the window dims in one beat; for "could", everything on or outside it. **Line.** "The third side lives between the difference and the sum, and never at the ends." **Trap.** Treating the boundary as a triangle: 6 + 8 = 14, "they reach". The arcs touch on the base line and the hinge lies flat with nothing inside; the number line's ends are hollow. **Kit.** 4.4 `arc` with `r` bound to a segment, `meet` points that hide when the arcs miss, `gap`, the hinge with a live `@len` and the number line beneath with hollow ends; board `dim`. **Writer.** Strongest: the foundation film of the skill, and the hinge-to-number-line argument is the design.

## `triangle-side-test` — Which set of three lengths makes a triangle

**Standard.** 7.G.A.2, as above: "noticing when the conditions determine a unique triangle, more than one triangle, or no triangle."

**Foundations first.** One-beat recall of the hinge: two sides must reach past the third. Then the one idea this type adds: only one check is needed. If the two shorter sides reach past the longest, the other two pairs reach for free, because each of them already contains the longest side. So: longest side flat, add the other two, compare.

**Read.** "Which set of three lengths can be the side lengths of a triangle?" Four sets; exactly one works. At a glance: in each set find the longest number; the question is whether the other two together beat it.

**The example.** Sets 9, 16, 5 | 16, 5, 11 | 5, 16, 12 | 10, 16, 4, all in feet. Every set has 16 as its longest, so four bases of 16 are drawn to scale in a column. (1) Set one: arcs of 9 and 5 swing; 9 + 5 = 14, less than 16; they miss, and the gap is drawn. (2) Set two: 5 + 11 = 16; the arcs touch on the base line; flat, not a triangle. (3) Set three: 12 + 5 = 17, more than 16; the arcs cross and the triangle closes in teal. (4) Set four: 10 + 4 = 14; they miss. (5) The three failing sets dim together: "they don't reach." 5 ft, 16 ft, 12 ft.

**Fast.** Longest flat; add the other two; strictly greater wins. One check per set. **Sense.** A set whose two shorter sides are small next to the longest cannot close; the eye sees the gap before the sum. **Check.** For the chosen set, swing the arcs: they cross. **Rule out.** Equal to the longest is flat; less is a gap; both dim together. **Line.** "Two shorter sides must reach past the longest." **Trap.** Adding a pair that includes the longest (16 + 12 > 5, "yes") and calling the set good; and counting the touching set (5 + 11 = 16) as a triangle. The lesson says why the longest is the only side to test, and the touching arcs lie flat on the base line. **Kit.** 4.4 arcs, `meet`, `gap`; four small figures in one view. **Writer.** Cheaper model: one method, four pictures the concept names, shared opening.

## `triangle-third-side` — Least, greatest, or how many whole-number third sides

**Standard.** 7.G.A.2, as above. The whole-number count is the type's own addition.

**Foundations first.** One-beat recall of the hinge opening from the difference to the sum with the third side's length counting, and the number line beneath with hollow dots at both ends. Then the new idea: when the third side must be a whole number, the possible lengths are the whole-number ticks strictly inside the window; the ends are not ticks you may use.

**Read.** Three wordings: least, greatest, how many. All three come from one window. "Two sides measure 4 m and 23 m. The third side is a whole number of meters. How many different lengths are possible?" The asked word goes gold. At a glance: the window runs from 19 to 27, so the answer is a small count, and 19 and 27 are not in it.

**The example.** The count, because it holds the other two as steps and carries the off-by-one. Sides 4 and 23 hinged, drawn to scale. (1) Folded: the dashed third side reads 23 − 4 = 19; a hollow dot at 19 on the line beneath. (2) Flat: 23 + 4 = 27; a hollow dot at 27. (3) The hinge opens and the third side's length counts; whole-number ticks light as it passes them: 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26. The least is 20, the greatest 26, said as the hinge passes them. (4) Count the lit ticks: 7. (5) On the board: `27 − 19 = 8` is the gap between the ends; the ends themselves are not allowed, so `8 − 1 = 7`.

Quick second case, greatest (three beats): 8 cm and 15 cm; sum 23, hollow; the greatest whole number inside is 22. Quick third case, least (three beats): 4 mm and 5 mm; difference 1, hollow; the least is 2, a short, flat-looking triangle that still closes. These are the other two question words, each a single step of the main example, shown as the learner will meet them.

**Fast.** Least: difference plus 1. Greatest: sum minus 1. How many: sum minus difference, minus 1. **Sense.** The count is the lit ticks between two hollow dots; least and greatest sit one tick inside the ends. **Check.** List them: 20 through 26 is seven numbers; 22 + 8 > 15 and 2 + 4 > 5 close their triangles. **Line.** "Never at the ends. Count the ticks strictly inside." **Trap.** Giving the sum as the greatest or the difference as the least, and counting 8 (or 9) instead of 7 by taking the ends. The hollow dots and the ticks that light only as the hinge passes them make the ends unusable. **Kit.** 4.4 hinge with `@len`, a point gliding on a circle, the number line with hollow ends and ticks that light as the readout passes them. **Writer.** Cheaper model: one window, three readings of it, the counting motion specified to the tick.
