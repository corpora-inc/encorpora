# Multiplication and division, part 1: ten concepts

Grades 2 to 3: the array is born, turned and cut, and run backwards into division. Every concept below stands on the family design's foundations 1 to 7 and uses its picture, the `array` (dots or unit squares, every one drawn, both factors labeled in their own colors, the total in teal as a live count). Shared vocabulary is defined where it first appears and only named afterwards: *row* and *column* (grade 2), *equal groups*, *times*, *factor*, *product*, *array* (`equal-tray-total`), *divided by*, *total*, *divisor*, *quotient*, *sharing*, *grouping* (`share-or-group`). The three-number read (groups, each, total; one is missing) is said in full in `equal-tray-total` and `share-or-group` and named in one line everywhere else.

## Grade 2: arrays (`arrays`)

### `small-array-total` — How many unit squares in the array?

**First:** the film seed is a 1 × 3 array (answer 3): nothing to see; seed 4 rows of 5. The app's method says "multiply by the number of rows"; multiplication is grade 3. The standards this type serves say *count* (2.G.A.2) and *add equal addends* (2.OA.C.4), and the lesson does exactly that. It also writes the sum equation and partitions the rectangle live, two clauses the app never asks for.

**Standard:** 2.G.A.2, "Partition a rectangle into rows and columns of same-size squares and count to find the total number of them." Also 2.OA.C.4, "Use addition to find the total number of objects arranged in rectangular arrays with up to 5 rows and up to 5 columns; write an equation to express the total as a sum of equal addends."

**Foundations first:** a plain rectangle. Three lines drawn across cut it into four *rows* (a row goes across, like a row of seats). Four lines drawn down cut each row into five *unit squares*: small squares, all exactly the same size. The squares in a line going down are a *column*. Say what it is for: when every row holds the same number, you can count one row and know them all. Define nothing else.

**Read:** "How many unit squares are in the **whole** array?" Gold on "whole": all of them, not one row. At a glance: the top row holds 5 and there are 4 rows, so the answer is more than 5 and more than 4, and it is a number you reach counting by fives.

**The example:** 4 rows of 5. (1) The rectangle is cut live as above; the partition is the standard's first clause made visible. (2) Count the top row, each square lighting: one, two, three, four, five. "Every row has five." The 5 is written above in violet. (3) Count the rows down the left: four, in blue. (4) Add the rows: each row lights in turn and the total ticks 5, 10, 15, 20 (counting by fives, from grade 1). The board writes 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20: the standard's equation, four fives, one for each row. (5) 20 unit squares. (6) One beat, the same squares the other way: count by columns, 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 20; same squares, same answer. The seed of "turn the array" two grades early, without the word.

**Fast:** count one row; then count by that number, once for each row.
**Sense:** more than one row's count; with at most five rows of five, never more than 25.
**Check:** count the other way (by columns) and land on the same number.
**Line:** "Count one row. Every row is the same."
**Trap:** counting the squares one by one and losing the place (skipping one, counting one twice). The row count makes the one-by-one count unnecessary; at grade 2 it is allowed once, as the check, when the learner wants it.
**Kit:** `array` with `unit: "square"` needs a `partition` action that draws the row lines, then the column lines, one per counted word, from a plain rectangle; `count` by rows with the total ticking; `count` by columns. Nothing else.
**Writer:** a capable cheaper model. One picture, one method, every step named here.

## Grade 3: multiplication meaning (`multiplication-meaning`)

### `equal-tray-total` — Trays with the same number on each

**Standard:** 3.OA.A.1, "interpret 5 × 7 as the total number of objects in 5 groups of 7 objects each." Also 3.OA.A.3, word problems "involving equal groups."

**Foundations first:** this is where the picture is born, so the lesson takes its time. (1) *Equal groups*: 7 trays, each holding exactly 5 counters; the app's own trays, so the lesson's trays are the problem's. (2) The long way to the total: 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 (family 6), written out so its length is felt. (3) The short way: 7 × 5, read "seven times five", and *times* means "groups of": seven groups of five. The first number counts the groups, the second says how many in each. The two numbers are *factors*; the total is the *product*. (4) The trays slide into a column and their counters fall into straight rows: 7 rows of 5. That is an *array*: equal groups lined up. The total label never changes while they move. "Every equal-groups problem can be laid out like this."

**Read:** "There are 7 trays with 5 counters on each tray. How many counters are there **altogether**?" Gold on "altogether": the total is what is missing. The three numbers, said in colors: groups 7 (blue), each 5 (violet), total ? (teal). Total missing: multiply. At a glance: more than 7, more than 5; a count by fives, so it ends in 5 or 0.

**The example:** 7 × 5, on the array just built. (1) The question is read; the three numbers are named on the picture: 7 rows at the left in blue, 5 in each row above in violet. (2) Count by rows: rows light one at a time while the total ticks 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35 (skip-counting, family 1: the fast count of a row). (3) The board writes `\cb{7}\times\cv{5}=35` in the picture's colors. (4) "Thirty-five counters."

**Fast:** count by the tray size, once per tray; or know the fact.
**Sense:** more than either factor; six trays of five would be 30, so seven is a little more.
**Check:** count the array the other way, down the columns: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35.
**Line:** "Times means groups of."
**Trap:** adding the factors (7 + 5 = 12). One beat shows 12 counters beside the array of 35: twelve would not even fill three trays. The array makes "seven fives" a size, not a sum.
**Kit:** `array` with `layout: "groups"` (trays), `lineup` (total label fixed), `count` by rows, factor labels in blue and violet; the board writing the product in the same colors.
**Writer:** the strongest. The handoff from trays to array is the screenplay, and the pacing of a definition at grade 3 is not a recipe.

### `zero-one-groups` — Trays with nothing, one tray, one on each

**Standard:** 3.OA.A.1 at its edges: "the total number of objects in 5 groups of 7 objects each" when a group is empty or there is one group. Three cases in the variants (3 × 0, 1 × 8, 2 × 1), each a different picture, each short.

**Foundations first:** one line: "Times means groups of" (from `equal-tray-total`), with the trays. Nothing new is defined.

**Read:** the 0 or the 1 goes gold. At a glance: a 0 anywhere means no counters at all; a 1 means the total is the other number, nothing to count.

**The example:** "3 trays with 0 counters on each tray." (1) Three trays appear, empty. (2) Count what is on each: zero, zero, zero. (3) Line them up: three rows with nothing in them; there is no array to count. (4) `3\times 0=0`. "Three groups of nothing is nothing."
Two quick cases, different pictures: "1 tray with 8 counters": one tray, one row of 8; `1\times 8=8`, one group is just the group. "2 trays with 1 counter on each": two trays, two rows of one, a column of two dots; `2\times 1=2`, one in each: as many counters as trays.

**Fast:** a 0 factor: the total is 0. A 1 factor: the total is the other number.
**Sense:** empty trays hold nothing, however many there are; one tray holds what it holds.
**Check:** count the counters on the screen.
**Line:** "Empty groups: nothing. One group: just the group."
**Trap:** 3 × 0 = 3 (counting the trays instead of the counters) and 1 × 8 = 9 (adding). The three empty trays are counted aloud as trays, then as counters, and the two counts differ; the one row of 8 is counted and there is no ninth.
**Kit:** `array` groups with zero counters per tray; nothing new.
**Writer:** a capable cheaper model.

## Grade 3: multiplication facts (`multiplication-facts`)

### `multiply-facts` — Products of two one-digit numbers

**First:** the type holds four kinds of question under one method: an array given (and the turned fact asked), a card over an array, a bare fact, and a story of rows. The lesson teaches one way to see a fact and one dependable way to rebuild a forgotten one. Honest about the standard: by the end of the year these are known from memory; until then the array rebuilds any one in seconds.

**Standard:** 3.OA.C.7, "Fluently multiply and divide within 100, using strategies such as ... properties of operations. By the end of Grade 3, know from memory all products of two one-digit numbers." Also 3.OA.A.3 (the stories) and 3.OA.A.1.

**Foundations first:** a *fact* is a product of two one-digit numbers. Any fact is an array: the first factor rows, the second in each row ("times means groups of", named once). The product is counted by rows, never dot by dot. Two things the array shows that nothing else does, each built live before the examples: **Turn**: 5 rows of 8 lifts, quarter-turns, lands as 8 rows of 5; the labels swap sides but the colors follow the numbers; the dots never changed, so 5 × 8 and 8 × 5 are one fact and half the table comes free. **Cut**: a line slides across an array after the fifth row; the two parts have their own counts and they add to the whole. Why five: every one-digit factor is five and a little more, the fives are half the tens (5 × 8 is half of 80), and the little more is at most four rows, which doubling gives. So a forgotten fact is always two known facts.

**Read:** an array: rows and in-each are the factors. "Use it to find 8 × 5" under 5 rows of 8: the turned fact, same dots. A card: the first row and first column are left showing, and they are the two factors; the dots underneath need not be seen. A story: "rows of" and "in each row" are the array. At a glance, size: 8 rows of 9 is just under 8 rows of 10, so just under 80; parity: an even factor makes an even product.

**The example:** "The array shows 5 rows of 8 dots. Use it to find 8 × 5." (1) 5 at the left in blue, 8 above in violet. (2) Count by rows, the total ticking 8, 16, 24, 32, 40; `\cb{5}\times\cv{8}=40`. (3) The question asks eight times five: eight rows of five. The array turns; now 8 rows of 5; count by fives, 5, 10, ... 40; `\cv{8}\times\cb{5}=40`. Same dots. (4) 40.
Second example, the forgotten fact, fully worked because it is the method the other cases use: "What is 6 × 8?" Sketch the array: 6 rows of 8. Cut after five rows: 5 × 8 = 40 (half of 80) and 1 × 8 = 8; 40 + 8 = 48. Both parts named on the picture; the sum written under them.
Quick, the card: first row shows 9, first column shows 8: 8 rows of 9, 8 × 9. "You don't need to see them to count them." Lift the card: the hidden dots show in rust for one beat, then the fact: cut at five, 45 + 27 = 72.
Quick, the story: "A garden has 7 rows of bean plants, with 9 plants in each row." Rows and in-each: the array, 7 × 9; 45 + 18 = 63 plants. Two or three beats: the point is the reading.

**Fast:** know it. Forgotten: turn it, or cut it at five.
**Sense:** 8 rows of 9 is a little less than 80; 6 × 8 is a little more than 5 × 8 = 40.
**Check:** count by the other factor; or the last digit (an even factor, an even product).
**Line:** "Turn the array. The dots don't change." and "A fact you forgot is two facts you know."
**Trap:** adding the factors (6 + 8); counting every dot and losing count (the card is there precisely so counting is impossible); and, after a cut, answering the first part only (40 for 6 × 8). The two parts sit on the picture with their counts and the sum is written beneath before the answer is boxed.
**Kit:** `array` with `count`, `turn` (labels ride the numbers), `cover` and `lift` (rust reveal), `cut` at a row with per-part labels and products; the board sum in the parts' colors.
**Writer:** the strongest. Four kinds under one method, the turn and the cut staged rather than described, and pacing that stays patient across four screens (if the film runs past five minutes, the card and story cases can be trimmed to two beats each, never dropped).

### `multiplication-fact-grid-select` — Which facts have this product?

**First:** 11 × 8, 12 × 6, 7 × 12 in a grade-3 type whose standard says one-digit factors; the 4.NBT.B.5 link is a stretch. Keep it as the elevens-and-twelves extension and say so in the template. Select 2 of 6.

**Standard:** 3.OA.C.7, "know from memory all products of two one-digit numbers", extended to 11 and 12.

**Foundations first:** one number can be several arrays: 72 dots as 8 rows of 9 and as 6 rows of 12, side by side, both labeled 72. The eleven and twelve facts are not new facts: 12 rows of 6 cut after ten rows is 10 × 6 = 60 and 2 × 6 = 12, so 72 (the cut at ten; "the zeros ride along" is one grade away, so here it is just ten rows of six, sixty). And a product's last digit comes from the factors' last digits: 12 × 6 ends the way 2 × 6 does, because the ten rows end in 0. One beat on the picture; it is the rule-out.

**Read:** "Select the **2** multiplication facts that have a product of **72**." Gold on 72 and on 2: exactly two. At a glance, the last digit: 72 ends in 2. 7 × 9 ends in 3; 11 × 8 in 8; 7 × 12 in 4 (7 × 2 = 14); 5 × 12 in 0 (anything times 5 ends in 0 or 5). Four of six are gone before any multiplying. Size agrees: 5 × 12 is 60, too small; 11 × 8 is 88, too big.

**The example:** the grid 12 × 6 | 11 × 8 | 7 × 9 | 7 × 12 | 5 × 12 | 9 × 8. (1) The last-digit glance dims the four together, with the reason said once. (2) The two that remain are proved, not assumed: 12 × 6 on the array cut at ten, 60 + 12 = 72; 9 × 8 = 72, a known fact, 72 dots in 9 rows. (3) Both lit; "two chosen, as the question asks." (4) One beat says the glance only throws out: a product ending in 2 could still be 12 or 112, so the survivors are always multiplied.

**Fast:** the last digit to throw out; multiply the rest; twelves are ten rows and two more.
**Sense:** 72 is just under 8 × 10 = 80, so a fact with an 8 in it and a partner near 9 is the shape to expect.
**Check:** exactly two chosen; both multiplied again.
**Rule out:** by last digit, then by size, all in one beat, choices dimming together.
**Line:** "Eleven and twelve: ten rows, then one or two more."
**Trap:** choosing one and stopping. The read counts the asked number, and the last beat counts the chosen ones. Also 12 × 6 computed as 62 or 66 (ten rows forgotten as sixty): the cut at ten writes 60 on the picture.
**Kit:** `dim` over a six-pill grid; `array` `cut` at row 10.
**Writer:** a capable cheaper model; the method and the rule-out are stated in full.

## Grade 3: division meaning (`division-meaning`)

### `share-or-group` — Share into groups, or make groups of a size

**First:** a mixed type with two kinds, and the standard's own two interpretations; it teaches reading. The pilot named 36 stickers and 54 apples; both quotients are 6, so a learner could take the 6 as the answer's habit. Use 36 ÷ 6 = 6 each and 48 eggs, 6 to a box = 8 boxes instead: the same divisor 6 is six friends in one and six per box in the other, which is 3.OA.A.2's two readings of one division made visible.

**Standard:** 3.OA.A.2, "interpret 56 ÷ 8 as the number of objects in each share when 56 objects are partitioned equally into 8 shares, or as a number of shares when 56 objects are partitioned into equal shares of 8 objects each." Also 3.OA.A.3.

**Foundations first:** the array has a total and two sides (named: rows, in each). Multiplication knows the sides and asks the total. *Division* knows the total and one side and asks the other: it is the missing side. Define ÷, "divided by": split into equal parts. The number split is the *total* and it is written first, always; the side you know is the *divisor*; the missing side is the *quotient*, the answer. Two words for how a story gives you the known side: *sharing*, when you know how many groups (deal one to each in turn); *grouping*, when you know how many in each (gather that many into a row). Both end as an array.

**Read:** three numbers: groups, each, total. The total is given, so divide; the missing one says what the answer means. "36 stickers shared equally by 6 friends. How many does **each** get?": groups 6 known, each missing. "48 eggs packed 6 to a box. How many **boxes**?": each 6 known, groups missing. At a glance: a share is less than the total; "6 times what is 36", "what times 6 is 48".

**The example (sharing):** 36 stickers, 6 friends. (1) 36 counters in teal with the label 36; six empty rows labeled as six friends in blue. (2) Deal: one round gives one sticker to each friend, six stickers, on "one each"; each round on its own word, the per-row count ticking 1, 2, ... 6, the pile shrinking to nothing. (3) What is left is an array: 6 rows of 6. The board writes `36\div\cb{6}=\cg{?}` beside `\cb{6}\times\cg{?}=36`: the division is a multiplication with a hole in it. The hole is 6. (4) "Each friend gets six stickers." Check: 6 × 6 = 36.
**The second example (grouping), fully worked because it is the other reading:** 48 eggs, 6 to a box. (1) 48 counters. (2) Bundle: six slide together into a row, one box; again; the box count ticks 1, 2, ... 8 until the pile is empty. (3) The array again: 8 rows of 6. `48\div\cv{6}=\cg{?}`, `\cg{?}\times\cv{6}=48`. The hole is 8. (4) "Eight boxes." Check: 8 × 6 = 48. One beat on the two together: both divide by six; in the first, six was the friends and the answer was stickers each; in the second, six was the eggs in a box and the answer was boxes. "The missing side says what the answer means."

**Fast:** total first; divide by the number you know; think "known side times what makes the total."
**Sense:** fewer than 36 each; fewer than 48 boxes; six friends sharing thirty-six get about half a dozen.
**Check:** multiply back: quotient × divisor = total, written every time.
**Line:** "Division is the missing side. The total goes first."
**Trap:** dividing the small number by the big one (6 ÷ 36): the total is the pile, and the pile is written first. Mislabeling the answer (8 eggs each instead of 8 boxes): the read names the missing side before any dealing starts, and the bundle makes boxes, not shares.
**Kit:** `array` `deal` (one round per word, per-row count ticking) and `bundle` (k at a time into a row, row count ticking); the equation with a gold `?`; the two-kind recap naming each by what happens in the story.
**Writer:** the strongest. Two kinds in one lesson, the deal and the bundle staged to end as one array, and a recap that must not become a recipe list.

### `share-whole-items` — Share counters equally among trays

**First:** this duplicates the sharing half of `share-or-group`; one lesson covers both, and the app should merge them. If it stays, it is `share-or-group`'s sharing example re-skinned to trays, short. Its seed 64 ÷ 8 = 8 is a square, so the answer equals the divisor and the learner cannot tell which number was which; seed 56 ÷ 8 = 7, the standard's own example.

**Standard:** 3.OA.A.2, "the number of objects in each share when 56 objects are partitioned equally into 8 shares."

**Foundations first:** named in one line each: division is the missing side; the total goes first; sharing is dealing.

**Read:** "Share 56 counters equally among 8 trays. How many belong on **each** tray?" Trays known (8), each missing. At a glance: 8 times what is 56; fewer than 56 each.

**The example:** 56 ÷ 8. (1) 56 counters, 8 empty trays in blue. (2) Deal a round: one per tray, eight gone; rounds counted 1 ... 7, the per-tray count ticking. (3) The array: 8 rows of 7. `56\div\cb{8}=7`; `\cb{8}\times 7=56`. (4) "Seven on each tray."

**Fast:** 8 times what makes 56. **Sense:** fewer than 56 on a tray; eight sevens is a little under eight eights. **Check:** 8 × 7 = 56. **Line:** "The total goes first." **Trap:** none beyond `share-or-group`'s. **Kit:** `array` `deal` with trays as the rows. **Writer:** a capable cheaper model.

### `measure-whole-pieces` — How many pieces of this length?

**Standard:** 3.OA.A.3, "word problems in situations involving ... measurement quantities"; the grouping reading of 3.OA.A.2 with a length as the total.

**Foundations first:** a length is a total too. A ribbon 12 cm long is drawn to scale above a centimeter ruler (family 19, named: a ruler is a number line on a tool). A piece 4 cm long is a group: four centimeters gathered. "How many pieces" is "how many fours fit in twelve": grouping, so division, 12 ÷ 4.

**Read:** "A ribbon is 12 cm long. Cut it into pieces exactly 4 cm long. How many **pieces**?" Total 12 and each 4 given; groups missing. Gold on "pieces": the answer is a count of pieces, not centimeters. At a glance: 4 times what is 12; fewer pieces than centimeters.

**The example:** 12 cm, pieces of 4. (1) The ribbon on the ruler, 0 to 12. (2) A 4-cm piece is measured off and cut on the word; a hop of 4 lands on the ruler at 4; again at 8; again at 12, the ribbon used up; the pieces light and are counted 1, 2, 3. (3) `12\div 4=3`; `3\times 4=12`. (4) "Three pieces."

**Fast:** hop by the piece length to the total; count the hops. **Sense:** three pieces of four make twelve, and three is less than twelve. **Check:** 3 × 4 = 12. **Line:** "How many fours make twelve? That is twelve divided by four."
**Trap:** answering "3 cm" (the unit of the total, not of the count): the three pieces are counted as pieces on the picture. Subtracting once (12 − 4 = 8): one hop is one cut; the hops continue until the ribbon is gone.
**Kit:** `bar` (exists) to scale with cuts on the word and its amount scale in cm, plus the specified `numberline` `arcs` for the hops; or the app's ruler scene. Nothing new.
**Writer:** a capable cheaper model.

## Grade 3: division facts (`division-facts`)

### `divide-facts` — A division from a multiplication fact

**Standard:** 3.OA.B.6, "find 32 ÷ 8 by finding the number that makes 32 when multiplied by 8"; 3.OA.C.7, "the relationship between multiplication and division (e.g., knowing that 8 × 5 = 40, one knows 40 ÷ 5 = 8)"; 3.OA.A.3 for the stories.

**Foundations first:** named in one line: division is the missing side, the total goes first. Built: an array with one side hidden. 63 dots in 9 rows, the row length covered and marked `?` in gold. The division 63 ÷ 9 asks the same thing as `9\times\cg{?}=63`: a multiplication fact with a hole in it. The fact you know fills the hole.

**Read:** "What is 63 ÷ 9?" The total first; "nine times what is sixty-three." A given fact ("9 × 2 = 18. Use this fact to find 18 ÷ 2"): the three numbers are already on the table; the total goes first in the division and the divisor is the factor named. A story: "81 chairs in 9 equal rows" asks the length of a row; "27 chairs in rows of 3" asks the number of rows; either way the total goes first. At a glance: the quotient is less than the total; 63 ÷ 9 is a little less than 70 ÷ 10 = 7.

**The example:** 63 ÷ 9. (1) The array, 9 rows, each row's length hidden; `63\div 9=\cg{?}` and `9\times\cg{?}=63` side by side. (2) Count by nines down the columns, 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63: seven columns; the cover slides off and each row has 7. (3) The hole is 7: `9\times 7=63`, so `63\div 9=7`. (4) Check written: 9 × 7 = 63.
Quick, the given fact (the standard's exact clause): "9 × 2 = 18. Find 18 ÷ 2." The fact is 9 rows of 2; the division writes the total first, 18, then the divisor named, 2; the hole is the other factor: 9. Two beats.
Quick, a story: "27 chairs in rows of 3. How many rows?" `\cg{?}\times 3=27`; 9. Two beats; the reading is the point.

**Fast:** think "divisor times what makes the total"; or count by the divisor and count the hops.
**Sense:** less than the total; near the rounded estimate.
**Check:** multiply back, every time.
**Line:** "Division is a multiplication with a hole in it."
**Trap:** reading the wrong number out of a given fact (9 × 2 = 18, so 18 ÷ 2 = 2): the 2 is already in the division, so it cannot also be the answer; the array shows the hole is the other side. A skip count off by one (63 ÷ 9 = 8): multiplying back catches it in a second.
**Kit:** `array` with one side covered and a gold `?` label; `count` by columns; `cover` and `lift`. Nothing new.
**Writer:** a capable cheaper model.

### `array-division-slots` — Which number goes where?

**First:** the answer format places three numbers into total, divisor and quotient slots; the kit must know what the slot menu offers so the rule-out is honest. Two cases in the variants (divide by rows to find each; divide by each to find rows), both needed, the second short.

**Standard:** 3.OA.A.2, both readings of one quotient; 3.OA.A.3, "equations with a symbol for the unknown number to represent the problem."

**Foundations first:** one array, 7 rows of 4, with its three numbers in their colors: rows 7 blue, in each 4 violet, total 28 teal (counted by rows, 4, 8, ... 28, and written as `\cb{7}\times\cv{4}=28`). The one array holds two divisions, because either side can be the missing one: deal 28 into 7 rows and each has 4, `28\div\cb{7}=\cv{4}`; bundle 28 into rows of 4 and there are 7 rows, `28\div\cv{4}=\cb{7}`. The total goes first in both. What comes next is the side you *use*; what comes last is the side you *find*.

**Read:** "The array has 7 rows with 4 counters in each row. Complete the division that **uses the number of rows** to **find the number in each row**." Gold on "uses" and "find": the number you use is the divisor; the number you find is the quotient. At a glance: the biggest number is the total and goes first; two true divisions exist and the words choose one.

**The example:** (1) The array and its three numbers; the total 28 drops into the first slot: "the total goes first." (2) "Uses the number of rows": the 7 drops into the divisor slot, and the array shows why: 28 dealt into 7 rows. (3) "Find the number in each row": each row has 4; the 4 drops into the quotient slot. `28\div\cb{7}=\cv{4}`. (4) Check: 7 × 4 = 28.
Quick second case: 9 rows of 3, "uses the number in each row to find the number of rows": 27 first; bundle into rows of 3: `27\div\cv{3}=\cb{9}`; 9 × 3 = 27. Three beats.

**Fast:** total first; the number the question says "uses" next; the other number last.
**Sense:** the big number is first and alone; the two small ones follow.
**Check:** quotient × divisor = total.
**Rule out:** anything other than the total in the first slot; if the menu offers a number not in the array (a sum like 11), it is nothing the array can show.
**Line:** "The total goes first. Divide by the one you use."
**Trap:** writing the other true division (28 ÷ 4 = 7) when the question asked to use the rows. Both are true of the array, but the question chose; the gold word "uses" and the deal motion fix the divisor before the slot is filled. Also the total anywhere but first (7 ÷ 28): the pile is what gets split.
**Kit:** `array` `deal` and `bundle` on the same array; slot cards on the board in the three colors, filled on the word.
**Writer:** a capable cheaper model; the method is fully stated and the picture is one array with two motions.
