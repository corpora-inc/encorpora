## Whole multiplication (`whole-multiplication`, grade 4)

### `facts-to-twelve` — Facts with 11 and 12.

**Misfiled.** A facts type (products and quotients with 11 or 12) filed under the grade-4 algorithm standard. It is the elevens-and-twelves extension of `multiplication-facts` and `division-facts` and should be filed there; 4.NBT.B.5 is not served. Mixing multiplication and division stories in one type is fine here, because the lesson's whole point is that they are one fact.

**Standard.** 3.OA.C.7, "Fluently multiply and divide within 100, using strategies such as the relationship between multiplication and division (e.g., knowing that 8 × 5 = 40, one knows 40 ÷ 5 = 8)", extended past 100 by 11 × 12 = 132. The stories practice 3.OA.A.3, "solve word problems in situations involving equal groups". Foundations 1, 4, 6, 7.

**Foundations first.** One line each, with the picture: times means groups of; division is the missing side; three numbers, groups, each, total, one missing. Then the one new thing: a row of 11 is a rod and a square; a row of 12 is a rod and two squares. So an array of elevens or twelves is cut after the tenth column into a part you can count by tens and a small part you already know. "Eleven of anything is ten of them and one more."

**Read.** "Student B buys 11 packs with 7 stickers in each pack. How many stickers?" Groups 11, each 7, total missing: multiply. At a glance: a bit more than 7 tens, so in the seventies.

**The example.** Array 7 rows of 11 dots, blue 7 on the left, violet 11 above. (1) The cut slides in after the tenth column: 7 rows of 10 and 7 rows of 1. (2) The big part counts by tens, 10, 20 ... 70, and the dots of each row of ten fuse into a rod: 7 rods. (3) The small part is 7 squares. (4) 70 + 7 = 77 written under the array in the parts' colors; the total label reads 77. (5) The board: 11 × 7 = 70 + 7 = 77. Eleven times a digit writes the digit twice, and the picture says why: a rod and a square per row.

**Second example (a different case: the total is given).** "What is 96 ÷ 12?" Total 96, each row 12, rows missing. The array is 12 wide, height "?". Twelve times what is 96? Cut 12 as 10 | 2: ten rows of ten is 80... no: ten times the rows plus two times the rows. Try 8: 10 × 8 = 80, 2 × 8 = 16, 96. Eight rows. The board: 12 × ? = 96, then 12 × 8 = 96, then 96 ÷ 12 = 8. A one-beat note on 132 ÷ 11: 11 × 10 = 110, 22 left is 11 × 2, so 12; the digit-twice pattern stops at nine because 11 × 12 is 120 + 12, not 1212.

**Fast.** Multiply: cut off the tens, add the small part. Divide: say the multiplication with a hole and fill it; try the tens first (12 × 8 is 80 and 16).

**Sense.** Eleven sevens is just over seven tens. Ninety-six split into twelves is a little fewer than split into tens, so under ten.

**Check.** 77 − 7 = 70, seven tens. 12 × 8 = 96 said aloud; for a story, the three numbers read back as a sentence (eight strands of twelve beads is ninety-six).

**Line.** "Eleven is ten and one. Twelve is ten and two. Cut off the tens."

**Trap.** Stretching the digit-twice pattern past nine: 11 × 12 = 1212 or 112. The cut shows 120 + 12. In stories, multiplying when the total was given (77 pages over 11 days as 847): the three-number read, said before any work.

**Kit.** `array` with `cut` at column 10 and the fused-rod relabel; nothing new.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one picture, one method, the concept names every number.

## Whole division (`whole-division`, grade 4)

### `division-area-model` — Read a division as an area model.

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.6, "Illustrate and explain the calculation by using equations, rectangular arrays, and/or area models", and for the `w` variants 4.MD.A.3, "find the width of a rectangular room given the area of the flooring and the length, by viewing the area formula as a multiplication equation with an unknown factor". Foundations 6, 9, 10. Three question shapes under one name: the equation 7 × w = 350; the quotient from two parts; the equation of partial quotients from three parts. One idea, three readings.

**Foundations first.** A rectangle's area is height times width (named from family 24, one line). An area model of a division is a rectangle whose area and height are known and whose width is missing: 5 × w = 290, a multiplication with a hole; the quotient is the missing side (6). The hole is filled a piece at a time: the area is cut into parts that the height divides easily (hundreds, tens, ones), each part's width is a *partial quotient*, and the widths add up along the top. The first time, say the handoff: "The areas sit inside. The quotient is the top edge."

**Read.** "A rectangle 8 units tall has its area split into two columns with areas 240 and 40. What is the quotient of 280 ÷ 8?" The gold word is "quotient": the whole width, not an area and not one part. At a glance: 280 ÷ 8 is about 240 ÷ 8 = 30 and a bit; the quotient is in the thirties.

**The example.** To scale: a rectangle 8 tall, cut at the true fraction into a part 30 wide and a part 5 wide; the ten-lines make the big part 8 rows of 3 rods and the small part 8 rows of 5 squares. (1) Areas written inside: 240, 40. (2) The height 8 outside the left side; "?" in gold along the top. (3) The big part: 8 times what is 240? 8 × 30; "30" appears above it. (4) The small part: 8 × 5 = 40; "5" above it. (5) The widths add along the top: 30 + 5 = 35. The board: 8 × w = 280; 240 ÷ 8 = 30; 40 ÷ 8 = 5; 35. The app's own sketch draws the parts as equal boxes; the lesson says once that the real rectangle has the parts at their true size, and that only the numbers inside matter for the work.

**Second example (the three-part question, worked fully because its choices are the misconceptions).** "An area model divides 548 by 4. The rectangle is 4 units tall; its area is split into 400, 120 and 28. Which equation shows the quotient?" The rectangle is a strip 4 tall and 137 wide (a one-digit divisor always makes a strip; drawn to scale across the card, parts 100, 30 and 7 wide, the 28's width label outside with a leader). Divide each part by 4: 100, 30, 7; widths add: 137. The answer is the equation whose terms are the widths: 100 + 30 + 7 = 137.

**Quick case (the equation shape).** "Area 350 square meters, height 7, width w. Which equation shows 350 ÷ 7 as an area model?" Height times width is area: 7 × w = 350.

**Fast.** Divide each part by the height, add the widths. Start with the smallest part (28 ÷ 4 = 7): it settles most choices at once.

**Sense.** 548 ÷ 4 is about 500 ÷ 4 = 125: three digits beginning with 1. A width is always smaller than the area when the height is more than 1.

**Check.** Multiply back: 137 × 4 = 400 + 120 + 28, the parts come back. 35 × 8 = 280.

**Rule out.** Three parts: the equation whose sum is the area (400 + 120 + 28 = 548) names areas, not widths; the one with 28 as a term kept an area undivided; the one starting 10 has 10 × 4 = 40, not 400: all dim in one beat. The equation question: w ÷ 7 = 350 and 350 × 7 = w make the width bigger than the whole area; 7 + w = 350 adds, and an area is a product.

**Line.** "The areas are inside. The quotient is the width along the top."

**Trap.** Adding the three areas and calling the sum the quotient; it is the choice every variant offers. The picture puts the areas inside and the widths above, in different colors, and the voice reads the top edge.

**Kit.** `area` with a missing side, true-scale parts in a strip, labels with leaders for thin parts; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest: the picture is being handed off here (dots to a rectangle with the count written in; the top edge as the quotient), and the three readings must not become three recipes.

### `divide-by-one-digit` — Divide a number up to four digits by a one-digit number.

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.6, "Find whole-number quotients and remainders with up to four-digit dividends and one-digit divisors, using strategies based on place value ... Illustrate and explain the calculation by using equations, rectangular arrays, and/or area models." Foundations 6, 10; family 2's trade in reverse. Pilot lesson 4.

**Foundations first.** Division is the missing side; the total goes first (one line). The dividend is built in the blocks chart (thousands, hundreds, tens, ones; the thousand as its own piece), with four empty rows beneath: the divisor is the number of rows, the quotient is what each row gets. Share the biggest place first. A place that will not share goes to the row-sharing as nothing and is traded down, ten for one, into the next place: the same trade as family 2, run backwards. Beside the chart, the bracket is introduced as the same work written down: divisor outside, dividend inside, the quotient on top under the chart's place headers. Each quotient digit stands over the place it came from.

**Read.** "What is 2,416 ÷ 4?" Total 2,416, four rows, how many in each. At a glance: 4 does not fit into 2, so the thousands will not share and the quotient has three digits; 2,400 ÷ 4 = 600, so about 600.

**The example.** (1) 2 thousands, 4 flats, 1 rod, 6 squares in the chart; four rows below. (2) Thousands: two pieces cannot be dealt into four rows. Trade: each thousand splits into ten flats; 24 flats. (3) Deal the flats: 6 to each row, 24 used, none left. On the bracket, on the same words: 6 written over the hundreds, 24 under the 24, subtract, 0. (4) Tens: one rod, four rows. It cannot be shared: each row gets 0 rods. The 0 is written over the tens, tinted, on the word. Trade the rod for ten squares: 16 squares. On the bracket: bring down the 1, 0 times 4 is 0, 1 left, bring down the 6. (5) Deal 16 squares: 4 each. On the bracket: 4 over the ones, 16, 0. (6) Each row holds 6 flats, 0 rods, 4 squares: 604. The quotient row reads 604 under the four rows and on top of the bracket.

**Second example (fully worked: a place that shares with something left).** "What is 348 ÷ 4?" In 2,416 every share came out even; here 3 flats trade to 30 rods, 34 rods share 8 each (32), 2 rods are left and trade to 20 squares, 28 squares share 7 each. On the bracket: 8 over the tens, 32, 2 left, bring down 8, 28, 7, 0. The leftover rods that come down to the next place are the bring-down. 87.

**Fast.** Biggest place first; write a 0 when a place will not share; bring down; the quotient's digits sit over the dividend's places.

**Sense.** About 600; three digits, as the glance said. 87 is under 100 because 4 × 100 = 400 > 348.

**Check.** Multiply back, written every time: 604 × 4 = 2,416; 87 × 4 = 348.

**Line.** "Share the biggest place first. What won't share, trade down."

**Trap.** Losing the zero: 2,416 ÷ 4 = 64. The rod that cannot be shared four ways is drawn, each row visibly gets no rods, the 0 is written over the tens on the word, and the headers over the quotient show 64 would be sixty-four, not six hundred four.

**Kit.** `blocks` with `share` into n rows and a thousand piece (a 3D cube); `longdiv` with headers, `step` per place, tinted 0.

**Writer.** Strongest: this is the handoff from blocks to the bracket, and every motion must land on the word that writes the digit.

### `division-remainder` — Find the remainder.

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.6, "quotients and remainders with up to four-digit dividends and one-digit divisors". Foundations 10, 11.

**Foundations first.** Division shares into rows, biggest place first, trading down what will not share (named from `divide-by-one-digit` in one line, with the chart). The new word: the *remainder* is what is left at the end that cannot make another row, so it is always smaller than the divisor. If what is left is as big as the divisor, another row fits and the division is not finished.

**Read.** "What is the remainder when 493 is divided by 8?" Gold word: "remainder". The question wants only the leftover, but the leftover is found by doing the division. At a glance: the answer is one of 0 to 7; 8 does not fit into 4, so the quotient has two digits, about 480 ÷ 8 = 60.

**The example.** (1) 4 flats, 9 rods, 3 squares; eight rows. (2) Flats will not share eight ways; trade: 49 rods. (3) Deal: 6 rods each, 48 used, 1 rod left. Bracket: 6 over the tens, 48, 1. (4) Trade the rod: 13 squares. Bring down the 3. (5) Deal: 1 square each, 8 used; 5 squares left, in rust, outside the rows with their count. Bracket: 1 over the ones, 8, 5. (6) Five cannot make a row of eight. Quotient 61, remainder 5. The question asked for the remainder: 5.

**Quick case (a four-digit dividend).** 8,153 ÷ 9: 9 into 8 does not fit; 81 shares 9 each; 5 will not share, write 0; 53 shares 5 each with 8 left. 905 R 8. One beat, if the divisor is 9: eight plus one plus five plus three is seventeen, one plus seven is eight: the digit sum of a number tells its remainder when divided by nine, because every rod and every flat leaves exactly one square when dealt into nines (the `nines` picture, shown once). It is a check, not the method.

**Fast.** Run the division; the remainder is the last number left; it must be smaller than the divisor.

**Sense.** Smaller than 8. The quotient is near 60, as estimated.

**Check.** 8 × 61 + 5 = 488 + 5 = 493, written on the board.

**Line.** "What's left is always smaller than a group."

**Trap.** Stopping too early: 60 rows of 8 use 480, 13 left, "60 R 13". Thirteen rust squares make one more row of eight with five left; the picture is the proof that 13 is not finished. Also answering 61 to a question that said "remainder": the gold word, and the answer line says "remainder 5".

**Kit.** `blocks` `share` with the leftover held in rust; `longdiv` `remainder`; `nines` for the one-beat check.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model, once `divide-by-one-digit` has set the blocks-and-bracket idiom.

### `quotient-remainder-one-digit` — Full groups and what is left, as a count or as a fraction of a group.

**Off grade and seeded badly.** The "what fraction of a box is left" variants are 5.NF.B.3 inside a grade-4 type; keep them as the lesson's second case. The film seed (socks in pairs of 2) hides every idea: a remainder of 0 or 1 teaches nothing. Seed a divisor of 8 or 9.

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.6, "quotients and remainders with up to four-digit dividends and one-digit divisors"; 4.OA.A.3, "problems in which remainders must be interpreted"; for the fraction case 5.NF.B.3, "when 3 wholes are shared equally among 4 people each person has a share of size 3/4" applied to a part of a group. Foundations 7, 10, 11.

**Foundations first.** The three numbers: total, size of a group, number of full groups missing: divide. Then, before any division, what the story does with the leftover: here it is reported, either as a count ("how many muffins are left over") or as a fraction of a group ("what fraction of a box is left"): the leftover muffins fill part of one more box, and the part is leftover over group size. The division with its remainder (named from `division-remainder`, one line).

**Read.** "The bakery has 2,073 muffins to pack into boxes of 9. How many full boxes can be filled, and how many muffins are left over?" Total 2,073, group 9, full boxes missing; the leftover is reported as a count. At a glance: 9 does not fit into 2, so the quotient has three digits; 2,073 is about 1,800 + 270, so about 230; the leftover is 0 to 8.

**The example.** (1) 2 thousands, 0 flats, 7 rods, 3 squares; nine rows. (2) Thousands trade to 20 flats; 2 each, 18 used, 2 flats left. Bracket: 2 over the hundreds, 18, 2. (3) Two flats trade to 20 rods, 27 rods; 3 each, 27 used, none left. Bracket: 3 over the tens, 27, 0. (4) Bring down the 3 squares: three cannot be shared nine ways; 0 over the ones. Three rust squares outside the rows. (5) 230 full boxes; 3 muffins left over. Each slot filled from the picture: the quotient row 230, the rust count 3.

**Second example (a different question about the same leftover).** "The photo studio has 9,469 photos to put on album pages of 4. How many full pages can be filled, and what fraction of a page is left?" 9,469 ÷ 4: 9 shares 2 (8), 1 left; 14 shares 3 (12), 2 left; 26 shares 6 (24), 2 left; 29 shares 7 (28), 1 left. 2,367 full pages, 1 photo left. A page of four is a 2 × 2 grid; the one rust photo sits in one of its four spaces: one fourth of a page. The fraction is the leftover over the group size, 1 over 4; the slot wants the top number, 1.

**Fast.** Divide; the quotient is the full groups; the remainder is the leftover count; the fraction is remainder over divisor.

**Sense.** The leftover is smaller than a group. The fraction is less than one whole page. The quotient matches the estimate.

**Check.** 9 × 230 + 3 = 2,073; 4 × 2,367 + 1 = 9,469. Written both times.

**Line.** "What's left is part of one more group: leftover over group size."

**Trap.** Writing the leftover as a decimal (230.3) or as a fraction of the wrong thing (1 over 2,367). The leftover is drawn sitting in a group of the divisor's size: three muffins in a box of nine, one photo on a page of four. There is nothing to put the quotient under.

**Kit.** `blocks` `share`, `longdiv` with `remainder` written as R n and as n over the divisor; a small group grid (`grid` 2 × 2 or 3 × 3) that the rust leftover slides into.

**Writer.** Strongest: a mixed type that teaches reading the leftover before dividing, with two slots per answer.

## Factors and multiples (`factors-multiples`, grade 4)

### `mcas-prime-factor-select` — Select the factors of a number, or the primes in a list.

**Two questions under one name**, as the design noted: a factor question (select 3 of 6) and a prime question (select 2 of 5). They share one picture, the rectangles of a number, so one lesson teaches both as two kinds, never as one recipe.

**Standard.** 4.OA.B.4, "Find all factor pairs for a whole number in the range 1–100" and "Determine whether a given whole number in the range 1–100 is prime or composite." Foundation 12.

**Foundations first.** Define *factor* on the picture: a factor of 54 is a side a rectangle of 54 dots can have; factors come in pairs, the two sides; 1 and 54 are always a pair (the single row). The pairs walk in from both ends: 1 × 54, 2 × 27, 3 × 18, 6 × 9; 4 and 5 do not divide; at 9 the partner is 6, a rectangle already drawn turned on its side, so the walk stops. Define *prime*: a number whose only rectangle is the single row, so its only factors are 1 and itself. 54 has four rectangles; 7 has one.

**Read, kind A.** "Which 3 of these are factors of 54? 108, 48, 27, 6, 31, 9." Gold: "factors of 54". At a glance: a factor fits inside the number, so 108 is gone before any work. The answer is three numbers from the pairs list.

**The example, kind A.** (1) The rectangles of 54 walk in to scale, each pair written in the two-column pairs card: 1 | 54, 2 | 27, 3 | 18, 4 muted (54 is 52 and 2), 5 muted (does not end in 0 or 5), 6 | 9; 7 and 8 muted; 9's partner is 6: stop line. (2) The choices against the list: 27, 6, 9 are in it; 48 and 31 are not (54 ÷ 48 leaves 6; 54 ÷ 31 leaves 23). (3) 27, 6, 9.

**Read, kind B.** "Which 2 of these are prime? 81, 61, 49, 93, 53." Gold: "prime". At a glance: 81 and 49 are squares you know (9 × 9, 7 × 7): two rectangles at least, gone. Nothing here is even or ends in 5.

**The example, kind B.** The sure test under 121: try 2, 3, 5 and 7; if none divides, it is prime. Why only four, in one line: a composite number has a factor no bigger than its square root, and 11 × 11 is 121. (1) 93: digits 9 + 3 = 12, so 3 divides it: 3 × 31. Gone. (2) 61: odd; 6 + 1 = 7, not 3; not 5; 7 × 8 = 56, 7 × 9 = 63, not 7. Prime: one rectangle, drawn as the single row. (3) 53: odd; 5 + 3 = 8; not 5; 7 × 7 = 49, 7 × 8 = 56, not 7. Prime. (4) 61 and 53.

**Fast.** Factors: pairs from 1 up, stop when the partner is smaller than the trial. Primes under 121: 2, 3, 5, 7.

**Sense.** Three factors chosen, two primes chosen, as each question said. No factor is bigger than 54.

**Check.** Each chosen factor's pair multiplied back: 2 × 27, 6 × 9. Each chosen prime rechecked against the four trials.

**Rule out.** Factors: anything bigger than the number dims first; then anything not on the pairs list. Primes: squares and numbers whose digits add to a multiple of 3 dim together, with the reason said.

**Line.** "Pairs meet in the middle. Under a hundred: not 2, 3, 5 or 7, then prime."

**Trap.** Taking 108 for a factor of 54 (a multiple is a full count that contains 54; a factor fits inside). Calling 93 prime because it is odd and "looks prime": the digit sum, and the rectangle 3 × 31 drawn. The app's 91 and 77 elsewhere in this skill are the same trap.

**Kit.** `area` `rectangles` with `walkin` and the turned twin; `pairs` with muted trials and the stop line; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest: two kinds in one lesson, and the walk-in with its stop is a picture being born.

### `divisibility-rule-check` — Which number is divisible by d; which hidden digit makes it so.

**Misfiled and past range.** Linked to 4.NBT.B.6, which it does not serve. Divisibility rules are not in the Common Core; the closest clause is 4.OA.B.4, "Determine whether a given whole number in the range 1–100 is a multiple of a given one-digit number", and the numbers (7,862; 8,628) are far past 100. Keep it as common grade-4 content under 4.OA.B.4, and say in the template that the range is extended.

**Standard.** 4.OA.B.4, the multiples clause above, extended. Foundation 12 (divisibility as place value), with family 2's blocks.

**Foundations first.** "Divisible by 4" means rows of 4 use it all, nothing left (one line, the array). Then why the rules work, each shown once in blocks and never as a list: every rod is two fives and five twos and one ten, and every flat too, so for 2, 5 and 10 only the loose squares, the ones digit, can leave anything. A flat is four twenty-fives, so for 4 every flat shares out and only the rods and squares, the last two digits, decide. For 9 (and 3): take nine squares from every rod and ninety-nine from every flat; each piece leaves exactly one square, so the whole number leaves its digit sum, and the number shares into nines exactly when its digit sum does (`nines`). For 6: rows of 6 are rows of 2 and rows of 3, so both tests must pass.

**Read.** "Which number is divisible by 4? 7,862, 8,628, 9,090, 7,329." Gold: "divisible by 4". At a glance: 7,329 is odd, and anything divisible by 4 is even: gone. The others are decided by their last two digits.

**The example.** (1) 7,862: last two digits 62; 4 × 15 = 60, 2 left: no. (2) 8,628: 28 is 4 × 7: yes. (3) 9,090: 90 is 88 and 2: no. (4) 8,628. On screen the thousands and hundreds of each number fade to muted when the rule is said, so only the two digits that matter stay bright.

**Second example (a different kind of question: the hidden digit).** "One digit is hidden: 47▢5. Which digit can replace ▢ so that the number is divisible by 3? 1, 5, 9, 0." The digits you can see add to 16. Each choice: 17, 21, 25, 16; only 21 is a multiple of 3. The digit is 5; 4,755 = 3 × 1,585. Quick beat for 6: 15▢ with choices 1, 7, 8, 0: the number must be even (1 and 7 dim) and its digits must add to a multiple of 3: 1 + 5 + 8 = 14 no, 1 + 5 + 0 = 6 yes. 0; 150 = 6 × 25.

**Fast.** The rule that fits the divisor: last digit (2, 5, 10), last two digits (4), digit sum (3, 9), both 2 and 3 (6). For a hidden digit, try each choice in the rule.

**Sense.** A number divisible by 4 or 6 is even; a number divisible by 3 need not end in 3, 6 or 9.

**Check.** Divide the chosen number: 8,628 ÷ 4 = 2,157. Add the digits once more: 4 + 7 + 5 + 5 = 21.

**Rule out.** For 4 and 6: odd choices dim together. For 6: choices that leave the number odd dim first. For 3: nothing dims at a glance; the digit sum decides each.

**Line.** "Ends decide 2, 5, 10 and 4. The digit sum decides 3 and 9."

**Trap.** Using the 2-rule for 4: 9,090 ends in 0, so "divisible by 4". The flat is four twenty-fives but the rods are not: 90 is 88 and 2. Judging 3 by the last digit (47▢5 ends in 5, "so not 3"); the digit-sum picture. Checking only 2 or only 3 for 6: 158 is even and fails.

**Kit.** `blocks` with `nines` and a `quarters` split of a flat into four 25s; a digit-row fade for the places the rule ignores; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest: the value of this lesson is the *why* in blocks, and a weak author will write the rule list.

### `prime-composite-sort` — Which number is prime, composite, or neither.

**Standard.** 4.OA.B.4, "Determine whether a given whole number in the range 1–100 is prime or composite." Foundation 12.

**Foundations first.** The rectangles of a number, to scale (one line from `factor-pairs-select` if it has been seen; otherwise built here): 12 has three, 7 has one. *Prime*: a number bigger than 1 whose only rectangle is the single row. *Composite*: a number with more than one rectangle, so a factor other than 1 and itself. *1*: one dot; its only rectangle is one row that is also one column, and its only factor is 1 itself; it is neither prime nor composite, by definition. The sure test under 121: try 2, 3, 5 and 7, and why only those (a composite has a factor no bigger than its square root; 11 × 11 = 121).

**Read.** "Which number is prime? 77, 11, 91, 65." Gold: "prime". At a glance: 65 ends in 5, so 5 divides it: gone. Every choice is odd, so 2 rules out nothing here; odd does not mean prime.

**The example.** (1) 77: 7 × 11, a fact from the elevens: two rectangles, drawn. Gone. (2) 91: odd; 9 + 1 = 10, not 3; not 5; 7 × 13 = 91. The rectangle 7 × 13 walks in. Gone. (3) 11: not 2, 3, 5 or 7; the single row; prime. (4) 11.

**Quick cases.** "Which is composite? 43, 83, 19, 91": 43, 83 and 19 each pass the four trials; 91 is 7 × 13 again. "Which is neither? 49, 3, 91, 1": the single dot, 1.

**Fast.** Under 121: 2 (even), 3 (digit sum), 5 (last digit), 7 (the sevens up to 7 × 17). Nothing goes in: prime.

**Sense.** A prime over 2 is odd, but most odd numbers in the choices are not prime; that is what the choices are made of.

**Check.** For the composite, name its rectangle (7 × 13 = 91). For the prime, say the four trials once more.

**Rule out.** Even numbers (except 2) and numbers ending in 5 (except 5) dim at once; squares the learner knows (49, 81) dim; the rest take the digit sum and the sevens.

**Line.** "Under a hundred: not 2, 3, 5 or 7, then prime."

**Trap.** "Odd means prime" (91, 77, 65, 51 are the classic traps and the app uses three of them); "1 is prime"; "2 is not prime because it is even". The rectangles of 91 and the single dot for 1; one beat on 2 as the only even prime, its single row of two.

**Kit.** `area` `rectangles` for small numbers; `pairs`; `dim`. Nothing new.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method stated in full, with the pictures named.

### `factor-partner` — The other number in a factor pair.

**Standard.** 4.OA.B.4, "Find all factor pairs for a whole number in the range 1–100." Foundation 6 (the missing side), 12. One variant only (90 and 10); the lesson adds a second pair the learner will meet, so the idea is built and not one fact.

**Foundations first.** A *factor pair* is the two sides of a rectangle: 90 dots in rows of 10 make a rectangle 10 wide, and its other side is the partner. Finding the partner is division, the missing side: 10 × ? = 90.

**Read.** "One factor of 90 is 10. What is its partner in a factor pair with product 90?" At a glance: rows of 10 are rods; 90 is 9 rods, so the answer is 9 before any work.

**The example.** (1) The rectangle, height 10 in blue, area 90 inside, width "?" in gold. (2) Each row of 10 is a rod; 90 is nine rods stacked: 9 rows. (3) 10 × 9 = 90, so 90 ÷ 10 = 9; the "?" becomes 9 in violet. (4) 9.

**Quick case (a pair that is not tens).** 42 with factor 6: rows of 6; 6 × 7 = 42; partner 7.

**Fast.** Divide the product by the known factor; or say the multiplication with a hole and fill it from a fact.

**Sense.** The partner is smaller than the product (unless the known factor is 1).

**Check.** 10 × 9 = 90.

**Line.** "A factor pair is two sides. The partner is the missing side."

**Trap.** None worth naming at this size; the realistic slip is answering another factor of 90 that is not the partner of 10, and the rectangle with 10 on its side makes that impossible.

**Kit.** `area` with a "?" side; nothing new.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model; a short lesson.

### `factor-pairs-select` — Select every factor of a number.

**Range.** 118, 86 and 94 are past 4.OA.B.4's 1–100; fine as a stretch, but the template should say so. Several variants are twice a prime (86, 94, 118), so most trials fail; the lesson must teach when to stop.

**Standard.** 4.OA.B.4, "Find all factor pairs for a whole number in the range 1–100." Foundation 12.

**Foundations first.** A factor is a side a rectangle of the number can have; factors come in pairs, the two sides; 1 and the number are always a pair, the single row. The pairs walk in from both ends and stop when the partner is smaller than the trial: past that, every rectangle is one already drawn, turned. The story reading: chairs in equal rows with none left over, tiles that make a rectangle using every tile, numbers that go in with no remainder: all ask for the factors.

**Read.** "Which of these numbers divide 50 with nothing left over? 51, 5, 2, 49, 25, 50, 10, 1." Gold: "nothing left over": factors of 50. At a glance: 1 and 50 are in; 51 is bigger than 50, out; 49 is one less than 50 and leaves 1, out. Even, so 2 is in; ends in 0, so 5 and 10 are in.

**The example.** (1) The rectangles of 50 walk in, to scale, side by side: 1 × 50 (a strip), 2 × 25, 5 × 10; the pairs card writes each pair as its rectangle lands: 1 | 50, 2 | 25, 3 muted (5 + 0 = 5, not 3), 4 muted (50 is 48 and 2), 5 | 10, 6 muted, 7 muted (7 × 7 = 49). (2) At 8 the partner would be smaller than 8 (8 × 6 = 48, 8 × 7 = 56): the stop line; a 10 × 5 rectangle turns and lands on the 5 × 10 already there. (3) The factors: 1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50. (4) The choices against the list: six chosen; 51 and 49 stay dim.

**Second example (fully worked: a stretch number with only two pairs, in a story).** "Student A is setting 118 chairs in equal rows with no chair left over. Which numbers of chairs could be in each row? 2, 3, 119, 59, 117, 1, 118." The number in each row must divide 118. 1 and 118 at once; 119 and 117 out at a glance. 2: even, partner 59. 3: 1 + 1 + 8 = 10, no. 4: 18 is 16 and 2, no. 5, 6, 7 (7 × 17 = 119), 8, 9, 10: no. 11 × 11 = 121 is past 118, so the partner of 11 would be smaller than 11: stop. Factors: 1, 2, 59, 118. Two rectangles only; the 1 × 118 strip and the 2 × 59 drawn to scale across the card.

**Fast.** 1 and the number first. 2 if even, 5 if it ends in 0 or 5, 3 by the digit sum. Then the remaining trials in order until the partner drops below the trial.

**Sense.** Nothing bigger than the number; nothing one more or one less than it (except for 1 and 2). A chosen number times its partner is the number.

**Check.** Multiply each chosen pair back (2 × 59 = 118; 5 × 10 = 50); count the chosen against the list.

**Rule out.** Choices bigger than the number, and the number minus one, dim together with the reason; odd numbers when the number is odd keep 2 out at a glance.

**Line.** "Pairs meet in the middle. One and the number itself are always a pair."

**Trap.** Forgetting 1 and the number itself: the single-row rectangle is drawn first, every time. Picking a near miss (3 for 118, 117 or 119): the digit sum, and the rectangle that will not close.

**Kit.** `area` `rectangles` with `walkin` and the turned twin; `pairs` with muted trials and the stop line; `dim`.

**Writer.** Strongest: the walk-in and its stop are a picture being born, and the select-many rule-out must be staged, not described.
