# Multiplication and division, part 2: per-type concepts

Ten types across grades 3 and 4. Every concept below stands on the family design: the array with its two sides labeled in blue and violet and its count in teal, the cut, the rectangle drawn to scale, and the five moves (read, at a glance, the sure fast way, sense, check). Each names the foundation it stands on in one line and never re-teaches it.

## Division facts (grade 3)

### `related-division-sentence` — The multiplication hiding in a division, and the division hiding in a fact

A note first: the goal and the app's method mention multiples of ten ("divide by 40, not 4"), but neither produced wording carries one. If the generator ever emits 240 ÷ 40, the method below does not change; one beat says the 40 is one number (foundation 8), not a 4 with a zero to lose.

**Standard.** 3.OA.B.6: "Understand division as an unknown-factor problem. For example, find 32 ÷ 8 by finding the number that makes 32 when multiplied by 8." 3.OA.C.7: "the relationship between multiplication and division (e.g., knowing that 8 × 5 = 40, one knows 40 ÷ 5 = 8)."

**Foundations first.** Foundation 6, built live. A 5 × 9 array: blue 5 at the left (rows), violet 9 above (in each row), teal 45 beneath as the live count by rows (9, 18, 27, 36, 45). Define *product*: the total, the dots. Define *factor*: a side. Write 5 × 9 = 45 in the three colors. Now hide the violet 9 and put a gold ? in its place: same array, same dots, one side unknown. That is 5 × ? = 45, and it is also 45 ÷ 5 = ?: the total goes first, then the side we know, and the answer is the side we don't. Say it once: "Division is the missing side. In a times sentence the total comes last; in a divide sentence it comes first." (Term cards: *product*, *factor*.)

**Read.** Two directions, so two reads. Given a division: the number before ÷ is the total; the other is a known side; n is the missing side. Given a fact: the biggest number is the total; a division that uses this fact must begin with it. At a glance: n is a side, so it is smaller than the total.

**The example (division to multiplication).** "Here is a division sentence: 45 ÷ 5 = n. Which multiplication sentence helps you find n?" Choices: 45 × 5 = n | 5 + n = 45 | 5 × n = 45 | 5 × 45 = n. (1) Gold on 45: the total; it goes first in a division, so in the multiplication it goes last. (2) The array: 5 rows, ? in each, 45 dots. (3) Write 5 × n = 45 beside it: the same picture with the hole. (4) Prove it: count by rows 9, 18, 27, 36, 45; the ? becomes 9. 5 × 9 = 45, so 45 ÷ 5 = 9.

**Second example (fact to division), different direction, briefer.** "Here is a multiplication fact: 5 × 4 = 20. Which division sentence can this fact help you solve?" Choices: 5 + 20 = n | 20 ÷ 5 = n | 5 ÷ 4 = n | 4 ÷ 20 = n. The array 5 rows of 4, 20 dots. A division from this fact starts at 20 and hides one side: 20 ÷ 5 = n (hide the 4) or 20 ÷ 4 = n (hide the 5). Only one of those is offered: 20 ÷ 5 = n. Fill it: n = 4; read the fact back.

**Fast.** Find the total (last in a times sentence, first in a divide sentence). Write the other sentence around it.

**Sense.** n is a side, so smaller than the total: 9 and 4 are.

**Check.** Put n in and read both sentences aloud about the one array: 5 × 9 = 45 and 45 ÷ 5 = 9 are the same dots.

**Rule out.** Anything with + is not groups: gone. In a multiplication, any choice with n after the equals sign is multiplying the total as if it were a side (45 × 5 would be 225 dots, not 45): both dim together. In a division, any choice that does not start with the total: 5 ÷ 4 and 4 ÷ 20 dim together.

**Line.** "Division is the missing side. The total goes first."

**Trap.** Putting the known numbers in any slot: 45 × 5 = n, 5 ÷ 45. The array makes it impossible: 45 is the dots, and dots cannot be a side.

**Kit.** `array` with a side label that can be replaced by a gold ?; `dim`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one picture, two directions, the rule-out fully stated.

## Operation properties (grade 3)

### `split-array-part` — Squares in one region of a cut array

Badly conceived for its filing: it sits under 3.OA.B.5 (the distributive property) but asks only for one region's count, which is a plain product. It practices 3.OA.A.1 and A.3. The lesson below answers the question the app asks and, because the picture is already the cut, spends two beats showing that the regions add to the whole, which is the B.5 idea the skill is named for. A variant "use regions A and B to find 7 × 8" would make the type serve its standard.

**Standard.** As practiced: 3.OA.A.1 ("interpret 5 × 7 as the total number of objects in 5 groups of 7 objects each") and 3.OA.A.3 ("equal groups, arrays"). Filed under 3.OA.B.5: "Knowing that 8 × 5 = 40 and 8 × 2 = 16, one can find 8 × 7 as 8 × (5 + 2)."

**Foundations first.** Foundations 2 and 4. Define *unit square*: one square is one; *array*: squares in rows, every row the same length; *row*, *column*. Build a 7-row, 8-column array of unit squares; blue 7 at the left, violet 8 above; count by rows 8, 16, ... 56; write 7 × 8 = 56. Then define *region*: a line cuts the array straight across, and the two parts are regions A and B. The cut changes nothing about the rows: every row is still 8 long, above the line and below it.

**Read.** "How many unit squares are in region B?" The letter goes gold. A region is rows of 8, so the question is: how many rows are in B? At a glance: B is the smaller part, so fewer than half of 56.

**The example.** The 7 × 8 array cut after 5 rows: A is the top 5 rows, B the bottom 2. (1) Gold on B. (2) Count B's rows: 1, 2 (each lights). (3) Each row is 8, the same 8 as above the cut: 2 rows of 8. (4) Count by rows: 8, 16. Region B has 16 squares. (5) Two beats more: A is 5 rows of 8, 40; 40 + 16 = 56, the whole array, which never changed. "Cut it, count the parts, add."

**Quick second case.** A region that is one row (the app's answer 5 for B): one row of 5 is 5. Foundation 5 in one beat.

**Fast.** Rows in the region times squares in one row.

**Sense.** B is smaller than A and both together make the whole.

**Check.** A + B = 56 = 7 × 8.

**Line.** "Cut it, count the parts, add."

**Trap.** Counting every square one by one and losing the count; or giving the whole array's count. The cover motion is not needed; counting by rows with the row size said aloud ("each row is still eight") is the whole method, and the gold letter keeps the eye on the region asked.

**Kit.** `array` with `unit: "square"`, `cut` at a row, region letters as labels.

**Writer.** The strongest writer, because this is the skill's only picture of the cut and the two beats that make it the distributive property must be staged, not described.

### `nested-bag-total` — Groups inside groups

**Standard.** 3.OA.B.5: "3 × 5 × 2 can be found by 3 × 5 = 15, then 15 × 2 = 30, or by 5 × 2 = 10, then 3 × 10 = 30. (Associative property of multiplication.)" Also 3.OA.A.3 (equal-groups word problems) and 3.OA.D.8 (two-step problems). The app's wording uses 3 crates of 3 bags, where the two threes blur which is which; the lesson's example uses three different numbers.

**Foundations first.** Foundation 1 in one line ("times means groups of"), then foundation 4's regrouping, built live. Four crates drawn; each opens to show 3 bags; each bag holds 5 balls. Two ways to count the same balls, and the picture must show both end in the same dots.

**Read.** "4 crates. Each crate has 3 bags, and each bag holds 5 balls. How many balls?" Gold on "balls": the thing asked. Four numbers now: crates, bags in each, balls in each, and the total of balls, which is missing. Total missing: multiply, and twice, because there are two "each"es. At a glance: more than the balls in one crate; four crates of about fifteen, about sixty.

**The example.** (1) Open one crate: 3 bags of 5; the balls line up as 3 rows of 5; count 5, 10, 15. One crate holds 15 balls. Write 3 × 5 = 15. (2) Four crates of 15: the four crates' rows stack; count 15, 30, 45, 60. Write 4 × 15 = 60. (3) The other way: all the bags slide out of the crates into one column: 4 × 3 = 12 bags. 12 rows of 5: count by rows to 60. Write 12 × 5 = 60. (4) Same balls, same array, counted two ways. Board: (4 × 3) × 5 = 4 × (3 × 5) = 60. Said once: "Group them either way. The balls don't change."

**Fast.** Multiply the pair that gives the fact you know best, then multiply by the third.

**Sense.** About four fifteens, about sixty; more than the 12 bags and more than the 15 in one crate.

**Check.** Count the other way; both groupings land on 60.

**Line.** "Group them either way. The balls don't change."

**Trap.** Stopping after one multiplication and answering 12 (bags) or 15 (one crate's balls); or adding 4 + 3 + 5. The gold "balls" and the picture: 12 is bags, with the balls still inside them.

**Kit.** `array` with `layout: "groups"`, `nested`, and `regroup`.

**Writer.** The strongest writer: the regrouping motion is the property, and the second count must visibly be the same dots.

### `operation-property-identify` — Which property, which equation

The type has no standard id. Give it 3.OA.B.5 (the three named multiplication properties) and 1.OA.B.3 for the addition names. The "identity property" is a common textbook name, not a Common Core clause; the lesson teaches it as what it is: times one or plus zero leaves the number itself.

**Standard.** 3.OA.B.5: "If 6 × 4 = 24 is known, then 4 × 6 = 24 is also known. (Commutative property of multiplication.) ... (Associative property of multiplication.) ... (Distributive property.)"

**Foundations first.** The whole lesson is here; the examples are reading. A *property* is something that is always true about how numbers can be moved. Four names, each defined as a motion on a small array, one beat each, with the equation written beside in the array's colors:
- *Commutative*: swap. 2 rows of 4 lifts and turns into 4 rows of 2; the two labels trade places. 2 × 4 = 4 × 2. Same with addition: 2 + 4 = 4 + 2, two piles swapped.
- *Associative*: regroup. 2 crates of 3 bags of 4 counted as (2 × 3) × 4 or 2 × (3 × 4): the numbers stay in order; only the parentheses, the grouping, move.
- *Distributive*: cut. 3 × (4 + 2) is a 3-row array cut after 4 columns: 3 × 4 and 3 × 2, added. A number times a sum becomes two products.
- *Identity*: itself. 6 × 1 is one row of 6; 6 + 0 is six with nothing arriving. The number is unchanged.
Then the one reading rule: look from the left side to the right side and name the one change: swap, regroup, cut, or itself. Then read the sign to say addition or multiplication. (Term cards: *commutative*, *associative*; the other two said in passing.)

**Read.** Two directions. Name to equation: find the equation showing that one change with that sign. Equation to name: name the change, then the sign. At a glance: no parentheses means not associative; no + inside a × means not distributive; no 1 or 0 means not identity.

**The example (name to equation).** "Which equation shows the associative property of multiplication?" Choices: 8 × (3 + 6) = 8 × 3 + 8 × 6 | (8 + 3) + 6 = 8 + (3 + 6) | (8 × 3) × 6 = 8 × (3 × 6) | 8 × 1 = 8. (1) Gold on "multiplication": the one with only plus signs dims. (2) Gold on "associative": regroup, parentheses moving, numbers in place. 8 × 1 has no parentheses; the first choice has a + inside and two products on the right, that is a cut. Both dim together. (3) (8 × 3) × 6 = 8 × (3 × 6): same numbers, same order, parentheses moved. Say it as the crates: eight crates of three bags of six, counted either way.

**Second example (equation to name), brief.** "Which property does 4 × 2 = 2 × 4 show?" The 4 and the 2 traded places: swap, commutative. The sign is ×: of multiplication. The decoy "commutative property of addition" is the right change with the wrong sign; the two identity names dim together (nothing is 1 or 0).

**Fast.** Name the change (swap, regroup, cut, itself), then read the sign.

**Sense.** A property is always true, so the equation must be true: 4 × 2 and 2 × 4 are both 8.

**Check.** Compute both sides.

**Rule out.** Wrong sign dims first; then every choice whose shape does not match the change named.

**Line.** "Swap, regroup, cut, or itself. Then read the sign."

**Trap.** Commutative and associative confused, because both "move things": in commutative the numbers change places; in associative the numbers stay put and the parentheses move. The turn and the regroup are two different motions, and the lesson shows them one after the other on the same numbers.

**Kit.** Nothing beyond `array` `turn`, `regroup`, `cut`, and `dim`.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model from this concept: the design's worry was that the names be mapped to motions, and that mapping is now written out; the lesson is short.

## Multiply tens (grade 3)

### `multiply-by-tens` — A one-digit number times a multiple of ten

The film seed, 10 × 7, is degenerate as a main example but perfect as the foundation beat below; the main example is 6 × 70.

**Standard.** 3.NBT.A.3: "Multiply one-digit whole numbers by multiples of 10 in the range 10–90 (e.g., 9 × 80, 5 × 60) using strategies based on place value and properties of operations."

**Foundations first.** Foundations 1 and 8, with family 2 named in one line ("ten squares make a rod"). Build it from the seed: 10 × 7 is ten rows of 7 dots; turn it: 7 rows of 10; each row of ten dots slides together and fuses into a rod: 7 rods, which the chart reads as 70. Times ten is one place left. Define *multiple of ten* (the name of a number made of whole rods: 10, 20, ..., 90).

**Read.** "What is 6 × 70?" Gold on the 0 of 70: one factor is rods, not squares. At a glance: the answer ends in 0, and it is the fact 6 × 7 with the zero riding along.

**The example.** 6 × 70. (1) The fact first: a 6 × 7 array of dots, counted by rows 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42. Write 6 × 7 = 42. (2) Tens up: every dot becomes a rod; the array is now 6 rows of 7 rods; the count label relabels 42 dots, 42 rods, 42 tens. (3) Into the chart: ten rods fuse into a flat, four times: 4 flats, 2 rods; the digit row reads 420. (4) Write 6 × 70 = 420 under 6 × 7 = 42: the same digits, one place left, a zero in the ones.

**Quick second case.** 5 × 60: 5 × 6 = 30; 30 rods; three flats: 300. Shown because the fact already ends in 0 and that is where children stop.

**Fast.** Multiply the two digits; put the zero back.

**Sense.** 6 × 70 is between 6 × 50 = 300 and 6 × 100 = 600.

**Check.** Count by seventies on hops: 70, 140, 210, 280, 350, 420; or 420 ÷ 6 = 70.

**Line.** "The zeros ride along."

**Trap.** 6 × 70 = 42 (the zero forgotten), and 5 × 60 = 30 ("thirty already has its zero"). The chart makes both impossible: 42 rods are not 42 squares, and 30 rods are three flats.

**Kit.** `array` `tensup` into a `blocks` chart; `numberline` `hops` for the check.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: one method, one named motion.

## Whole multiplication (grade 4)

### `mcas-true-product-equations` — Which stated products are correct

A design finding first: in both produced sets, every wrong equation shares its last digit with the true product and is off by exactly one hundred or one ten (604 × 4 is 2,416, stated as 2,516; 516 × 4 is 2,064, stated 2,164; 484 × 5 is 2,420, stated 2,410; 609 × 4 is 2,436, stated 2,336). So the last-digit test and a rounded estimate never rule anything out here; the decoys are built to pass them. The lesson still takes the one-second glance, because it costs nothing and orders the work, but it tells the truth: every equation must be computed, and the computing is three quick parts.

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.5: "Multiply a whole number of up to four digits by a one-digit whole number ... using strategies based on place value." The habit is 4.OA.A.3's "Assess the reasonableness of answers using mental computation and estimation."

**Foundations first.** Foundation 9 on blocks: 604 × 4 is four rows of 6 flats, 0 rods, 4 squares: 24 flats, 16 squares; 2,400 + 16 = 2,416. "Each place times the digit, then add." Two free facts: a product's last digit comes from the ones digits (4 × 4 = 16, so it ends in 6); its size comes from the hundreds (600 × 4 is about 2,400).

**Read.** "Choose the 3 equations whose product is correct." Gold on "3" and "correct". The product is written on the left here; which side does not matter. Each equation is judged on its own. At a glance: the ones digits all agree and the sizes all look right, so this set has no free throw-outs; compute all six.

**The example.** The six equations. For each, one board line: the split and the sum, then a verdict mark. (1) 604 × 4: 2,400 + 0 + 16 = 2,416. Not 2,516: false. The first is shown in blocks as the foundation above; the rest are lines. (2) 435 × 3: 1,200 + 90 + 15 = 1,305: true. (3) 516 × 4: 2,000 + 40 + 24 = 2,064. Not 2,164: false. (4) 587 × 2: 1,000 + 160 + 14 = 1,174: true. (5) 484 × 5: 2,000 + 400 + 20 = 2,420. Not 2,410: false. (6) 778 × 5: 3,500 + 350 + 40 = 3,890: true. Count the true ones: three, as asked. Say once what the false ones have in common: each is a hundred or a ten away, the kind of slip a lost carry makes, which is why a glance cannot catch them.

**Second example (the true-or-false pair format), brief.** "Decide whether each of the 3 equations is true or false." 609 × 4 = 2,400 + 36 = 2,436, so "2,336 = 609 × 4 is false" is the statement to choose; 676 × 3 = 1,800 + 210 + 18 = 2,028, true; 395 × 5 = 1,500 + 450 + 25 = 1,975, true. Each equation gets exactly one of its pair.

**Fast.** Hundreds times the digit, tens times the digit, ones times the digit, add; compare.

**Sense.** Each product is in the hundreds-times-digit range.

**Check.** Exactly the asked number chosen; for each chosen one, the last digit and the sum re-read.

**Rule out.** Honestly: none at a glance in this type. The glance is kept because in other sets it wins.

**Line.** "Decide each one on its own. Hundreds, tens, ones, then add."

**Trap.** Marking an equation true because its size and last digit look right: that is exactly what the decoys are. The lesson says it in so many words.

**Kit.** A verdict mark per choice on the problem card (true/false, in muted for false), and `dim`; otherwise the board.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model: the method and every number are stated here.

### `multiply-by-one-digit` — A number up to four digits times a one-digit number

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.5: "Multiply a whole number of up to four digits by a one-digit whole number ... using strategies based on place value and the properties of operations. Illustrate and explain the calculation by using equations, rectangular arrays, and/or area models."

**Foundations first.** Foundation 9 run place by place, with family 2's trade named in one line. Shown first on a case that fits the card: 23 × 4 as four rows of 2 rods, 3 squares beside a chart-headed stack. Ones: 4 × 3 = 12 squares; ten fuse into a rod and slide to the tens; the stack writes 2 and a small 1 above the tens. Tens: 4 × 2 = 8 rods, and the traded rod arrives: 9. 92. Said once as the handoff: "Multiply the place, then add what arrived." Then: "Too many to draw for bigger numbers. The numbers in their places are the picture now."

**Read.** "What is 754 × 9?" One factor has one digit; the total is missing: multiply. At a glance: between 700 × 9 = 6,300 and 800 × 9 = 7,200, so four digits starting with 6 or 7; it ends in 6, because 4 × 9 = 36.

**The example.** 754 × 9 in the chart-headed stack, a small blocks inset only at the moment that matters. (1) Ones: 9 × 4 = 36. Write 6, carry 3. (2) Tens: 9 × 5 = 45 rods; the inset shows 45 rods, then the 3 traded rods arriving: 48. Write 8, carry 4. Here the voice slows: "Multiply first. The three arrives after." (3) Hundreds: 9 × 7 = 63 flats, and 4 arrive: 67. Nothing is left to multiply, so write 67. (4) 6,786.

**Second example, a different case, brisker.** 7,339 × 6: four digits, and a column that comes out to a round ten. 9 × 6 = 54, write 4 carry 5; 3 × 6 = 18 + 5 = 23, write 3 carry 2; 3 × 6 = 18 + 2 = 20, write 0 carry 2; 7 × 6 = 42 + 2 = 44. 44,034. The 0 in the hundreds is written on the word; a column that makes twenty is 0 of that place and 2 of the next.

**Fast.** The stack, right to left: multiply, then add the carry.

**Sense.** The estimate: 6,786 sits between 6,300 and 7,200; 44,034 is about 7,000 × 6 = 42,000.

**Check.** The split written out, which is also the standard's "explain": 700 × 9 = 6,300, 50 × 9 = 450, 4 × 9 = 36, sum 6,786. Last digit 6.

**Line.** "Multiply, then add the carry. Never the other way."

**Trap.** Adding the carry before multiplying: (5 + 3) × 9 = 72 in the tens. The inset makes it impossible: nine rows of five rods are 45 rods on the table before the three traded rods arrive.

**Kit.** `stack` with `op: "×"`, `headers: true`, a `carry` sub-word in `column` beats, a tinted 0 for a round column; `blocks` `copy` and `trade` for the 23 × 4 foundation and the inset.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model once `stack ×` exists and the blocks-in-rows idiom has been set by `divide-by-one-digit`; the storyboard reviewed frame by frame, because the inset's timing is the lesson.

### `area-model-product` — The product from an area model split by place

Verify the app's figure first: if its four parts are drawn as equal boxes, the problem shows a sketch and the lesson a true rectangle, and the learner sees two pictures. The app's model should be to scale, or label itself a sketch. This concept draws to scale.

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.5: "multiply two two-digit numbers, using strategies based on place value ... Illustrate and explain the calculation by using equations, rectangular arrays, and/or area models."

**Foundations first.** Foundation 9, with the grade-3 cut named in one line ("cut it, count the parts, add; you did this with 8 × 7"). Define *area model*: a rectangle whose sides are the two factors, drawn so 40 of 46 really is 40 of 46, cut by place, with each part's count written inside. The ten-lines make the parts visible as blocks: a tens-by-tens part is flats, a tens-by-ones part is rods, a ones-by-ones part is squares. Define *partial product*: the count in one part.

**Read.** "The area model splits 46 × 56 by place value. What is 46 × 56?" Asked: the whole, all four parts together. At a glance: the big part, 40 × 50, is 20 flats, 2,000, and most of the answer; the whole is under 50 × 60 = 3,000; it ends in 6 because 6 × 6 = 36.

**The example.** 46 wide, 56 tall, cut at 40 | 6 and 50 | 6. (1) Read the sides: 46 is 40 and 6, 56 is 50 and 6. (2) Each part is its width times its height. 40 × 50: four tens by five tens is 20 flats, 2,000, written in. 40 × 6: 24 rods, 240. 6 × 50: 30 rods, 300. 6 × 6: 36 squares. (3) The parts fill the rectangle, so they add: a stack of the four, 2,000 + 240 + 300 + 36 = 2,576, in the parts' colors. (4) 46 × 56 = 2,576.

**Fast.** Four parts, biggest first, add.

**Sense.** 2,000 and change; under 3,000; ends in 6.

**Check.** Add the parts in strips instead: the top strip 2,000 + 240 = 2,240, the bottom 300 + 36 = 336, together 2,576.

**Line.** "Cut it, count the parts, add."

**Trap.** 40 × 50 = 200, the dropped zero ("20 already has a zero"): four tens by five tens is twenty flats, and a flat is a hundred. And leaving out one of the four parts: the stack has four rows because the rectangle has four parts, counted on screen.

**Kit.** `area` with `splits`, `lines: "tens"`, per-part `product` labels; the stack in the area model's colors.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model once `multiply-two-digit` (the pilot lesson where dots become the rectangle) exists; this is the same picture read from the problem's own figure.

### `tens-contribution` — The tens partial product

A narrow type: one part of a cut. Its value is the word *partial product* and the cut, which `area-model-product` and `multiply-two-digit` then use. Keep the lesson short.

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.5 (two-digit times one-digit by place value) and 3.NBT.A.3 ("Multiply one-digit whole numbers by multiples of 10": 6 × 20 is that).

**Foundations first.** Foundation 9 in its smallest form. 26 × 6 is a rectangle 26 wide, 6 tall, cut at 20 | 6. Define *partial product*: the count of one part. The tens part is 6 rows of 2 rods: 12 rods; the ones part is 6 rows of 6 squares.

**Read.** "To find 26 × 6, split 26 into 20 + 6. What is the tens partial product?" Gold on "tens": one part, not the whole. At a glance: a multiple of ten; the fact 6 × 2 with the zero riding along; the bigger of the two parts.

**The example.** (1) The rectangle with its cut and the ten-lines. (2) The tens part lights: 6 rows of 2 rods, 12 rods. Count by rows 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 rods: 12 tens: 120. Write 20 × 6 = 120 inside it. (3) 120 is the tens partial product. (4) One beat more, muted: the ones part is 6 × 6 = 36, and 120 + 36 = 156 is 26 × 6, which the question did not ask.

**Fast.** The tens digit times the factor, then the zero.

**Sense.** 120 is most of 26 × 6, which is a bit more than 150.

**Check.** 120 ÷ 6 = 20; or 120 + 36 = 156 = 26 × 6.

**Line.** "The zeros ride along."

**Trap.** Answering 156, the whole product, because the question mentioned 26 × 6; or 12 with the zero lost. The gold "tens partial product" and the count label reading 12 rods, 120.

**Kit.** `area` with `lines: "tens"`; nothing new.

**Writer.** A capable cheaper model; nearly a one-idea lesson.

### `multiply-two-digit` — Two two-digit numbers, no model given

Pilot lesson 3. On the goal's "three-digit factor": none of the produced wordings has one; every variant is two-digit by two-digit (45 × 75, 31 × 74, 34 × 54, 13 × 45, 92 × 32, 12 × 12). If a three-digit factor ever appears it is 5.NBT.B.5 and belongs in `multiply-multidigit`.

**Standard.** 4.NBT.B.5: "multiply two two-digit numbers, using strategies based on place value and the properties of operations. Illustrate and explain the calculation by using equations, rectangular arrays, and/or area models."

**Foundations first.** The handoff, said aloud as the point of the moment: 45 rows of 75 is an array too big to draw dot by dot, so the dots become a rectangle drawn to scale, with the count written in. Foundation 9 built live: cut both sides by place at the true fractions, 40 | 5 across and 70 | 5 down; the ten-lines make the parts flats, rods and squares; each part's count is a fact with the zeros riding along. Define *partial product* (one part's count) and *product* (all four). Then the second handoff: the same four numbers written under each other are a stack, and the stack can be squeezed to two rows, one per strip.

**Read.** "The farm plants 45 rows with 75 plants in each row. How many plants?" Three numbers: rows, each, total; the total is missing: multiply. Gold on "how many plants". At a glance: about 50 × 70 = 3,500, so between 3,000 and 4,000, four digits; ends in 5, because 5 × 5 = 25.

**The example.** 45 wide, 75 tall. (1) Cut and count the parts: 40 × 70 is four tens by seven tens, 28 flats, 2,800; 40 × 5 is 20 rods, 200; 5 × 70 is 35 rods, 350; 5 × 5 is 25 squares. (2) The stack of four in the parts' colors: 2,800 + 200 + 350 + 25 = 3,375. (3) Two rows instead of four: the bottom strip, 5 tall, is 200 + 25 = 225, which is 45 × 5; the tall strip, 70 tall, is 2,800 + 350 = 3,150, which is 45 × 70. A second stack: 45 × 75, ones row 225, tens row 3,150, sum 3,375. The tens row is the long part of the rectangle, and it starts in the tens place because it counts tens. (4) 3,375 plants.

**Second example, the thin-part case where its picture is the stack.** "Multiply 31 by 74." About 30 × 70 = 2,100, so four digits. Ones row: 4 × 31 = 124. Tens row: 70 × 31, so a tinted 0 in the ones place, written on the word, then 7 × 31 = 217 beside it: 2,170. Sum 2,294.
