# Learning mathematics with AMP

AMP is designed around a learner's growing ability to explain, represent, and use mathematics. Its curriculum graph connects skills from early counting through precalculus. A practice session should move among useful strands, return to older learning, and offer a manageable challenge. A sequence of correct answers to one familiar template is evidence about that task; it is not evidence of complete mathematical mastery.

This document specifies the learning experience and editorial requirements. It does not certify that every behavior or curriculum component has been implemented. Availability and review evidence belong in the content coverage manifest.

## A varied session with a purpose

An illustrative eight-question session might contain a fraction magnitude question, a geometry retrieval question, a fraction number-line question, a multiplication prerequisite check, a proportional reasoning challenge, a delayed fraction comparison, a data interpretation question, and a choice of two final challenges. The sequence responds to evidence; it is not a fixed quota or a random shuffle. Related ideas can appear together when comparison helps learning. A short teaching sequence may hold one topic for several steps, then return to the wider mix.

The learner should understand each transition. Useful labels include “A fresh challenge,” “An idea to bring back,” and “A quick check that may help.” Do not present a lower-grade prerequisite as a demotion. Show the mathematical purpose: “Equal groups will help us understand this fraction.” Grade labels help adults navigate materials; they should not define a child's identity or cap access.

## Placement is a question, mastery is a body of evidence

An initial probe estimates a starting point with a small number of informative tasks. A correct advanced response can justify a harder probe; an incorrect response should narrow uncertainty through a different representation or a nearby prerequisite. Placement must allow “I haven't learned this yet.” Guessing, reading difficulty, unfamiliar notation, and arithmetic slips can produce the same incorrect numeric response.

Store evidence at the task and assessed-facet level. Distinguish independent attempts from hinted, revealed, repeated, or coached attempts. Speed can inform a fluency objective after accuracy is established, but a slow correct explanation is not failure. Do not penalize accessibility accommodations or infer a diagnosis from response times.

A mastery claim needs successful independent work across materially different examples, representation changes, an appropriately delayed revisit, and transfer beyond the practiced wording. Explanation and construction facets require their own evidence; a numeric grader cannot establish them. Show adults the evidence and its limitations rather than an unexplained percentage. Suggested statuses are “exploring,” “practicing,” “ready to revisit,” and “demonstrated across checks,” with explicit criteria before the last label is used.

## Repair a prerequisite without creating a trap

When a learner struggles with adding fractions, first determine whether the obstacle is the unit, equivalent naming, whole-number arithmetic, or reading the situation. Offer one diagnostic contrast, a concise explanation, and a small repair task. Then retry the original idea with fresh values. A successful return is evidence that the detour helped.

Cap an uninterrupted repair sequence. After several unsuccessful attempts, offer a diagram, a different explanation, a book section, or a supported stop; also let the learner work in another strand. Endless easier questions can conceal the real obstacle and exhaust the learner. Allow a later probe to bypass a stale prerequisite estimate. The graph supplies useful hypotheses, not mandatory gates through every ancestor.

## Remembering, reasoning, and transfer

A revisit should sometimes change the surface and representation: a shaded strip becomes a number-line location; a calculation becomes an estimate; a word problem asks which operation applies before asking for the answer. Space revisits across sessions and widen intervals when independent retrieval is successful. Bring an idea back sooner after difficulty without treating one miss as erasure of all previous learning. Calendar intervals and promotion thresholds need evaluation against actual learners, not claims of scientific precision from arbitrary constants.

Challenge can mean explaining an impossibility, comparing two methods, repairing a convincing error, or solving with missing information. It need not mean larger numbers. Offer both accessible entry and extension: “Show it with a picture” and “Can you make a different example where this is true?” Reward a useful revision and a justified conjecture as part of learning, while keeping assessment credit tied to demonstrated outcomes.

## A learner's voice and age

Let learners choose between suitable strands, request a hint, switch representation, mark a question unclear, and explain an answer orally to an adult or in writing. A choice must affect the next experience rather than decorate a predetermined sequence. Learners can decline a challenge without losing access to other mathematics.

Use concise concrete language for young readers and optional read-aloud support. Older learners revisiting foundational ideas deserve mature contexts, respectful typography, and the same mathematical clarity. Adapt reading load and presentation separately from mathematical difficulty. Avoid babyish rewards for an older learner practicing multiplication and avoid dense prose merely because the mathematics is advanced.

## Books are teaching experiences

A book section needs a coherent idea, explanatory diagrams, original worked reasoning, guided practice, independent work, mixed retrieval, and detailed solutions. Each diagram must name the whole or unit and carry part of the explanation. Decorative pictures cannot substitute for a mathematical representation. Preserve legibility when printed in grayscale; use labels and patterns as well as color.

Build a progression from words to pictures to symbols, then ask the learner to move back among them. Reveal a rule after the learner has a reason to need it. A worked example should explain why a step preserves meaning, not merely name an operation. Follow it with a task that changes one important feature, then one that asks the learner to select a method independently. Leave enough physical room to draw, revise, and explain.

The Fraction Field Guide develops measurement, sharing, and scaling through a field-station context. Its opening fraction strips establish equal parts and the reference whole. A later number-line page asks why 5/4 lies beyond 1. Equivalence is shown by subdividing the same length before introducing multiplication of numerator and denominator. Addition preserves a common unit; multiplication changes a quantity by a scale factor; division asks either how many groups fit or how much belongs to each group.

One exemplar lesson places 2/3 and 3/4 on identical trails. The learner predicts which distance is longer, overlays twelfths, and explains why 8/12 and 9/12 are comparable. Its next task changes the trail lengths so “the larger fraction means the longer distance” is no longer safe. A division workshop shows 3/2 liters poured into 1/4-liter containers, then asks what changes if the same liquid is shared among four people. The first answer counts containers; the second measures liters per person. The pictures, units, and explanations distinguish the two questions before any reciprocal rule appears.

## Release evidence and honest limits

Every published task needs an exact solution, a check against the displayed givens, and review of ambiguous language and diagram geometry. Every book needs separate mathematical and instructional review, accessible reading order, and visual inspection of actual pages. Tasks must link to their solutions by stable identifiers. A publication should expose its revision and coverage so an adult can judge suitability.

The K–12 curriculum and SAT Math crosswalk are scope guides. Neither an inventory nor a subscription guarantees a particular score. Families need accurate information about available instruction, supported assessment modes, and where adult discussion or outside teaching is useful.
