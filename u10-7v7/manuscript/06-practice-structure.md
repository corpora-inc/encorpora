# Practice Structure

Every practice is sixty minutes. Not seventy. Not ninety. Sixty. This is deliberate. A focused sixty-minute practice where every second has a purpose produces more development than ninety minutes at half intensity. Respect the players' time and your own by keeping it tight.

Your team practices twice a week. Wednesday is Touches and Conditioning, covered in chapter 7. Thursday is Strategy and Brain Day, covered in chapter 8. Both practices follow the same structural framework described in this chapter. The content differs, but the skeleton stays the same. Consistency in structure lets the players focus on the content because they already know what comes next.

## The 60-Minute Framework

Every practice follows this timeline:

- **Minutes 0-10:** Arrival, warm-up, opening circle
- **Minutes 10-25:** Block 1 (primary drill or activity)
- **Minutes 25-27:** Water break
- **Minutes 27-42:** Block 2 (secondary drill or activity)
- **Minutes 42-44:** Water break
- **Minutes 44-57:** Block 3 (scrimmage or applied activity)
- **Minutes 57-60:** Closing circle

This framework gives you three working blocks of roughly fifteen minutes each, bookended by the opening and closing circles, with two short water breaks. The blocks are long enough to get meaningful repetitions in a drill and short enough that attention does not wander.

Print this timeline. Tape it to the inside of your coaching binder or clipboard. Before every practice, fill in the three blocks with specific activities. Do this before you arrive at the field. A coach who shows up without a plan wastes ten minutes figuring out what to do while fourteen kids run in circles. A coach who shows up with a plan starts the clock and runs.

## The First Ten Minutes

Players arrive at different times. Some are early. Some are right on time. Some are a few minutes late because their parents hit traffic. The first ten minutes absorb that variability while still being productive.

As players arrive, they pair up with whoever is available and start the standard warm-up. The warm-up is the same every practice: pairs passing at ten yards, starting with the inside of the foot, then the outside, then one-touch. This takes about five minutes and requires no instruction after the first practice because the routine is always the same. Arrival pairs do not need to be deliberate — whoever shows up at the same time works together. The real shuffling happens after the opening circle, when you count off into fresh pairs for Block 1.

While pairs are warming up, you observe. Watch footwork. Watch first touch. Note who looks sharp and who looks sluggish. Make mental notes for your coaching notebook. If a player's first touch is consistently heavy, that tells you something about their readiness and about what to focus on in their development.

At the ten-minute mark, or when the last expected player arrives — whichever comes first — blow the whistle and call the circle. Opening circle takes thirty seconds. What are we working on, why are we working on it, let us go.

### The Teaching Circle

Circles are not just for the start and end of practice. Before any drill block, bring the team into a brief teaching circle — thirty to sixty seconds — to explain what the drill is and why it matters. If you have a whiteboard with magnetic player dots, bring it. Show them the positions and the scenario. "Here is the situation: the right back just won the ball in our box. What does he do? Where does the wing go? What is the sweeper looking for?" Let the players answer. Then run the drill.

This connects every drill to a real game moment. A player who understands that the passing drill they are about to do simulates a build-out from the back will focus differently than a player who thinks they are just passing for the sake of passing. The teaching circle takes almost no time — less than a minute — but it transforms a drill from an exercise into a rehearsal. When the same situation happens on Saturday, the player has already thought about it.

If a player arrives late, they join in wherever the team currently is. They do not get a separate warm-up. They do not get a lecture about being late. If chronic lateness becomes a pattern, you address it with the family privately. During practice, late arrivals join and go.

## The No-Lines Rule

This is the single most important rule for practice design: **no activity should have more than three players waiting in a line at any time.** If you have fourteen players and a drill that involves one player going at a time while thirteen watch, you have wasted practice. Every player should be moving, touching the ball, or actively involved at all times.

This means every drill in this book is designed for small groups — pairs, trios, or groups of four to six — depending on what the drill demands. If you need to run a shooting drill, set up two or three stations so that players rotate through quickly. If you need to run a possession drill, split into multiple small grids rather than one large group. If you need to work on a set piece, have the players not involved in the set piece doing a parallel activity.

The math is simple. In a sixty-minute practice with ten minutes of warm-up, circles, and water breaks, you have fifty minutes of working time. If a player spends half of that time standing in a line, they got twenty-five minutes of development. If they spend all of it active, they got fifty minutes. Over a ten-week season with twenty practices, that is the difference between 500 minutes and 1,000 minutes of development. The player with 1,000 minutes improves faster. Every time.

When designing a drill, ask yourself: what is every player doing right now? If the answer for more than two players is "waiting," redesign the drill.

## Shuffling and Pairing for Drills

Chapter 2 covered the shuffle system and the social reasons behind it. Here are the logistical details.

Pairs are the ideal unit size for technical drills that focus on repetition — passing, receiving, 1v1 defending. By making pairs the default unit for these activities, you keep everyone active and the field organized. With six or seven pairs spread across the field, you can scan and see all fourteen players engaged at once. For tactical drills that teach positional relationships — how the back triangle works, how wings combine with the point — you need groups of three, four, or more.

Shuffle between blocks, not just at the start of practice. When you call water, the current pairs dissolve. When the players come back, count off new pairs for the next block. This takes ten seconds once the team is used to it, and it means every player works with two or three different partners in a single practice. In the early weeks, shuffle even more aggressively — reshuffle mid-block if a drill finishes early or if you want to change the dynamic.

For drills that require more than two players — 2v1, 3v2, or positional work — count off into larger groups. Twelve players, groups of three: count 1 through 4 three times. Twelve players, groups of four: count 1 through 3 four times. The count-off system scales to any group size. The players learn the pattern and the math handles itself.

Over the season, deliberately vary group sizes. Individual dribbling work where everyone has a ball, full field and back. Pairs for passing and 1v1. Trios for 2v1 and back-triangle positioning. Groups of four for 3v2 scenarios. Groups of six for half-team tactical work. Full 7v7 scrimmage. Each size teaches something the others cannot.

## Water Breaks

Two water breaks per practice. Each one is two minutes. Players jog to their water bottles, drink, and jog back. The jog matters. Walking to water and walking back is four minutes of dead time instead of two. At U10, two minutes is plenty for water if the players are not dawdling.

Water bottles should be on the sideline, not at a central station. Every player brings their own bottle and places it on the near sideline at the start of practice. This eliminates the cluster around a shared cooler and the inevitable "which one is mine" conversation.

Do not use water breaks as coaching moments. Water breaks are for water. If you need to give instructions, do it before the water break or after. Players do not retain information while they are drinking and catching their breath. Let the break be a break.

## The Closing Circle

The closing circle is three minutes. More than the opening circle because it involves interaction.

Stand in the circle. Pick one thing the team did well. Be specific. Not "good job today" but "our 6-0 in the scrimmage was excellent — everyone tracked back every time and we did not give up a single goal through the center." Specificity reinforces the behavior you want to see.

Then pick one thing to work on. Again, specific. "Our throw-ins in the attacking half need work. We keep throwing it backward instead of looking for the forward option. We will work on that Thursday."

Then ask a player to name something they learned or noticed. Rotate this responsibility. Do not always pick the most vocal kid. Pick the quiet ones. Give them a moment to think. If they struggle, prompt them: "What was the drill we did in block two? What were you trying to do in that drill?" This teaches reflection, which is a skill that develops slowly but pays off enormously.

End with the team break. Same word every time. Make it short and sharp. Hands in, word out, practice over.

## Equipment Checklist

Bring the following to every practice:

- A bag of cones (at least twenty). Flat disc cones are better than tall cones for marking grids because they do not interfere with the ball.
- At least four balls, preferably one per pair plus spares. Size 4 for U10.
- A set of pinnies (colored scrimmage vests) in two colors for distinguishing teams during scrimmages. If you have three colors, even better — you can do uneven sides or rotating groups.
- A whistle. You do not need to use it constantly, but it is the clearest way to start and stop activities when the field is noisy.
- Your coaching notebook and a pen.
- A watch or phone for tracking time. Set timers for each block so you do not have to constantly check.
- A small first-aid kit. Band-aids, antiseptic wipes, and an ice pack cover ninety percent of U10 injuries.

Do not bring a full goal if the field does not have one. Use cones to make small goals. Four cones can make two goals for a small-sided game. The ball going between two cones is a goal. This is sufficient for scrimmages and keeps setup simple.
