# The Formation

Soccer is a thinking game. Every second the ball is in play, every player on the field should be processing: where is the ball, where are my teammates, where are the opponents, and what is the best thing I can do right now for my team? The position gives you a home and a set of responsibilities. The context — what is actually happening on the field — tells you what to do with them. **Position plus context equals action.** That equation runs constantly, for every player, for the entire game.

Most U10 teams have not learned to think this way yet — and that is completely normal. Without a system, players default to standing in one spot or chasing the ball in a clump. The difference between standing in a spot and playing a position is thinking: processing what the team needs right now and acting on it. That is what this system teaches.

Every player on this team plays a position. Every player will have a home, a set of responsibilities, and the understanding that those responsibilities change second by second based on what is happening on the field. This chapter teaches you the formation system that makes that possible.

## Formation as a Framework for Thinking

Formation is a framework for thinking. Every player has a home — a default area and a set of responsibilities. The game tells them how to fulfill those responsibilities second by second. The left back's home is the defensive third, but if a runner is breaking through the center, the left back's job right now is to get to the center and stop that runner. The right wing's home is the attacking third, but if the team is defending, the right wing's job right now is to sprint back and help. A player who understands this is thinking. A player who stands in one spot waiting for the ball to come to them is not.

Playing a formation is seven brains processing the game in real time, making decisions, adjusting, communicating, and contributing to the team's success on both sides of the ball, every second. Formation creates the space where creativity happens — when five of your six field players are in position, the player with the ball has passing options, open lanes, and room to make decisions. The player who understands their position deeply — who knows when to hold, when to cheat toward the center, when to make a run, when to come back — is the most creative player on the field, because they are making real decisions based on real information.

## Six Positions, Not Zones

This system does not use generic zones like "left midfield" or "right midfield." It uses six distinct field positions, each with a specific name and a specific job. The positions are: left back, right back, sweeper, point, left wing, and right wing. Plus the goalkeeper, for seven total.

The sweeper and point are the spine of the formation. They occupy the center of the field, stacked vertically. The sweeper sits deeper — a defensive midfielder who anchors the middle, reads the game, distributes the ball, and organizes the defense. The point sits higher — an attacking midfielder who links the center to the front line, creates chances, and leads the press. These are the two most important positions on the field and they are not interchangeable with each other.

The left wing and right wing are the most versatile players on the field. On a 7v7 pitch, the field is small enough that every player can cover nearly the whole surface — and the wings do exactly that. In the 2-1-3 attacking shape, the wings are strikers: making runs, finding angles, and creating scoring opportunities. In the 6-0, they are wide defenders: tracking back to the corners, working with the backs to trap opponents against the sideline. In the neutral 2-1-1-2, they read the game from the attacking half and go wherever the opportunity is. The wings are completely context-dependent — their role changes with the shape, and they need to be ready for all of it.

The wings' full-field role is what makes this system work. A wing who tracks back into the 6-0, wins the ball wide, and explodes forward already has momentum while the opponent is still turning around. That is a breakaway — and it starts with defense. The counter-attack is at its most dangerous when the wing launches from deep — full sprint, forward momentum, the opponent still turning around. Getting back, winning the ball, and exploding forward is how wings produce goals at U10.

The left back and right back are the defensive foundation. Their primary job is to control the center of the field alongside the sweeper. They pinch in toward the middle, they clog passing lanes through the center, and they send the ball out toward the sidelines when they win it. The sidelines are the best defenders on the field — let the opponent chase the ball into the corners while your team reorganizes.

This distinction matters because it shapes how the players think about their role. A player told they are a "midfielder" thinks about the middle third of the field. A player told they are a "wing striker" thinks about getting forward and scoring. A player told they are the "sweeper" thinks about controlling the center and seeing everything. The language you use to describe positions shapes how players play them.

## Control the Center

This is the organizing principle of the entire system, on both sides of the ball. Think of it like chess: the player who controls the center of the board controls the game. The same is true on a soccer field.

On defense, the center is where the danger is. At U10, almost every goal comes through the middle of the field. A player who dribbles straight at the goal through the center is in the most dangerous position possible. A player who has the ball out wide near the sideline is in the least dangerous position. The sideline is a defender — it limits the attacker's options to one direction, makes it easy for your players to apply pressure, and makes it likely the ball goes out of bounds. Your defensive structure exists to deny the center and force the opponent wide. If they want to try to score from the corner, let them. They will not.

On offense, the center is where the goals are. The shortest distance between your goalkeeper and the opponent's goal is a straight line through the middle. If nobody is stopping the ball in the center, go straight up the middle and score. Do not pass it wide for the sake of passing it wide. Do not swing the ball to the wings when the center is open. A through ball from the sweeper to the point through the center of the defense is the highest-percentage play in U10 soccer. The wings exist to stretch the defense and create space in the center, and to receive the ball when the center is clogged — then swing it back through the center via the point or sweeper to find the opposite wing for a breakaway.

> This center-first philosophy simplifies everything. **On defense: stop the middle, send it out. On offense: attack the middle first, go wide only when the middle is blocked.**

## The Three Shapes

Defense comes first. No easy goals. Everyone plays defense. Being a wing does not mean only striker — sixty, sixty, sixty. That mindset is the foundation of this system, and the three shapes that follow are built on it.

This system uses three formations that the team shifts between based on the game situation. Think of them as shapes rather than rigid positions. The players are the same. Their responsibilities shift depending on whether the team is defending, in transition, or attacking. The beauty of this system is that it is really one formation with three modes. Players do not need to learn three completely different sets of positions. They need to learn their base position and then understand how it compresses or stretches depending on the situation.

## The Defensive Shape: 6-0

When the opponent has the ball in your half and is threatening your goal, the entire team defends. Not four players. Not five. All six field players collapse into the defensive third. This is the 6-0.

![The defensive shape: 6-0 with all six field players in or near the defensive third. LB and RB pinch into the center alongside the Sweeper, Point controls the middle, LW and RW track back to defend but stay alert for the counter.](images/formation-6-0-defensive.png)

**Every player on this team plays defense.** When the ball is in your end, all six field players are in the defensive third — organized, compact, and ready. Everyone gets back. Everyone gets goalside. Everyone defends.

The 6-0 does not mean six players standing on the goal line. It means six players packed into the defensive third, organized, and making it impossible for the opponent to come through the center. The backs pinch into the center alongside the sweeper — three players clogging the most dangerous zone on the field. If the opponent attacks in a 3v2, the backs can stack and switch sides to match up. The point sits in front of them, adding a fourth body in the center. The wings defend wide — they are the sideline trappers, working with the backs to push opponents out of the center and toward the touchline where their options shrink.

The principle is simple: **deny the center, force them wide.** If the opponent has to pass out to a player near the sideline, that player is in the least dangerous position on the field. The sideline limits their options. Your wing strikers can apply pressure against the touchline. And if the ball goes out of bounds, you get a throw-in and your team resets. Let the opponent play in the corners. They are not scoring from there. What you cannot allow is a run or a pass through the center of the defense. That is where goals happen.

The power of the 6-0 is density. At U10, an organized defense stops almost everything. When you pack six players into the defensive third, there are no gaps. The opponent has to play around the outside, take low-percentage shots, or make a mistake. A packed defense is rare at U10, and your organized team has a distinct advantage. When six players fill the defensive third, the ball stays with you.

But here is what makes the 6-0 different from a passive bunker: the wing strikers and the point are not just defending. They are coiled. They are playing defense with their heads up, watching for the moment possession changes. The instant your team wins the ball, those three players are ready to explode forward into a counter-attack. **The transition from 6-0 defense to a three-player counter-attack is the most dangerous weapon in this system.** The wings tracking back does not hurt your offense — it is your offense. The wing who is already goalside and sprinting forward the moment the ball turns over has momentum, has position, and has the defense scrambling to recover. That is where goals come from.

The trigger to drop into the 6-0 is simple: the opponent has the ball in your half and is moving forward. You do not need to wait for a specific event. As soon as the ball crosses midfield in the opponent's possession, the team starts compressing. The earlier the team recognizes the trigger, the more organized the 6-0 will be when the attack arrives.

The trigger to come out of the 6-0 is equally simple: your team wins the ball. The moment possession changes, the wing strikers and point burst forward. The sweeper looks to distribute. The backs push up to the midfield line. The team transitions from full defense to full attack in seconds.

## The Neutral Shape: 2-1-1-2

The neutral shape is where your team starts and where it returns after every reset. Think of it as two interlocking triangles. The back triangle has the left back and right back at its base and the sweeper at its apex. The front triangle has the sweeper at its base, and the left wing and right wing at the top corners with the point at the apex. The sweeper is the shared vertex — the hinge that connects defense to attack.

![The neutral shape: 2-1-1-2 formation with GK at the bottom, two backs (LB and RB) in the defensive third, the Sweeper in the center of the middle third, the Point in the upper middle third, and two wing strikers (LW and RW) in the attacking third.](images/formation-2-1-1-2-neutral.png)

Read it bottom to top: two backs, one sweeper, one point, two wing strikers. The 2-1-1-2. You can also see it as a 3-3 — three players in the back half of the field (LB, RB, Sweeper) and three in the front half (Point, LW, RW). The two-triangle framing is more useful for coaching because it shows the connections between positions, but the 3-3 framing helps players see the balance.

The key principle of the 2-1-1-2 is spacing. The back triangle should cover the defensive third with the sweeper sitting fifteen to twenty yards ahead of the two backs. The front triangle mirrors it — the point sits fifteen to twenty yards behind the two wing strikers, who spread across the attacking third and look for runs. The overall shape should stretch from goal to goal. The wing strikers are not glued to any lane — they are reading the field and going where the opportunity is. Sometimes that means stretching wide to pull defenders apart. Sometimes that means crashing the center for a rebound. Sometimes that means lurking between two defenders, waiting for a through ball. Good spacing means they are far enough apart to make the defense uncomfortable but free to exploit whatever gap opens up.

**Spacing is the single most important concept in this entire formation system.** If the spacing collapses, the formation collapses, and your players lose the framework that tells them what to do. Every coaching intervention you make during scrimmages should start with spacing and thinking. "Are you in your spot? Can you see both triangles? Good. Now look around — what does the team need from you right now? Make a decision."

The two-triangle structure also shows passing lanes. The sweeper can play to either back or forward to the point. The point can play to either wing striker or back to the sweeper. Every position has at least two natural passing options without anyone having to move. That built-in connectivity is what makes the 2-1-1-2 work — the ball always has somewhere to go.

## The Attacking Shape: 2-1-3

When your team has sustained possession in the opponent's half, the team stretches into the attacking shape. The backs hold their positions in the defensive third. The sweeper holds the center of the middle third as the safety valve. The point and both wing strikers push high, creating a front three in the attacking third.

![The attacking shape: 2-1-3 formation with GK at the bottom, two backs (LB and RB) holding the defensive third, the Sweeper in the center of the middle third, and three attackers (LW, Point, RW) spread across the attacking third.](images/formation-2-1-3-attacking.png)

The 2-1-3 is your team's posture when it smells a goal. The first option is always through the center. If the point can receive a through ball from the sweeper and go straight at goal, that is the play. The center is where goals live, and the shortest path to the goal is a straight line through the middle. At U10, if nobody is stopping the ball in the center, you go straight up the middle and score. Do not complicate it.

The wing strikers in the 2-1-3 are completely opportunistic. They have the full width and depth of the attacking third to work with, and they go wherever the probability of scoring is highest. If the center is open, they attack it. If the defense is bunched in the middle, they stretch wide and demand the ball in space. If the point shoots and it bounces off the goalkeeper, both wings should be trailing the play, crashing the goal for the rebound. If the ball is on one side and the defense shifts, the opposite wing reads the gap — which might be central, which might be wide — and makes a run into it. They can lure a defender into the corner and cross it back. They can receive a through ball at the top of the box and go straight at goal. They can pull wide to create a two-on-one down the flank. The wings think about probabilities and angles, not about staying in a lane.

The wings naturally create width by being two separate players on different sides of the field. When the whole defense collapses to the right because the ball is on the right, the left wing is open on the other side — not because they are hugging the touchline but because they read the space. The ball swings back through the sweeper or the point and finds the left wing running into the gap. Breakaway with the keeper.

The sweeper is the switchboard. If the attack stalls on one side, the ball comes back to the sweeper, who can see the whole field and redirect it through the center or swing it to the opposite wing. The sweeper's discipline in the 2-1-3 is what keeps the attack patient, composed, and central.

The two backs stay home. This is the rule that makes the attacking shape safe to use. If both backs push into the midfield, a single clearance by the opponent becomes a breakaway against your goalkeeper. The backs' discipline in the attacking shape is the insurance policy. They protect the space behind the sweeper and are the first line of defense if the ball turns over.

The trigger to move into the attacking shape is sustained possession in the opponent's half. If your team has strung together several passes and the ball is firmly in the attacking third, the wing strikers push high and look for runs, the point positions for a shot or a final ball, and the sweeper reads the play to either support or sit as the safety valve.

## Transitions

Transitions — the moments when possession changes and the team shifts from one shape to another — are where games are won at U10. Most goals at this age come from turnovers in the midfield, not from set pieces or sustained build-up play. The team that transitions faster controls the game.

There are two transitions your team needs to master: defensive transition (losing the ball and shifting to the 6-0) and attacking transition (winning the ball and launching the counter).

Defensive transition is more important and should get more practice time. When your team loses the ball, two things need to happen simultaneously. First, the nearest player to the ball pressures immediately. This is the first defender, and it does not matter what position that player plays. If the point loses the ball and the left wing is closest, the left wing pressures. Second, everyone else sprints goalside. Everyone. The wing strikers, the point, the sweeper, the backs — all of them get between the ball and their own goal and compress into the 6-0.

The speed of defensive transition determines how many goals you concede. If your team takes three seconds to recognize the turnover and start dropping, the opponent has three seconds of free space to exploit. If your team transitions in one second, the opponent has nowhere to go. Drill this constantly. In every scrimmage, stop play after a turnover and check: did the team transition? Did the nearest player press? Did everyone else get goalside? If not, reset and do it again.

Attacking transition is about recognizing opportunity — and this is where the 6-0 becomes a weapon. When your team wins the ball in your own defensive third, the wing strikers and point already have forward momentum because they were defending with their heads up, watching for the turnover. The moment possession changes, they release. The back who won the ball sends it out of the defensive third — to the sideline, to the sweeper, wherever it is safe. The sweeper looks through the center first for the point making a run. A through ball to the point accelerating through the center is the most dangerous play in U10 soccer. If the center is covered, the sweeper plays it to a wing striker who is already sprinting forward, looking for space, making a run at goal.

This counter-attack out of the 6-0 is the signature play of this system. The team defends with total commitment, wins the ball, sends it out of the danger zone, and the attackers explode forward before the opponent can recover. Practice it until it is instinctive. "When we win the ball, what do we do? Send it out. Where does the sweeper look? Through the center first. Where do the wings go? Forward. Into space. At the goal."

## Context-Driven Shifting

The three shapes are responses to what is happening on the field. The goal is for your players to read the game and shift on their own. But they also have a code — a shorthand you can call from the sideline to get everyone's mind right.

**"Sixty."** The 6-0. Six on defense. When you call sixty, every player knows: get back, get goalside, defend, pressure. One word and the whole team shifts. The wings sprint back to their wide defensive positions. The backs pinch to the center. Six players lock into the defensive shape in seconds. One word resets the entire team's posture. You will use this one the most.

**"Twenty-one twelve."** The 2-1-1-2. The neutral shape. This one has a nice symmetry to it — say it out loud and it sounds right. You will rarely need to call it during a game because it is the default. But it has value as a reset: after a chaotic sequence, after a goal kick, after any moment where the team has lost its shape, "twenty-one twelve" tells everyone to find their home and rebuild the two triangles. It is calming. Nothing special, just run the twenty-one twelve, everything is under control. It is also something the players can visualize on their own — in the car, before bed, whenever they are thinking about how the team plays. The symmetry of the number helps them see the symmetry of the shape.

**"Two thirteen."** The 2-1-3. The attacking shape. When you call two-thirteen, the team knows: push up, put it on them, keep it on their side. The backs move to the midfield line and send everything back in. The sweeper owns the central angles. The wing strikers and point get after every ball in the attacking third. You call two-thirteen in two situations. First, when you are dominating and want to put the game away — keep the pressure on, do not let them breathe. Second, when you are down a goal and need to get serious about scoring — push everyone forward and go get one.

The code is your team's secret language. The other team does not know what sixty means, or twenty-one twelve, or two-thirteen. Your team does. It is a small thing, but small things that make a team feel like a team matter.

In the early weeks, you will call the code constantly — that is installation. By midseason, you should be calling it less. The players should be reading the triggers on their own — opponent possession in our half means sixty, our sustained possession in their half means two-thirteen, and twenty-one twelve is always the home base — and shifting without your voice. When the sweeper starts calling "sixty" before you do, the system has taken root.

To get there, use the freeze scrimmage technique described in chapter 8. Stop play mid-action, ask the players what shape they should be in, ask them why, and then let them adjust before resuming. Over weeks, this builds the game intelligence that lets them shift without prompting.

At U10, the shifts will never be perfect. You will have moments where a wing striker forgets to track back in the 6-0, or a back forgets to hold during the attacking shape. That is normal. The goal is not a perfect diagram on the field. The goal is that every player is thinking — processing the situation, adjusting, trying to do the best thing for the team right now. If you pause the game and see seven players who are all engaged, all in roughly the right areas, all aware of what is happening — that is the system working. If you see a player standing still and watching while the game happens around them, that is where you coach.

## The Build-Out Line

Some leagues use a build-out line, a horizontal line at roughly the top of the penalty area that affects how play restarts after a goal kick or goalkeeper possession. If your league uses a build-out line, the opposing team must retreat behind it when your goalkeeper has the ball. This gives your team time and space to play out of the back.

If your league has a build-out line, teach your team to use it. When the goalkeeper has the ball, the backs should position near the sidelines and the sweeper should drop into the center as an option. The goalkeeper distributes to a back near the sideline — not through the center. Even on a build-out line restart, the sideline is the safest first pass. The back receives, and now the team builds through the sweeper and into the attacking shape. The opponent cannot press until the ball crosses the build-out line or is played forward, so there is no rush. Take the free space.

If your league does not have a build-out line, goal kicks and goalkeeper distribution need to be more direct. The goalkeeper still has options — a short pass to a back or a longer kick to the midfield — but the opponent can press immediately. In this case, the backs need to be prepared to receive under pressure, and the wing strikers need to be prepared for a long ball. Chapter 5 covers the specific set-piece instructions for goal kicks in both scenarios.

Whether your league uses a build-out line or not, the formation system works the same way. The build-out line affects how possession starts, not how the formation operates. Once the ball is in play, the three shapes apply regardless of whether a build-out line exists.
