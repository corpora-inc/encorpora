# Wednesday: Touches and Conditioning

Wednesday is about the ball. Every drill on Wednesday involves the ball, and the fitness component is baked into the drills rather than isolated as running. Every minute on Wednesday has the ball at the center. Players build fitness through soccer — dribbling, passing, dueling, competing — and the conditioning happens inside the work.

The philosophy behind Wednesday is simple: **the more times a player touches the ball in a controlled setting, the better they get.** A player who gets 200 touches in a practice improves faster than a player who gets 50. Wednesday is designed to maximize touches through structured drills — pairs passing, individual dribbling circuits, 1v1 duels, 2v1 combinations, and competitive small-sided games that also build the fitness base your team needs for game day.

## Pairs Passing Progressions

This is your Block 1 staple on Wednesdays. Pairs start ten yards apart. The progression moves through five levels, spending about two to three minutes at each level. You call out the next level when the current one is clean.

**Level 1: Two-touch inside of the foot.** The ball arrives, the player takes a touch to control it, then passes back with the inside of the foot. Focus: clean first touch that sets the ball to the side, not under the feet. The pass should be firm enough to reach the partner without them having to chase it.

**Level 2: Two-touch, alternating feet.** Same as level 1, but the player must use a different foot for the control touch and the passing touch. If they control with the right, they pass with the left. Focus: forcing players to use their weak foot in a controlled setting. This is uncomfortable and that is the point.

**Level 3: One-touch.** No control touch. The ball arrives and the player passes it back immediately with the inside of the foot. Focus: body position. To play one-touch, the player must be set before the ball arrives — feet apart, body open, weight on the toes. If they are flat-footed, the one-touch pass goes sideways.

**Level 4: One-touch, moving.** Same as level 3, but both players are moving laterally. They slide along a line, maintaining ten yards of distance while passing one-touch. Focus: passing while moving, which is what happens in a game. A player who can only pass while standing still is not game-ready.

**Level 5: Long-short combination.** One player plays a long pass (fifteen to twenty yards), the other plays it back one-touch, then the first player plays a short pass (five yards), and the partner plays it back. The pattern is long-short-long-short. Focus: varying weight of pass. The long ball needs pace. The short ball needs touch.

If you get through all five levels in fifteen minutes, your players are technically sharp that day. If they are struggling at level 3, stay at level 3. The progression adjusts to the team's readiness. Do not push to level 5 just because the plan says so. Stay at the level where the players are challenged but not failing on every rep.

## Dribbling Circuits

Dribbling circuits replace traditional cone-weaving drills with something more game-realistic. Set up a grid roughly twenty by twenty yards with cones scattered inside it at random intervals — eight to ten cones is enough. Every player has a ball. On the whistle, they dribble through the grid, navigating the cones and each other.

**Round 1: Free dribble.** Players dribble through the grid using any part of the foot, keeping the ball close. The only rule is they must keep moving and avoid hitting cones or other players. Focus: head up. Players who keep their head up navigate cleanly and find open space every time.

**Round 2: Right foot only.** Players may only touch the ball with their right foot. Focus: comfort with the dominant foot for some, discomfort with the non-dominant foot for others. Either way, they are getting reps with a specific foot.

**Round 3: Left foot only.** Same as round 2, mirrored. This round builds the weak foot faster than any other drill, and that growth is exactly why it exists.

**Round 4: Moves on command.** Players dribble through the grid, and when you call a move, they execute it on the nearest cone. Moves: pull-back (sole of the foot on top of the ball, pull it back, change direction), scissors (fake one way with a step over the ball, go the other way), inside-outside cut (push the ball with the inside of the foot, then quickly with the outside to change direction). You do not need more than three moves. Master three moves and a U10 player can beat most defenders.

**Round 5: Sharks.** Remove the ball from two players. They become sharks. The rest are dribblers. Sharks try to kick the dribblers' balls out of the grid. If your ball goes out, you become a shark. Last dribbler standing wins. Focus: protecting the ball, using the body as a shield, changing direction under pressure. This is also the conditioning round — players who are sharks sprint constantly.

The dribbling circuit takes about fifteen minutes if you spend two to three minutes per round. It is high-touch, high-intensity, and requires zero lines.

## 1v1 Duels

One-versus-one is the most fundamental situation in soccer. Every open-play moment eventually comes down to one player trying to get past another. The player who wins the 1v1 creates the advantage. Every duel is a chance to tip the game. Your players need to practice both sides — attacking and defending — in a structured setting.

Set up a ten-by-fifteen-yard channel with a small cone goal at each end. One player starts with the ball at one end, the other defends from the middle. The attacker tries to score. The defender tries to win the ball and score at the opposite goal. After each rep, they switch roles.

**Attacking principles:** The attacker should dribble at the defender with pace, not slow down and wait. They should use a move — pull-back, scissors, or cut — to change direction and get past the defender. If they beat the defender, they shoot. If the defender holds, the attacker shields the ball and resets for the next move. At U10, the best attacking habit is keeping the ball moving when a defender closes down. Coach them to maintain pace and commit.

**Defending principles:** The defender should close down the space quickly but slow down as they get within two yards of the attacker. This is called the "approach and slow" technique. Sprint to close, then shuffle to contain. The defender stays on their feet — no lunging, no diving in. They angle the attacker toward the sideline of the channel, taking away the center. If the attacker makes a move, the defender reads it and reacts. If the attacker tries to go past, the defender uses their body to stay goal-side and pokes the ball away when the opportunity comes.

Run this for ten minutes, rotating the pairs through so every player gets multiple reps as attacker and defender. This drill is physically demanding because the intensity is constant — there is no rest within a rep and only a few seconds between reps.

## 2v1 Scenarios

The 2v1 builds on the 1v1 by adding a passing element. Two attackers face one defender in a channel with a goal at one end. The attackers try to score. The defender tries to win the ball or force it out of bounds.

**Attacking principles:** The player with the ball drives at the defender. The player without the ball moves to an open position — usually wide and slightly ahead of the ball. When the defender commits to the ball carrier, the carrier passes to their partner who finishes. The timing of the pass is everything. Too early and the defender can adjust. Too late and the defender is on top of the ball carrier. The ideal moment is when the defender's weight shifts toward the ball carrier and they cannot recover to the passing lane.

**Defending principles:** The lone defender cannot win this situation through pure athleticism. They need to buy time and force a mistake. The defender should approach the ball carrier at an angle that takes away the pass while still containing the dribble. If they can delay the attack long enough, the advantage shifts. In a game, buying time means teammates can recover. In this drill, buying time means forcing the attackers to make multiple passes, which increases the chance of an error.

Run this drill for ten minutes. Rotate so every player gets reps in all three roles: ball carrier, support runner, and defender. The conditioning benefit is significant because the defender is working at maximum intensity to cover two attackers.

## Scenario Drills

Scenario drills simulate real game moments. Each one starts with a specific situation the team will face on Saturday, explains why the drill matters, and builds natural conditioning into the repetitions. These drills develop the relationships between positions — the back and the wing, the sweeper and the point — so that the connections become instinctive.

### Backline Clear to Counter

**The situation:** The right back wins the ball in your box during a 6-0 defensive sequence. The right wing has tracked back and is now ready to release.

**Why it matters:** This is the most common transition in the system — winning the ball in the defensive third and launching a counter-attack. Developing the relationship between the back and the wing is what makes this transition fast and dangerous.

**Setup:** Right back starts with the ball in the box. Right wing starts in the defensive third, simulating the track-back position. A goalkeeper is in goal at the opposite end. On the whistle, the right back clears the ball out to the sideline. The right wing collects it and explodes forward for a breakaway against the goalkeeper. After five reps, switch to the left side — left back clears to left wing. Rotate players through all three roles.

### Sweeper Distribution

**The situation:** Your goalkeeper has the ball after a save. The team needs to build out of the back and transition into the attacking shape.

**Why it matters:** This is the full build-out sequence — the chain of passes that turns a goalkeeper save into an attacking opportunity. Every position in the chain needs to know where to be and when to move.

**Setup:** Goalkeeper starts with the ball. A back positions near the sideline. The sweeper drops into the center as the secondary option. The point positions in the upper middle third. The opposite wing is high and wide. The goalkeeper throws to the back on the sideline. The back plays it inside to the sweeper. The sweeper looks through the center — if the point is open, through ball. If not, the sweeper swings it to the opposite wing for a breakaway. Run the full sequence five times, then rotate positions so every player experiences each role in the chain.

### Wing Recovery and Counter

**The situation:** The wing has tracked back into the 6-0. The opponent has the ball near the sideline.

**Why it matters:** This drill teaches the core principle that defense IS offense. The wing who tracks back and wins the ball is the wing who gets the breakaway. This is not a sacrifice — it is the setup for the most dangerous attacking play in the system.

**Setup:** The wing starts in the defensive third, simulating the track-back. A coach or player feeds the ball to a "defender" near the sideline, simulating an opponent in possession. The wing pressures the opponent against the sideline, wins the ball, and explodes forward for a breakaway against the goalkeeper. Run five reps per side, rotating the wing and the defender. The conditioning is built in — the wing sprints back, pressures, wins the ball, and sprints forward, simulating the full defensive-to-attacking transition.

### Point Drops Deep, Explodes Forward

**The situation:** The team is in the 6-0. The point has tracked all the way back to the goal line. The team wins the ball.

**Why it matters:** This drill reinforces that the point comes ALL the way back on defense and then covers the full length of the field on the counter. It builds the fitness required to do this repeatedly in a game and develops the one-touch combination play that makes the counter-attack lethal.

**Setup:** The point starts on the goal line. The sweeper has the ball at the edge of the box. The point receives from the sweeper, lays it off one-touch, and sprints forward into the center channel. The sweeper plays a through ball ahead of the point. The point finishes against the goalkeeper at the opposite end. Full-field conditioning built into every rep. Run five reps, then rotate the point and sweeper. This is the most physically demanding drill in the book — if the point can do this five times in practice, they can do it in a game.

## Conditioning Through Drills

Every drill in this chapter involves the ball. That is intentional. Conditioning at U10 should come from soccer activities, not from track-and-field exercises. Here is why.

First, the fitness demands of soccer are intermittent, not steady-state. A player sprints for five seconds, jogs for fifteen seconds, sprints again, walks for ten seconds, sprints again. Running laps develops steady-state endurance, while soccer demands intermittent sprint-jog-sprint fitness. Small-sided games and intense drills develop the sprint-jog-sprint pattern that matches actual game demands.

Second, conditioning with the ball develops two things at once — fitness and technical skill. A player doing 1v1 duels for ten minutes is getting both. A player doing 1v1 duels for ten minutes develops fitness at match intensity while sharpening their technique with every touch.

Third, kids love competitive drills. A player engaged in a game of sharks sprints harder than they would on any lap and enjoys every second of it. Engagement produces effort. Effort produces fitness.

When you want your team even fitter — fresher in the final ten minutes, sharper in the second half — increase the intensity of your drills. Shorter rest between reps. More rounds of 1v1. Longer scrimmages with fewer substitutions. The fitness comes from the work, and the work has the ball.

## Optional Scrimmage

If you have time remaining after the three drill blocks — sometimes things move faster than planned — use the remaining time for a scrimmage. Split the team into two groups. If you have fourteen players, play seven versus seven on the full practice field. If you have twelve, play six versus six on a smaller grid.

The Wednesday scrimmage is a free scrimmage. Minimal coaching intervention. Let them play. The purpose is to let players apply what they worked on in the drills in a game-like setting without the pressure of stopping and correcting every mistake. If you see a beautiful 2v1 combination that came straight from the drill, acknowledge it. If you see someone pull off a move they practiced in the dribbling circuit, acknowledge it. But do not freeze play or give tactical instruction. That is Thursday's job.

## Sample Wednesday Practice Plan

- **0-10:** Arrival. Pairs warm-up (standard passing routine). Opening circle: "Today we are working on our touch and our conditioning. Everything involves the ball."
- **10-25:** Block 1 — Pairs passing progression. Start at level 1. Push through as many levels as the group can handle cleanly.
- **25-27:** Water break.
- **27-42:** Block 2 — Dribbling circuit. All five rounds. Finish with sharks.
- **42-44:** Water break.
- **44-57:** Block 3 — 1v1 duels (4 min), 2v1 scenarios in trios (4 min), scenario drill of the week (5 min). Rotate groups between stations.
- **57-60:** Closing circle. One thing that went well, one thing to work on. Player reflection. Team break.
