# Building the Unit

A soccer team is not a collection of individuals who happen to wear the same color jersey. It is a unit. The players need to trust each other, communicate without thinking about it, and hold each other accountable. None of that happens by accident. It is built deliberately, starting at the first practice and reinforced every single session.

**The culture set in the first two weeks is the foundation for the entire season.** When every player knows every other player, when the team has a shared identity, and when families are part of the mission, everything else in the book falls into place. Start strong and the season builds momentum on its own.

## Circle Up

Every practice starts and ends in a circle. Everyone can see everyone else's face. You speak clearly and the players listen — twelve sets of eyes locked in, ready to go. That eye contact carries onto the field. Players who are used to looking their teammates in the eye during circle communicate better during the game.

The opening circle is where you set the agenda. Keep it short. Thirty seconds. Tell them what you are working on today and why. "Today we are working on defensive shape because we gave up three goals on the counter last game. By the end of practice you will know exactly where to be when we lose the ball." That is it. No speeches. No pep talks. Just the what and the why.

The closing circle is where you reinforce what happened. Pick one thing that went well and one thing to work on. Ask a player to name something they learned. Rotate who you ask so everyone gets a turn over the course of a few weeks. End with a team break. Pick a word. Keep it the same all season. Make it theirs.

## Pair Up and Shuffle

Pairs are the foundation of your practice structure, but they are not the only grouping. Over the season, players work in every combination: individually, in pairs, trios, groups of four, groups of six, and full 7v7. Pairs are where it starts because they maximize touches and build every one-on-one relationship on the team. But pairs are not fixed for the whole practice. You shuffle them — sometimes multiple times per session. The goal is that every player works with every other player frequently, and that the team gets fast at reorganizing itself.

Shuffling does three things. First, it builds team-wide connections. Constant reshuffling ensures every player works with every other player, and the whole team stays connected. Second, it builds communication and adaptability. Different partners require different adjustments. A player who adapts to any partner has a complete toolkit. A player who can adapt to anyone is more valuable. Third, the act of shuffling itself is a small exercise in organization and even mathematics.

There are several ways to shuffle. The simplest: stand in a circle and count off. If you have twelve players and need pairs, count 1 through 6 twice. The ones pair up, the twos pair up, and so on. Six pairs, no negotiation, ten seconds. If the next drill is 2v1 and you need groups of three, count 1 through 4 three times. Four groups of three. The kids pick up the counting pattern fast, and after a few weeks they will do it without you leading it.

You can also let the players shuffle themselves. "Find someone you have not paired with yet today." In the early weeks this takes a minute and involves some confusion. By week three it takes fifteen seconds. If a player is standing alone or gravitating toward their best friend again, redirect them. "You two were together last drill. Find someone new." The players learn to scan the group and solve the problem themselves. That is a social skill and a logistical skill rolled into one.

For coaches who want to sneak in a bit of thinking: ask the team a question before shuffling. "We have twelve players. How many pairs is that? Good — six. Now count off, one through six." Or before a 2v1 drill: "Twelve players, groups of three. How many groups?" Let them work it out. It takes five seconds and it connects the shuffling to basic division. You are not teaching a math class. You are making them think about group structure, which is what formation is. And the numbers are genuinely interesting. With 12 players, there are 66 possible pairs (12 choose 2). Each shuffle puts 6 pairs on the field at once, and with two or three shuffles per practice you cover a big chunk of those 66 every session. Within the first few weeks, every player has worked with every other player. With trios it is 220 combinations — the space grows fast, so shuffling stays fresh all season. You do not need to dwell on it, but dropping these numbers on the kids once or twice plants a seed: the team is bigger than any one relationship.

Shuffle at least twice per practice — once at the start, once between blocks. In the early weeks of the season, shuffle more often so that every player gets exposure to every other player quickly. As the season progresses and the team knows each other well, you can be more targeted: pair a strong player with one who needs a push, or group players by position for tactical work. The key is that every pairing builds a new connection, and every player keeps growing.

As the season progresses, the grouping sizes grow with the team's sophistication. Early weeks are heavy on pairs — building the individual relationships. By mid-season you are running trios (2v1, positional triangles) and groups of four (3v2 plus goalkeeper, half-field shape work). By peak weeks, 6v6 scrimmages and full 7v7 are the norm. The progression mirrors how the team learns: one relationship at a time, then small groups, then the whole system.

## Names

**Every player learns every other player's name by the end of the first week.** This is non-negotiable. You cannot communicate on a soccer field with "hey" and "over here." You need names. Players calling each other by name is the most basic form of soccer communication and it starts in practice.

If you have a large roster, use a name game during the first practice. Stand in a circle. First player says their name. Second player says the first player's name and their own. Third player says the first two names and their own. Keep going around the circle. By the end, the last player has to say everyone's name. It takes five minutes and it works.

After that, reinforce names constantly. When a player makes a good pass in a drill, do not just say "good pass." Say "good pass, Marcus" loud enough for the team to hear. When giving instructions, use names. "Sofia, you are left back. Jayden, you are on the right wing." Within two weeks, every player should know every name without thinking about it. If they do not, run the name game again.

## Trust and Accountability

Trust on a soccer team means two things. First, trusting that your teammate will be where they are supposed to be. If the left back knows the right back and sweeper have the center locked down, she can go challenge the wide attacker without worrying about the middle. That kind of trust comes from repetition in practice. When players run the formation fifty times, they stop guessing and start knowing.

Second, trust means believing that the work matters. Kids buy in when they understand why. Conditioning makes us fresher in the final ten minutes of a game when the other team is tired. Drills compress more quality touches into an hour than scrimmaging does. Understanding the system makes everyone better because each player knows what their teammates are going to do before they do it. When players understand the reasons behind the work, they want to do it. That is the goal — inspire them to want to work through charisma and explanation. A coach who can make a nine-year-old excited about defensive shape is a coach whose team will play real soccer.

Accountability runs in every direction. You hold players accountable for effort and execution. Players hold each other accountable by communicating. And you hold yourself accountable by being prepared, being on time, and following through on what you say. If you tell the team you are working on corners Thursday, you work on corners Thursday. If you tell a player they will get time at a new position, they get time at that position. **Following through is what builds trust. When you say something, do it.**

## Everyone Connects

The goal is a team where every player has a relationship with every other player. At this age, kids naturally gravitate toward their friends. Your job is to expand those circles so that by mid-season, every player on the roster is a friend. That is what the constant shuffling builds — not just passing skills, but a team where nobody is on the outside.

The constant shuffling is how you build these connections. You can also nudge the social dynamics in positive directions. During water breaks and before practice, look for chances to mix groups. Walk over and connect players who have not worked together yet. "Marcus, go work with Jayden on those juggles. Sofia, show Ava that move you were doing yesterday." Small moments like these build friendships across the whole team.

During scrimmages, mix up the teams. Do not let the same group always play together. Vary the teams every scrimmage so that by the end of a month, every player has been on the same scrimmage team as every other player multiple times.

If you notice a player who has not connected with the group yet, you can help. A quiet word after practice goes a long way. "Hey, can you pair up with Ava for the first drill tomorrow? I think you two would work well together." Nine-year-olds respond to being given a role. When you frame inclusion as a responsibility they are trusted with, they take it seriously.

## The Coach's Notebook

Carry a small notebook to every practice and every game. This is not optional. Your memory is not as good as you think it is, especially when you are managing fourteen kids, tracking substitutions, and adjusting tactics in real time.

In the notebook, track the following things. First, brief notes on each player after each practice — not a novel, just one line. "Jayden: developing left foot passing, needs reps." "Sofia: excellent positioning in scrimmage, ready for more responsibility." Third, game notes. What worked, what to build on at the next practice. Fourth, minutes played per player per game. This matters for fairness and for keeping families informed.

The notebook is also your planning tool. Before each practice, write down the plan. What drills, in what order, for how long. You will not stick to the plan perfectly. A plan means every minute of practice has a purpose.

## Parent Communication

At the beginning of the season, introduce yourself to all families and share the basics: practice schedule, game schedule, your coaching philosophy in two sentences, and what the team is building together. Share the code words the team uses during games (chapter 3) so that families who want to cheer can cheer in the same language. When everyone is on the same page, the players hear one clear signal and play with confidence.

## Week One

Your first two practices establish the foundation. By the end of the first week, every player should know every other player's name, understand the basic formation, and have a feel for the vocabulary the team uses.

### First Practice

The first practice is about introductions — to the team, to the system, and to each other.

Start with joint mobility and basic running warm-ups to get everyone loose. Then circle up. Introduce yourself. Run the name game. Tell them what kind of team you are building. Keep it concrete. "We are going to be the most organized team in this league. We are going to know our positions, know our set pieces, and communicate better than anyone we play. That starts today."

Do the first shuffle — count off into pairs. Run a simple pairs passing drill. This gets touches going immediately and starts building the one-on-one relationships that are the foundation of the team.

Circle up again. This is where you introduce the formation — the positions, the two triangles, and the basic idea that every player has a home and a job. A whiteboard with magnetic dots is ideal here. Walk through where each position lives on the field and what their role is. Keep it to five minutes. The details come later. Right now you are planting the seed: this team has a system.

Then run a scrimmage — 7v7 if you have the numbers, 6v6 or 5v5 on a half field if you do not. Two full teams, however you can manage it. Let this one run mostly free. You are observing everyone at their baseline — who moves, who communicates, who reads the game naturally. Drop in light coaching as the play develops: "Make a run." "Get back on defense." "Pinch in to the center." Between the circle explanation and these live-action cues, you are establishing the basic vocabulary and ideas that the rest of the season builds on.

Close with the circle. Tell them one thing that went well. Tell them what you are working on next practice. Break with the team word.

### Second Practice

The second practice builds on the vocabulary from the first. Now the players have heard the position names and seen the shape. This practice starts putting them into it.

After warm-ups and the opening circle, run scenarios with slightly larger groups — 3v2 combinations, dead-ball set pieces, positional walkthroughs. This is where you start explaining roles in context. "Sweeper, you are the middle of the back triangle. When the ball is here, where do you want to be?" The players have the vocabulary from the first practice, so now you can start asking them to use it.

Run a scrimmage again, but this time you can freeze play occasionally to teach. "Freeze. Look at the shape. Sweeper, where is your home? Good. Backs, are you pinched in? Good. That is our shape." The players have been introduced to the positions, so freezing to check their understanding now makes sense — you are reinforcing what they learned, not quizzing them on something they have never seen.

By the end of the first week, the team has a shared language, every player knows every name, and the formation is no longer abstract — they have played in it. That foundation carries forward all season.
