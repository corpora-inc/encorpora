# Game Day

Game day is where everything you have practiced gets tested. The formation, the set pieces, the communication, the conditioning — it all comes together in sixty minutes of competitive play. The coach's role shifts from teacher to manager. You are no longer installing concepts. You are deploying them, adjusting them, and supporting the players who are executing them under pressure.

The most important thing to understand about game day is that your preparation is already done. If you did the work during the week, your team is ready. If you did not, no amount of sideline coaching will make up for it. The game is a test. Your practices are the studying. By the time you arrive at the field on Saturday, the studying is over.

## Pre-Game Routine

Arrive thirty minutes before kickoff. The same routine every game, no variation. Consistency reduces anxiety and gives the players a familiar structure to settle into.

**Minutes 30-20 before kickoff:** Players arrive, put on their gear, and start the standard pairs warm-up. Same pairs passing routine they do at every practice. By now this is muscle memory. It gets their feet moving and their minds focused. You do not need to instruct or supervise. Walk the field. Check the goals. Introduce yourself to the referee. Note the condition of the surface — is it wet, dry, bumpy, sloped? Any of these can affect how you play.

**Minutes 20-10 before kickoff:** Dynamic stretching as a group. High knees, butt kicks, side shuffles, karaoke. Then a short passing sequence with movement — pairs passing while moving up and down a twenty-yard stretch. The intensity should increase during this period. The players should be warm and slightly out of breath by the ten-minute mark.

**Minutes 10-5 before kickoff:** Gather the team. Announce the starting lineup and the positions. "Sofia, you are left back. Marcus, right back. Jayden, sweeper. Ava, left wing. Liam, right wing. Emma, point. Diego, goalkeeper." No surprises. If you are rotating the goalkeeper, tell them before the game who starts and when the switch happens.

**Minutes 5-0 before kickoff:** Brief tactical reminder. One or two sentences. "Remember, when we lose the ball, everyone gets goalside. And on corners, we practiced the setup — point near post, sweeper back post. Let us go." Team break. Take the field.

Do not give long speeches before the game. The players are nervous or excited or both, and their ability to absorb new information is at its lowest. Everything you need to say should be a reminder of what they already know, not something new.

## Starting Lineup Decisions

Your starting lineup should accomplish two things: put your strongest available unit on the field and give every player meaningful minutes over the course of the game. These two goals sometimes conflict, and how you resolve the conflict depends on the context of the game.

In a regular season game, start with a lineup that balances strength with development. Put your core players in their primary positions and use the starting lineup to give developing players a chance in secondary positions. A player who has been working at right wing in practice deserves a start there, even if they are not your strongest option at that position.

In a playoff or must-win game, start your strongest lineup. Every player in their best position, your most reliable pairing at the back, your sharpest attackers up top. This is not about fairness in this single game. It is about giving the team its best chance. The players who do not start will get minutes, but the starters set the tone.

The goalkeeper decision deserves special attention. If you have a dedicated goalkeeper, start them. If you are rotating goalkeepers, establish the rotation before the season starts and stick to it. Changing the goalkeeper rotation mid-game because you are losing sends a terrible message to the kid who just got pulled. Goalkeeper changes happen at halftime or at predetermined intervals, not as a reaction to goals conceded.

Write the starting lineup in your notebook. Write the substitution plan. Know who is coming in for whom at each substitution opportunity. Do not wing it. The time pressure of a game makes it too easy to lose track of who has played where and for how long.

## Coaching During the Game

This is where discipline matters most — yours, not the players'. The temptation to coach every touch, every pass, every run from the sideline is overwhelming. Resist it. Your players cannot process real-time instructions while also playing the game. A player who is listening to the coach is not watching the ball. A player who is watching the ball is not listening to the coach. You cannot have both.

Your sideline coaching should be limited to four categories:

**The code.** "Sixty." "Twenty-one twelve." "Two-thirteen." One call resets the team's shape and gets everyone's mind right. This is the most efficient sideline coaching you can do — one word or phrase and the entire team knows what posture to take. See chapter 3 for the full code system.

**Positional reminders.** Short, specific cues when a player has drifted significantly out of position. "Sofia, push up." "Marcus, get central." "Jayden, drop." These are not instructions about what to do with the ball. They are corrections to the team's shape. Deliver them once, clearly, and then let the player adjust.

**Encouragement.** Genuine, specific, and brief. "Good tackle, Ava." "Nice pass, Liam." "Great recovery, Diego." Players hear this and it reinforces the behavior. Do not overdo it. If you yell "great job" after every touch, it becomes noise.

**Set piece organization.** When a dead ball is coming — a corner, a free kick, a throw-in — you can and should remind the players of the plan. "Corner. Point, near post. Sweeper, back post. Wings, crash the box for rebounds." This is the one moment where real-time coaching is effective because the ball is dead and the players have a moment to process.

Beyond those four categories, keep it simple. The game belongs to the players. Your voice should be a calm signal — shape calls, position reminders, encouragement — not a running commentary. Trust the preparation you put in during the week and let the players play.

If you see a pattern that needs adjusting — the shape is drifting, transitions are slow — note it in your notebook and address it at halftime. The sideline is not the place to fix patterns. Halftime is.

## Halftime

Halftime is typically five to eight minutes at U10. You have time for water, one tactical adjustment, and one motivational point. That is it. Do not try to overhaul the game plan. Do not try to make five corrections. Pick the one thing that will have the most impact in the second half and focus on that.

Gather the team. Let them drink water for the first sixty seconds. Then talk.

**If you are winning:** "Our shape has been excellent. Stay sharp — keep the defensive transition crisp and we are fine."

**If you are losing:** "We are not getting into the 6-0 fast enough. Everyone back the instant they cross midfield. Lock down the center and counter."

**If it is close:** "The difference in the second half is who is more organized. Remember the shape, the transitions, and the set pieces. Let us execute."

Make the substitutions you planned. Tell the players coming in what position they are playing. Tell the players coming out something positive about their first half, even if it was rough. "Good work. Rest up. You will be back in."

Keep feedback about the system, not individual moments. "We gave up that goal because the shape collapsed, not because one person made a mistake. We fix the shape together."

## Reading the Opponent

You do not need to be a tactical expert to read an opponent at U10. You need to answer three questions, and you can answer all three in the first ten minutes of the game.

**Where do they attack?** Watch the first five minutes. Does the opponent favor one side of the field? Do they play through the middle? Do they have one player who gets the ball more than anyone else? If you can identify the primary attacking channel or player, you can adjust. "Their number seven gets every ball. Left back, stay tight on seven. Do not give them space."

**How do they defend?** Do they press high? Do they sit back? Do they mark man-to-man or play in zones? If they press high, your backs will be under pressure on goal kicks and distribution — prepare for that. If they sit back, you will have time on the ball in your own half — use it to build into the attacking shape. If they man-mark, movement off the ball will create openings because a marking defender has to follow their player, which creates gaps.

**What are they weak at?** Every U10 team has a weakness. Maybe their left back is slow. Maybe their goalkeeper cannot handle crosses. Maybe they do not press on throw-ins. Identify the weakness in the first ten minutes and exploit it. "Their left back is giving us space. Right wing, every time you get the ball, take them on one-versus-one. You are faster."

Share these observations with the team at halftime or during a natural stoppage. Keep it simple. One strength to be aware of, one weakness to exploit. That is enough.

## Substitution Patterns

At U10, most leagues require minimum playing time for every player, often at least half the game. Even if your league does not mandate it, every player on the roster should play meaningful minutes every game. A player who sits on the bench for the entire first half and enters with five minutes left in a blowout did not play a meaningful game.

Plan your substitutions before the game. If you have fourteen players and seven positions, you need to rotate seven players through those positions during the game. The simplest approach: start seven players, substitute all seven at halftime, then adjust in the second half based on how the game is going. This gives every player roughly a full half.

A more nuanced approach: make two or three substitutions at natural stoppages in the first half (goal kicks, goals scored, injuries), then make the remaining substitutions at halftime and in the second half. This keeps the team from completely turning over at halftime, which can disrupt rhythm.

When making substitutions, communicate clearly. Tell the player coming off what they did well. Tell the player going in what position they are playing and any specific instructions. "You are going in at left wing. We have been attacking down the right side. If the ball gets switched to you, take them on." The player going in should know what is happening in the game, not just their position.

Track minutes in your notebook. After the game, log how many minutes each player played and at what position. Over the course of the season, the minutes should be roughly equitable for all players in regular season games. In playoff games, the distribution may tilt toward your strongest players, but even then, every player should get on the field.

## Post-Game

**What you say after the game sticks with the players longer than anything you say during it.** A nine-year-old who hears genuine recognition of their effort carries that confidence into the next week. Make those words count.

**After a win:** Acknowledge the result without overdoing it. "We won because we executed our formation and our set pieces. The corner kick goal was exactly how we practiced it. Enjoy the win. We will keep building on Thursday." Do not spend five minutes celebrating. A quick team break and move on. Wins are expected, not extraordinary.

**After a loss:** This is the harder conversation. Be honest without being harsh. "We lost today. That is okay. We lost because our defensive transition was too slow in the second half. I saw it, and we are going to work on it this week. What I also saw was that you never gave up. You kept playing hard. That matters more than the result. We will be better on Thursday."

**After a draw:** Draws are often the most frustrating result for kids because nobody wins. Reframe it. "We earned a point today against a good team. I liked how we adjusted in the second half. Let us build on that."

In every post-game talk, follow the same structure: **acknowledge the result, identify one thing the team did well, identify one thing to work on, and look forward to the next practice.** Keep it under two minutes. The players are tired, they want to see their parents, and their retention after a game is low.

Keep individual feedback private and forward-looking. If a player had a tough game, a quiet word after goes a long way. "Jayden, you had a tough game today. It happens to everyone. Here is what I want you to think about this week: when the ball is on the other side of the field, stay in your zone. Do not chase it. We will work on this together." Private, calm, forward-looking.

If a family has questions after the game, let them know you are always happy to talk during the week. A day or two of perspective helps everyone reflect.
