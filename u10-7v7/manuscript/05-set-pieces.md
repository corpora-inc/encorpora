# Set Pieces

A set piece is any restart of play from a dead ball: kickoffs, corner kicks, goal kicks, throw-ins, free kicks, and penalty kicks. At U10, set pieces account for a disproportionate number of goals because most teams do not practice them. The ball goes dead, the coach yells "just kick it," and possession immediately becomes a coin flip. Your team is going to be different. Every dead ball is an opportunity, and your players will know exactly what to do for each one.

The advantage of practicing set pieces is that they are fully controllable. You cannot control what happens in open play, but you can control exactly where your players stand and what they do on a corner kick. That predictability is powerful. When your team lines up for a corner and every player is in their assigned spot, the opponent is reacting to you. When your team lines up for a goal kick and the goalkeeper knows exactly where to play it, you keep possession instead of giving it away.

This chapter covers every dead-ball situation your team will encounter. For each one, you get the setup for when you have the ball and the setup for when the opponent has the ball.

## Kickoffs

### Offensive Kickoff

Your team has the ball to start the half or after the opponent scores. Two players stand at the center circle. The point takes the kick. The sweeper stands next to them, ready to receive the first pass.

![Kickoff positions: Point and Sweeper at center, LW and RW in the attacking third ready for runs, LB and RB in the defensive third, GK in goal.](images/placeholder.png)

The rest of the team sets up in the neutral 2-1-1-2 shape. The left wing and right wing are in the attacking third, ready to make runs. The left back and right back are in the defensive third. The goalkeeper is in goal.

On the whistle, the point taps the ball forward to the sweeper. The sweeper has three options based on what the opponent does. If the opponent is pressing the center, the sweeper plays it wide to the left wing or right wing. If the center is open, the sweeper turns and dribbles forward. If the opponent is sitting back, the sweeper plays it back to a defender to build possession calmly.

The key principle is that the kickoff is not a launch. You do not blast the ball forward and hope. You play a controlled pass and start building from the formation. Most U10 teams kick it long on kickoffs and immediately turn possession into a contested ball. Your team keeps it, and that alone is an advantage.

### Defensive Kickoff

The opponent has the ball. Your team sets up in the neutral 2-1-1-2 shape. The point and sweeper are at the edge of the center circle, ready to press as soon as the ball is played. The wing strikers, backs, and goalkeeper are in their standard positions.

The point and sweeper watch the ball. As soon as the opponent plays the kickoff, the nearest of the two presses the ball carrier while the other covers the passing lane. The goal is not necessarily to win the ball — though that is ideal — but to force the opponent into a hurried pass or a backwards play. If the opponent plays it long, the backs deal with it. If they play it short, the press is already on.

Do not have the entire team push up to press the kickoff. The sweeper and point press. The rest of the team holds shape. A kickoff that goes over them and finds a runner behind the wing strikers is a dangerous situation you can avoid by keeping shape.

## Corner Kicks

### Offensive Corners

Corner kicks are one of the best scoring opportunities in U10 soccer. Many teams score more from corners than from open play. Your setup puts maximum bodies in dangerous positions while keeping enough cover to prevent a counterattack.

![Corner kick setup: the kicker at the corner flag, Point and RW (or LW) in the six-yard box area, Sweeper at the penalty spot, the opposite wing at the top of the box, LB and RB at the halfway line, GK in goal.](images/placeholder.png)

The kicker is whichever wing striker is on the side of the corner. If the corner is on the right, the right wing takes it. If it is on the left, the left wing takes it. This keeps it simple and gives both wing strikers practice with corner delivery.

Three players attack the goal. The point positions near the front post, looking to flick the ball on. The sweeper positions at the back post or the penalty spot, looking for the ball to come to them. The wing striker who is not taking the corner positions at the top of the six-yard box, looking for rebounds or short balls.

Two players stay back. Both backs position at or near the halfway line. Their job is to prevent the counterattack. If the corner does not result in a goal and the opponent clears the ball, the backs are already in position to defend.

The delivery target is the area between the six-yard box and the penalty spot, roughly head height. At U10, getting the ball to this area consistently is a skill that needs practice. Have your corner takers practice delivering to that zone at every Thursday practice. Even if only one in three corners reaches the target area, that is one in three corners with a genuine scoring chance, which is better than most U10 teams manage.

If the corner taker cannot reach the six-yard box with a lofted ball, use a short corner instead. The taker plays a short pass to the nearest teammate, who takes a touch and either crosses from a closer position or dribbles toward goal. Short corners require practice but they are effective when the alternative is a corner that barely reaches the near post.

### Defensive Corners

When the opponent has a corner, your setup is simple: everyone defends. The goalkeeper positions in the center of the goal, a yard off the line. The point and sweeper mark the two most dangerous opponents in the box — usually the tallest or most aggressive ones. The two wing strikers position at the posts — one at the near post, one at the far post. The backs position at the edge of the six-yard box, ready to clear anything that comes through.

No one attacks on a defensive corner. Everyone's job is to get the ball away from the goal. Clear it out — to the sideline, away from the center, out of bounds if you have to. Never clear it back into the center of the box. The goalkeeper catches it if they can. Once the ball is cleared, the team transitions to the neutral 2-1-1-2 and builds from there.

The key to defensive corners is staying locked on your mark. Assign specific marks in practice so every player knows their responsibility before the ball is in the air. "Jayden, your job is number nine. Wherever number nine goes, you go. I do not care where the ball is."

## Goal Kicks

### With a Build-Out Line

If your league uses a build-out line, the opponent must retreat behind it when your goalkeeper takes a goal kick. This gives your team uncontested space to play out of the back.

The goalkeeper places the ball. The left back and right back position near their respective sidelines inside the defensive third. The sweeper drops into the center channel as a secondary option.

The goalkeeper's first look is a back near the sideline. The sideline is the safest first pass — it puts the ball in a low-danger area where a turnover is not catastrophic. The back receives near the touchline, plays it inside to the sweeper, and the team builds from there through the center. If both backs are marked, the goalkeeper can play to the sweeper in the center, but the sideline option is always preferred.

The key is that the pass stays on the ground and goes to a teammate near the touchline. Do not kick it long when you have a build-out line giving you free space. Do not play it through the center of the defensive third. Send it to the sideline, let the back receive safely, and build from there.

### Without a Build-Out Line

If your league does not use a build-out line, the opponent can press immediately on a goal kick. The setup changes.

The goalkeeper still has the option of playing short to the backs, but the backs need to be prepared to receive under pressure. They should check toward the ball — move toward the goalkeeper to shorten the pass — rather than standing flat-footed and waiting for the ball to arrive. A moving target receiving a short pass is much harder to press than a stationary one.

If the opponent is pressing aggressively and the short options are all covered, the goalkeeper kicks it long. The target is the sweeper or the point in the midfield area. The wing strikers should be prepared to win the second ball — the ball that bounces off the initial contest. Positioning near the expected landing zone and reading the flight of the ball gives your team a chance to maintain possession even off a long kick.

A third option is the goalkeeper carrying the ball to the edge of the box and distributing from there. This buys time for the team to get into position and can draw the opponent's press before releasing the ball to an open player. It carries risk — a turnover at the edge of your own box is dangerous — but it is viable when the opponent is pressing the short options and you do not want to kick it long.

## Throw-Ins

Throw-ins are the most frequent dead-ball situation in a soccer game, and most teams waste them. The ball goes out of bounds, a player picks it up, throws it to the nearest teammate, and possession becomes a contest. Your team is going to treat throw-ins as a mini set piece with a plan.

### Offensive Throw-Ins (in the Opponent's Half)

The player taking the throw-in looks for movement. Two teammates should be offering options: one checking toward the thrower for a short throw, one making a run up the line for a longer throw. The short option is usually safer. The thrower delivers to the feet of the checking player, who takes a touch and plays forward.

If both options are covered, the thrower can throw the ball down the line into space for a teammate to chase. This is less controlled but can catch the opponent off guard. The one thing the thrower should never do is throw the ball backward into their own half unless there is absolutely no forward option. A throw-in in the opponent's half that goes backward surrenders territory for free.

### Defensive Throw-Ins (in Your Own Half)

When the throw-in is in your own half, the priority shifts from attacking to keeping possession. The safest option is a short throw to the nearest teammate who is facing the field and can play the ball forward or across the field on their first touch. Do not throw the ball into a crowd near your own goal. If the short options are covered, throw it down the line. The least risky option is always better than the most ambitious one when the throw-in is near your own goal.

### Defending Throw-Ins

When the opponent has the throw-in, mark up. Every player near the ball has an opponent to watch. The player closest to the thrower positions to cut off the short throw. The rest of the players nearby mark the most likely targets. The backs stay goalside. No one turns their back on the ball.

The most dangerous throw-in for the defending team is one in the attacking third near the corner flag, because it functions like a corner kick. When the opponent has a throw-in in that area, treat it like a defensive corner: mark up tight, clear the ball when it comes in, and do not ball-watch.

## Free Kicks

### Direct Free Kicks (Attacking)

A direct free kick is awarded when the opponent commits a foul. The ball is placed at the spot of the foul, and your team can shoot directly or pass. The decision depends on distance.

If the free kick is within shooting range — roughly fifteen yards or less from goal at U10 — the best player with a dead-ball shot takes it. The rest of the team positions as they would for a corner: point near the goal for a rebound, sweeper at the edge of the box for a pullback, backs staying home. The shooter aims low and to the side. At U10, goalkeepers struggle with low shots to the corners more than any other type.

If the free kick is farther from goal, treat it like a build-up opportunity rather than a shooting chance. Pass the ball to a teammate in space and attack from there. A free kick from your own half should be treated like a goal kick: build out calmly and transition to the neutral or attacking shape.

### Indirect Free Kicks (Attacking)

An indirect free kick means you cannot score directly — the ball must touch a second player before entering the goal. The simplest setup: one player taps the ball to a teammate standing two yards away, and that player shoots or passes into the box. Practice this sequence so it is automatic. The first touch is short and firm. The second player does not wait — they strike it or play it immediately. Delay gives the defense time to close down.

### Defending Free Kicks

When the opponent has a free kick near your goal, set a wall. At U10, the wall does not need to be elaborate. Two players stand shoulder to shoulder, ten yards from the ball (or wherever the referee positions them), blocking the near post. The goalkeeper positions for the far post and the area the wall does not cover. The rest of the team marks up in the box.

The wall's job is to block the near side. They do not duck, turn, or flinch. They stand firm. The goalkeeper's job is everything else. If the kick goes over or around the wall, the goalkeeper is positioned to make the save. If the kick goes through the wall — because someone ducked — that is a coaching failure, not a goalkeeping failure. Drill the wall to stand still.

For free kicks farther from goal, you do not need a wall. Just mark up in your defensive shape and deal with the ball as it comes in.

## Penalty Kicks

Penalty kicks are rare at U10, but they happen, and your team should be prepared for both sides.

### Taking a Penalty

Pick your best penalty taker before the season starts. It should be a player who is calm under pressure, strikes the ball cleanly, and can place their shot. At U10, the goalkeeper is small and the goal is the same size as in 11v11 — seven yards wide. A well-placed shot to the corner should go in almost every time.

The technique is simple: pick a side before you walk to the spot, do not change your mind, take a smooth run-up, strike the ball firmly with the inside of the foot toward the corner you chose, and follow through. Do not try to blast it. Placement beats power on penalties. Do not look at the goalkeeper. Look at the spot you are aiming for.

Practice penalties regularly. Once a week, have your penalty taker take five penalties after practice. Repetition builds the calm that prevents nerves from taking over in a game.

### Defending a Penalty

The goalkeeper stands on the line, centered in the goal. At U10, the odds favor the shooter, so the goalkeeper should not feel pressure to save every penalty. The technique: stay on the line until the ball is struck, then react. Watch the ball, not the player's body. Dive to the side of the shot. Make yourself as big as possible.

Some goalkeepers at this age pick a side before the kick and dive that way regardless of where the shot goes. This works about half the time and looks spectacular when it does. But it is a guess, and it means they are wrong the other half. Teaching the goalkeeper to react to the ball rather than guess is a better long-term approach, even if it means they get beaten cleanly by well-placed shots. Reacting is a skill. Guessing is luck.

## Goalkeeper Distribution

This is not technically a "set piece" in the traditional sense, but it functions like one. Every time the goalkeeper has the ball — whether from a save, a back pass, or a pick-up — the team should have a plan for what happens next.

### After a Save or Catch

The goalkeeper looks up immediately. Quick distribution after a save catches the opponent out of position because they were attacking and have players committed forward. The first look is a back near the touchline — a quick throw to a back on the sideline gets the ball out of the danger zone safely. If a wing striker has already turned and is sprinting forward into space, the goalkeeper can throw it ahead of them to launch a counter-attack, but the safe option is always the back near the sideline first.

If no one is open for a quick counter, the goalkeeper takes a moment. The backs position near the sidelines. The goalkeeper distributes with a throw to a back near the touchline. Not to the center. The sideline is the best defender. Get the ball out of the danger zone and let the sweeper build from there.

### After a Back Pass

When a defender passes the ball back to the goalkeeper, the opponent typically has time to set up their press. The goalkeeper picks the ball up (if it was a deliberate pass with the feet, they cannot pick it up — rules of the game — so they must play it with their feet). They look for the backs near the sidelines. Play it short to the touchline if possible. Play it long down the sideline if the press is too tight.

The principle across all goalkeeper distribution is the same: send it to the sideline. A throw to a back near the touchline is always the safest option. A throw into the center of the defensive third is always the most dangerous. The only time to punt is when every short option is covered, and even then, punt it toward the sideline, not into the center of the field where it becomes a coin flip.
