# Shoguns and Castles

Imagine you are a lookout standing on the coast of northern Kyushu, staring out at the sea on a gray November morning in 1274. The wind carries the smell of salt and rain. Then, through the mist, you see them: ships. Not ten ships, not fifty — *hundreds* of ships, stretching across the horizon like a dark, floating city. Flags snap in the wind, and you can hear the distant rumble of war drums echoing across the water.

The Mongol Empire — the largest empire the world had ever known — has come to conquer Japan.

You turn and run to raise the alarm. But what army could possibly stop this?

This is the story of the shoguns who ruled Japan, the castles they built, and the two terrifying invasions that nearly ended everything.

## The First Shogun

After the Genpei War, **Minamoto no Yoritomo** held more power than any warrior in Japanese history. In 1185, he was the undisputed military ruler. In 1192, the emperor officially gave him the title of **shogun** — short for *sei-i taishogun*, meaning "great general who subdues the barbarians."

Yoritomo did something very clever. Instead of moving to Kyoto, where the emperor's court was, he set up his government in **Kamakura**, a small town on the eastern coast, surrounded on three sides by forested hills and on the fourth by the sea. It was easy to defend and far from the distractions of court life. This was a military headquarters, not a palace of poetry.

His government was called the **Kamakura Bakufu** — *bakufu* literally means "tent government," like the field headquarters of an army on campaign. And that is exactly what it felt like: practical, tough, no-nonsense.

![Minamoto no Yoritomo in his role as shogun, seated in formal warrior attire](images/kamakura-shogun.png)

## Emperor Reigns, Shogun Rules

Here is where Japan's government gets really interesting — and a little confusing.

The emperor did not disappear. He still lived in his palace in Kyoto, performed religious ceremonies, and was officially the ruler of Japan. Everyone still bowed to him. His title was sacred.

But the real power — the ability to command armies, collect taxes, give out land, and punish criminals — belonged to the shogun in Kamakura. Think of it like this: the emperor was like the flag of a country. Everyone respects the flag, everyone salutes it. But the flag does not make decisions. The general does.

This arrangement — an emperor who reigns but does not rule, and a shogun who rules but is not emperor — lasted in various forms for almost seven hundred years. It is one of the most unusual systems of government in world history.

The samurai who served the shogun were given estates in exchange for their loyalty and military service. This system was a bit like feudalism in medieval Europe, where knights served lords who served kings. The samurai owed their shogun military service, and in return they received land and the right to govern it.

> **Did You Know?** After Yoritomo died in 1199 (he fell off his horse — a rather unheroic end for a great general), real power passed to his wife's family, the Hojo clan. The Hojo ruled as "regents" for the shogun, who was technically regent for the emperor. So Japan had a regent for a regent for an emperor — three layers of rulers! It sounds absurd, but it actually worked quite well for over a hundred years.

## Thunder from the West

While the Kamakura shogunate was busy governing Japan, something terrifying was happening on the Asian mainland. A Mongol warrior named **Temujin** had united the warring tribes of the steppe, taken the name **Genghis Khan**, and built the largest land empire in human history. By the time his grandson **Kublai Khan** took power, the Mongol Empire stretched from Korea to Hungary — a quarter of the world's land surface.

Kublai Khan ruled China (he founded the Yuan Dynasty) and he had his eye on Japan. In 1268, he sent messengers to Japan demanding that the Japanese submit to Mongol rule. The letter was polite on the surface but the threat was clear: obey, or face the consequences.

The Kamakura shogunate, led by the young regent Hojo Tokimune, did something incredibly bold. They ignored the letter. When Kublai Khan sent more messengers, the Japanese sent them away without a reply. This was, in the language of diplomacy, an extreme insult.

Kublai Khan began building an invasion fleet.

## The First Invasion: 1274

In November 1274, a massive fleet of roughly 900 ships carrying around 30,000 Mongol, Chinese, and Korean soldiers sailed from Korea toward Japan. They quickly captured the islands of Tsushima and Iki, overwhelming the small Japanese garrisons there.

Then the fleet landed on the beaches of Hakata Bay in northern Kyushu, and the Japanese samurai got their first taste of Mongol warfare. It was a shock.

Japanese warfare at this time was almost like a ritual. A samurai would ride forward, announce his name and ancestry in a loud voice, and challenge a worthy opponent to single combat. This was noble, honorable, and completely useless against the Mongols.

The Mongols fought as a coordinated unit. They used formations, signal drums, and poisoned arrows. They threw exploding ceramic bombs — early grenades — that terrified the Japanese horses with their noise and smoke. The samurai had never seen anything like it.

"Their soldiers fought without any order," one Japanese account noted about their own side, trying to make sense of the disaster. The Mongols pushed the samurai back from the beaches.

But then something unexpected happened. That night, a powerful storm blew in. The Mongol commanders, worried about their ships being smashed against the rocky shore, ordered a retreat back to the fleet. During the night, the storm worsened. Many ships were damaged or sunk. The Mongols sailed home, having lost perhaps a third of their force — not to Japanese swords, but to wind and waves.

Japan was saved. But everyone knew the Mongols would be back.

## Preparing for Round Two

The Hojo regents did not waste the breathing room. Over the next seven years, they organized the most ambitious defense project Japan had ever seen.

Along the entire shoreline of Hakata Bay, they built a massive **stone wall** — about twenty kilometers long and two meters high. It was not tall enough to stop a determined army, but it was high enough to prevent the Mongols from simply charging off their ships and onto the beach. Samurai from across western Japan were stationed behind the wall, training and waiting.

They also built small, fast attack boats designed to row out and harass the Mongol fleet before it could land. The Japanese had learned from the first invasion: do not let the Mongols form up on the beach. Hit them while they are still on the water.

## The Second Invasion: 1281

Kublai Khan tried again in 1281 — and this time, he was not taking any chances. He assembled **two enormous fleets**: one from Korea with 40,000 soldiers, and another from southern China with over 100,000 soldiers. Together, this was one of the largest naval invasion forces in history until the twentieth century — far larger than the Spanish Armada that would attack England three hundred years later.

The Korean fleet arrived first and attacked Hakata Bay. But this time, the Japanese were ready. The stone wall held. Samurai in small boats rowed out at night to attack Mongol ships, climbing aboard in the darkness, fighting hand-to-hand, and setting fires before escaping. These daring night raids kept the Mongol fleet off balance for weeks.

When the massive southern Chinese fleet finally arrived and merged with the Korean fleet, the combined armada anchored in Hakata Bay, preparing for an overwhelming assault.

And then the sky darkened.

## Kamikaze: The Divine Wind

On August 15, 1281, a typhoon of extraordinary power slammed into the Mongol fleet. For two days, the storm raged. Enormous waves picked up ships and smashed them together like toys. Vessels were driven onto rocks. Thousands of soldiers who could not swim — many of them Chinese infantry who had never wanted to be there in the first place — drowned in the churning sea.

When the storm finally passed, the Mongol fleet was destroyed. Estimates suggest that half to two-thirds of the invasion force was lost. The survivors limped home. Kublai Khan planned a third invasion, but it never came — he died in 1294, and his empire was already beginning to crack apart.

The Japanese called the typhoon **kamikaze** — "divine wind." They believed the gods themselves had sent the storm to protect Japan. This idea — that Japan was a sacred land, protected by divine forces — became deeply embedded in Japanese culture for centuries to come.

> **Did You Know?** The word "kamikaze" became known worldwide during World War II, when Japanese pilots deliberately crashed their planes into enemy ships. They were named after the divine wind that had saved Japan almost seven hundred years earlier. The original kamikaze, though, was a typhoon — nature's fury, not a human act.

## Castles Rising

After the Mongol threat passed, Japan entered a turbulent period. The Kamakura shogunate weakened — partly because fighting off the Mongols had been hugely expensive, and the shogunate could not reward the samurai who had fought. There was no conquered land to hand out, no treasure captured. Samurai who had risked their lives were angry and resentful.

In 1333, the Kamakura shogunate fell. A new military government, the Ashikaga shogunate, rose in its place, based back in Kyoto. But the Ashikaga shoguns were never as powerful as the Kamakura rulers had been, and Japan gradually drifted toward civil war.

As the country grew more dangerous, a new kind of building began to appear on the Japanese landscape: the **castle**.

Japanese castles were engineering marvels. They were very different from the stone fortresses of medieval Europe. A Japanese castle started with massive **stone foundations** — walls of enormous boulders fitted together without mortar, curving gracefully outward at the base and inward at the top. These walls were designed to resist earthquakes (this is Japan, after all) and to deflect cannon fire.

![A magnificent Japanese castle with white walls, curved roofs, and stone foundations reflected in a moat](images/japanese-castle.png)

On top of the stone foundations rose **wooden towers**, painted white and topped with gracefully curving roofs. The main tower — the *tenshu* — soared several stories high, visible for miles around, a symbol of the lord's power and wealth.

Around the castle ran **moats** — sometimes two or three rings of them — filled with water and sometimes stocked with sharp stakes or even carp (which could serve as emergency food during a siege). Between the moats, winding paths and dead ends forced attackers into kill zones where defenders could rain arrows, rocks, and boiling water down on them.

The walls had narrow slots for archers and, later, for muskets. Some slots were round (for guns) and others were rectangular or triangular (for arrows). Fake gates led to dead ends. Hidden doors allowed defenders to launch surprise counterattacks.

The most famous Japanese castle is **Himeji Castle**, which still stands today in Hyogo Prefecture. It is called "White Heron Castle" because its gleaming white walls and curving roofs look like a great bird about to take flight. Walking through its winding corridors and steep staircases, you can feel what it would have been like to attack this fortress — and understand why so few ever succeeded.

> **Did You Know?** Himeji Castle has survived wars, earthquakes, firebombing in World War II (a bomb landed on the top floor but miraculously did not explode), and the great Hanshin earthquake of 1995. It is over 400 years old and is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, visited by millions of people every year.

## A Country Cracking Apart

By the mid-1400s, the Ashikaga shogunate was losing control. In 1467, a dispute over who would be the next shogun erupted into the **Onin War** — a devastating conflict that burned much of Kyoto to the ground and lasted eleven years.

When the smoke cleared, the shogun was still technically in charge, but no one listened to him anymore. Across Japan, local warlords — called **daimyo**, meaning "great names" — seized power in their own provinces. Each daimyo built castles, raised armies, and fought their neighbors for land and power.

Japan had shattered into dozens of warring mini-kingdoms. The age of the shoguns had not ended — far from it — but the next chapter of Japan's story would be written in fire, blood, and gunpowder.

The Age of Warring States had begun.
