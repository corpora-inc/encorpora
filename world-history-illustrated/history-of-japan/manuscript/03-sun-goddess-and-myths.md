# Sun Goddess and Myths

Before there was land, before there was sky, before there were trees or mountains or rivers — there was nothing but a swirling, formless chaos, like a giant egg filled with fog.

Then, slowly, the lighter parts of the chaos drifted upward and became the heavens. The heavier parts sank downward and became a dark, oily sea. And on the bridge between heaven and this dark ocean, two gods appeared.

Their names were **Izanagi** and **Izanami**.

They looked down at the swirling water below them and wondered: *What is down there?*

Izanagi took a jeweled spear — glittering, magnificent, longer than any weapon you have ever imagined — and dipped it into the ocean. He stirred. The water churned and frothed. And when he pulled the spear back up, drops of salty water fell from its tip, and where they landed, the drops piled up and hardened into an island.

The first land.

Izanagi and Izanami looked at each other and smiled. Then they stepped down from the heavenly bridge onto this brand-new island to begin creating the world.

This is how the Japanese told the story of how their islands were born — not from tectonic plates and volcanic eruptions, but from a jeweled spear and two brave gods standing on a bridge in the sky.

![Izanagi and Izanami standing on the Floating Bridge of Heaven, stirring the ocean below with a jeweled spear as the first island forms](images/izanagi-izanami-creation.png)

## Creating the Islands

Once they stood on the first island, Izanagi and Izanami did not stop. Together, they created the other islands of Japan — one after another, like a potter shaping bowls on a wheel. The stories say they gave birth to the islands, as a mother gives birth to children. Each island had its own name, its own personality, its own spirit.

Then they began to create gods — *kami* — to fill the world. They made a god of the sea, a god of the rivers, a god of the wind, a god of the mountains, a god of the trees. Each force of nature had its own kami, its own divine spirit.

But when Izanami gave birth to the god of fire, the flames burned her terribly, and she died.

Izanagi was overcome with grief. He wept. He raged. And then he did something desperate — he traveled to **Yomi**, the Land of the Dead, to bring Izanami back.

He wandered through a dark, cold underworld until he found her. "Come back with me," he begged. "We have not finished creating the world."

Izanami whispered from the darkness: "I will ask the gods of this place if I may leave. But you must promise — **do not look at me.**"

Izanagi promised. He waited. But the waiting grew unbearable. He broke off a tooth from his hair comb, lit it as a torch, and held up the flame.

What he saw horrified him.

Izanami's body had already begun to decay. She was no longer the radiant goddess he remembered. She was something from a nightmare.

Izanagi screamed and ran. Izanami, furious that he had broken his promise, chased him through the tunnels of the underworld. He burst out of the entrance to Yomi and rolled a massive boulder across the opening, sealing it shut.

From behind the stone, Izanami's voice came one last time: "If you do this, I will destroy a thousand of your people every day."

Izanagi, panting, replied: "Then I will create one thousand and five hundred every day."

And that, the old stories say, is why people are born and people die — a bargain struck between two gods at the door to the underworld.

> **Did You Know?** The myth of Izanagi traveling to the land of the dead to rescue his wife is very similar to the Greek myth of Orpheus going to the underworld to bring back Eurydice. In both stories, the hero is told not to look — and in both stories, he cannot resist. Scholars have debated for centuries whether these stories are connected or whether humans everywhere simply tell the same kinds of tales.

## The Birth of the Sun

After escaping from Yomi, Izanagi felt contaminated by the land of death. He went to a river and began to wash himself — a ritual purification, cleaning away the darkness.

And as he washed, something miraculous happened.

When he washed his left eye, a brilliant goddess sprang into being — radiant, warm, dazzling as the morning sun. Her name was **Amaterasu**, and she was the goddess of the sun.

When he washed his right eye, a cool, silver god appeared — **Tsukuyomi**, the god of the moon.

And when he washed his nose, a wild, stormy god burst forth — **Susanoo**, the god of storms and the sea.

Izanagi looked at his three children and declared: "Amaterasu, you shall rule the heavens. Tsukuyomi, you shall rule the night. Susanoo, you shall rule the seas."

Amaterasu took her place in the sky and lit the world with her warmth. The rice grew. The flowers opened. The people below looked up and felt her light on their faces.

But Susanoo was not happy with his assignment. He wanted to be with his mother, Izanami, in the land of the dead. He wept and raged, and his tantrums caused terrible storms that flattened forests and flooded rivers. Finally, before leaving the heavens, he went to say goodbye to his sister Amaterasu.

What happened next is one of the most famous stories in all of Japanese mythology.

## The Cave of Darkness

Susanoo visited Amaterasu's realm, but instead of saying a respectful goodbye, he went on a rampage. He destroyed her rice paddies, flooding the fields she had carefully tended. He threw a dead horse through the roof of the hall where she and her attendants were weaving cloth. The attendants screamed. One was so frightened that she was fatally hurt.

Amaterasu was horrified and heartbroken. She fled to a cave called **Ama-no-Iwato** — the Heavenly Rock Cave — and rolled a stone door shut behind her.

And because Amaterasu was the sun, when she hid, **the entire world went dark**.

![Amaterasu emerging from the Heavenly Rock Cave, brilliant light streaming out, with gods and goddesses gathered around the entrance in celebration](images/amaterasu-cave.png)

The darkness was total. The rice stopped growing. Evil spirits crept out of every shadow. The other gods and goddesses gathered outside the cave, desperate and afraid. They begged Amaterasu to come out. She refused.

What could they do? You cannot force the sun to shine.

Then a clever goddess named **Ame-no-Uzume** had an idea.

She turned over a wooden tub in front of the cave entrance, climbed on top of it, and began to dance. Not a quiet, graceful dance — a wild, stomping, hilarious dance. She danced so outrageously that the eight hundred gods gathered outside began to laugh. The laughter grew louder and louder, a roar of divine amusement echoing across the dark heavens.

Inside the cave, Amaterasu heard the laughter. She was confused. *How can they be laughing when the world is dark?*

She cracked the stone door open, just a sliver, and called out: "Why are you laughing? How can you celebrate when the world has no light?"

Ame-no-Uzume called back: "We are celebrating because we have found a goddess even more radiant than you!"

And at that moment, the other gods held up a **sacred mirror** they had crafted — the **Yata no Kagami** — directly in front of the crack in the door.

Amaterasu saw her own reflection — a light so brilliant, so beautiful, that she was stunned. She leaned forward to look more closely. A powerful god grabbed her hand and pulled her out of the cave. Another quickly stretched a sacred rope across the entrance so she could never hide again.

Light flooded back into the world. The rice paddies shimmered. The evil spirits fled. The gods cheered.

The sun had returned.

> **Did You Know?** The sacred mirror used to lure Amaterasu from the cave is said to be one of the three **Imperial Regalia of Japan** — the mirror, the sword, and the jewel — treasures that have been passed down by the imperial family for centuries. The mirror is believed to be kept at the **Grand Shrine of Ise**, one of the holiest places in Japan, though almost no one has ever seen it.

## What the Myths Explain

These ancient stories are not just exciting tales. They are Japan's way of explaining the deepest questions about the world.

Why is there land? Because Izanagi stirred the ocean with a spear.

Why do people die? Because of the bargain between Izanagi and Izanami.

Why does the sun rise every morning? Because Amaterasu was pulled from her cave and can never hide again.

Why are there storms? Because Susanoo, the storm god, rages and weeps.

Why do volcanoes erupt? Why do earthquakes shake the ground? Why do the seasons change? For every natural force, there was a story — a *kami* — a spirit at work.

This way of understanding the world has a name. It is called **Shinto**.

## The Way of the Gods

Shinto — *Shin* meaning "gods" and *to* meaning "way" — is "the way of the gods." It is not exactly a religion in the way that Christianity or Islam are religions. There is no single holy book, no founder, no strict set of rules that everyone must follow. Shinto is something older and more organic — a way of experiencing the world that grew naturally from the lives of the people who lived on Japan's volcanic, storm-swept islands for thousands of years.

At the heart of Shinto is one powerful idea: **the world is full of kami.**

Kami are not quite "gods" in the Western sense — they are not all-powerful beings sitting on thrones in the clouds. Kami are **spirits** or **essences** that exist in everything. There is a kami in an ancient tree. A kami in a rushing waterfall. A kami in a towering mountain. A kami in a field of rice at harvest time, when the stalks bend heavy with grain.

Some kami are great and famous, like Amaterasu. Others are small and local — the spirit of a particular rock by a particular river, known only to the people who live nearby.

To honor the kami, the Japanese built **shrines** — sacred places, often in beautiful natural settings, marked at the entrance by a distinctive gate called a **torii**. You have probably seen pictures of torii gates — they look like the letter "Pi" from mathematics, painted bright vermillion red, standing at the edge of a forest or even rising out of the sea.

![A vermillion torii gate standing at the entrance to a Shinto shrine, surrounded by ancient cedar trees, with a stone path leading through it](images/shinto-shrine-torii.png)

Walking through a torii gate is like stepping from the ordinary world into the sacred world. The path beyond is lined with stone lanterns. A small fountain with wooden ladles invites you to wash your hands and rinse your mouth — purification, just as Izanagi purified himself in the river after returning from Yomi. You clap your hands twice to get the attention of the kami, bow, and offer a silent prayer.

There are more than **80,000 Shinto shrines** in Japan today. Some are enormous complexes visited by millions of people each year. Others are tiny wooden shelters on mountaintops, visited only by the occasional hiker. But all of them share the same basic purpose: to honor the kami and to maintain harmony between humans and the natural world.

## Purity and Harmony

Shinto places enormous importance on two ideas: **purity** (*kiyome*) and **harmony** (*wa*).

Purity does not mean moral perfection. In Shinto, it means being clean — physically and spiritually. Washing your hands before entering a shrine, keeping your home tidy, bathing regularly — these are all Shinto ideas about maintaining purity. Death, disease, and blood are considered impure — not evil, but unclean in a spiritual sense, things that must be washed away before you can stand in the presence of the kami.

Harmony means living in balance — with nature, with your community, with the rhythms of the seasons. The festivals that mark the Japanese calendar — planting festivals in spring, harvest festivals in autumn, fire festivals in winter — are Shinto celebrations of these rhythms. They are reminders that humans are not separate from nature. We are part of it.

> **Did You Know?** Many Japanese people visit a Shinto shrine at New Year to pray for good fortune and visit a Buddhist temple for funerals. They see no contradiction in this. In Japan, it is very common to follow both Shinto and Buddhist traditions. There is even a saying: "Born Shinto, die Buddhist."

## Myths as Living Stories

In many countries, ancient myths are treated as quaint old tales — interesting, perhaps, but not really part of modern life. In Japan, things are different.

The imperial family of Japan traces its ancestry directly back to **Amaterasu**, the sun goddess. According to the ancient chronicles written in the 700s CE — the *Kojiki* ("Record of Ancient Matters") and the *Nihon Shoki* ("Chronicles of Japan") — Amaterasu sent her grandson, Ninigi, down from heaven to rule the Japanese islands. Ninigi's great-grandson became **Emperor Jimmu**, the legendary first emperor of Japan.

Now, modern historians do not take this genealogy literally. Emperor Jimmu is a mythological figure, not a historical one. But the *idea* of this divine ancestry has been deeply important in Japanese culture for over a thousand years. It is one of the reasons the imperial family has survived as an unbroken line — the longest-reigning royal family in the world.

And Shinto is not a relic of the past. It is alive and thriving. When a new building goes up in Tokyo, a Shinto priest often performs a ceremony to pacify the kami of the land. When a baby is born, the family brings the child to a shrine to be blessed. When students face important exams, they visit a shrine dedicated to the kami of learning and tie a prayer card to a wooden rack, asking for success.

The seasons change. The cherry blossoms fall. The typhoons roar. The volcanoes rumble. And at eighty thousand shrines across the islands, people still clap their hands, bow their heads, and speak to the kami — just as their ancestors did thousands of years ago, when the world was new and the gods walked on a bridge between heaven and the sea.

## Why the Stories Matter

Every civilization has its creation myth — its story of how the world began. The Norse had a giant's body becoming the earth. The Greeks had the Titans battling the Olympians. The Egyptians had the sun god Ra emerging from the waters of chaos.

Japan's story is different from all of these. In the Japanese telling, the islands were not conquered or stolen or built by force. They were *stirred into being* — gently, deliberately, by two gods working together. The world was not made through war but through creation.

And when darkness came — when the sun goddess hid and the world went black — it was not a warrior who saved the day. It was a dancer. It was laughter. It was a mirror showing the sun her own beauty.

There is something deeply Japanese in this. Something that values cleverness over brute force, beauty over conquest, cooperation over domination.

These myths are more than stories. They are a window into how the Japanese have understood themselves — as people born from divine islands, living among spirits, connected to every rock and river and gust of wind.

The gods stirred the ocean. The islands rose from the waves. The sun emerged from her cave.

And on these islands, a civilization was about to take its next great step.
