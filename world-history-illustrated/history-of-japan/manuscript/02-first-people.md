# First People

Imagine pressing your fingers into a lump of wet clay. Now twist a piece of rope and press *that* into the clay too, leaving behind a beautiful pattern of ridges and swirls. Roll the clay into a coil. Stack coil on top of coil, smoothing the inside, until you have built a pot — a vessel to cook your food, store your nuts, or carry water from the stream.

Now fire it over hot coals until it hardens into stone.

Congratulations. You have just done something that people living in Japan were doing **16,000 years ago** — making some of the oldest pottery in the entire world. Thousands of years before the Egyptians built their pyramids, before the first cities rose in Mesopotamia, people on these volcanic islands were already shaping clay with their hands and turning it into art.

These were the **Jomon people**. And their story is one of the most remarkable — and least known — in all of human history.

## The Rope Makers

The word *Jomon* means "cord-marked" or "rope-patterned," and it describes the beautiful designs the Jomon people pressed into their pottery. Using twisted plant fibers, sticks, and their own fingertips, they decorated their pots with swirling, elaborate patterns that look almost like waves or flames leaping from the surface.

But Jomon pottery was not just beautiful. It was revolutionary. Most archaeologists used to believe that pottery was invented by farming people who needed containers to store grain. The Jomon people proved that idea wrong. They were **hunter-gatherers** — people who survived by hunting animals, catching fish, and collecting wild plants — and yet they invented pottery anyway.

Why? Because they needed to cook. Japan's forests were full of acorns, chestnuts, and other nuts that are bitter or even toxic when raw. But boil them in water inside a clay pot, and the bitterness washes away, leaving nutritious food. Pottery meant the Jomon people could eat things that would have been impossible before. It was a cooking revolution, thousands of years before anyone planted a seed.

> **Did You Know?** Some Jomon pottery is so old — dating back to around 14,000 BCE — that it may be the oldest pottery ever found anywhere on Earth. Only pottery from China is close in age. Scientists are still debating who made pots first!

## Life in the Jomon World

Close your eyes and picture the Jomon world. It is around 5,000 BCE — roughly 7,000 years ago. The ice age ended long ago, and Japan's climate is warm and wet. Thick forests of chestnut, walnut, and oak trees cover the mountains. Rivers run full of salmon. The coasts are rich with clams, oysters, and fish.

A Jomon village sits near the edge of a forest, close to a river. The houses are **pit dwellings** — oval holes dug about half a meter into the earth, with wooden posts holding up a thatched roof of grass or bark. They are small, maybe four or five meters across, and dark inside, lit only by a fire pit in the center. But they are warm in winter and cool in summer, snug against the wind.

Around the village, life hums with activity. Women and men gather chestnuts in woven baskets and bring them back to boil in their big Jomon pots. Others scrape animal hides to make clothing. Down by the shore, fishermen paddle dugout canoes and cast lines made of twisted plant fiber into the ocean, pulling up sea bream and tuna. Children chase each other between the houses, their laughter carrying on the wind.

The Jomon people were not farmers. They did not plant crops or raise animals (except for dogs — the Jomon were among the first people in Asia to keep dogs as companions). Instead, they were experts at reading the natural world. They knew which mushrooms were safe to eat and which were poison. They knew where the deer would graze in autumn and where the salmon would run in spring. They understood the tides, the seasons, the forests, and the weather with a depth of knowledge that took thousands of years to accumulate.

And they were not just surviving. They were thriving.

## Mysterious Figures and Shell Mountains

The Jomon people left behind clues about their world that still puzzle scientists today.

One of the strangest is the **dogu** — small clay figurines with wide, staring eyes and exaggerated features. Some have what look like goggles or helmets. Some appear to be pregnant women. Some have heart-shaped faces. Nearly all of them were found deliberately broken, as though they were smashed on purpose as part of a ritual.

What were they for? Nobody knows for sure. Were they goddesses? Spirit guardians? Healing charms — broken to "absorb" an illness from a sick person? Were they toys? The Jomon left no writing, so their dogu keep their secrets.

The Jomon also left behind enormous **shell mounds** — huge piles of discarded shells, animal bones, broken pottery, and other refuse that accumulated over centuries. These might sound like just ancient garbage heaps, but to archaeologists, they are treasure chests. By studying what the Jomon threw away, scientists can learn what they ate, how their diet changed over time, and even what diseases they suffered from.

Some shell mounds are enormous. The Kasori Shell Mound near modern-day Tokyo stretches over 150 meters across. That is a lot of clam dinners.

> **Did You Know?** Jomon people practiced a kind of ancient dentistry. Archaeologists have found skulls with teeth that were deliberately pulled out — not because they were rotten, but as a coming-of-age ritual. When a young person reached adulthood, certain teeth were removed as a mark of their new status. Ouch!

## Thousands of Years of Peace

Here is something remarkable about the Jomon period: it lasted an incredibly long time. From roughly 14,000 BCE to about 300 BCE, the Jomon way of life continued for nearly **14,000 years**. To put that in perspective, all of recorded human history — from the invention of writing to the phone in your pocket — is only about 5,000 years. The Jomon era was almost three times longer than that.

And during all those thousands of years, the Jomon people seem to have lived relatively peacefully. Archaeologists have found very few Jomon skeletons with wounds from weapons. There are no fortifications around their villages, no great battles recorded in the archaeological record. This does not mean the Jomon never fought — all human societies have conflicts — but it does suggest that large-scale warfare was not a major part of their world.

Why? Perhaps because Japan's rich forests, rivers, and coastlines provided enough food for everyone without the need to fight over territory. When nature gives you enough, there is less reason to take from your neighbor.

But this long, slow, peaceful era was about to change — dramatically.

## Strangers from Across the Sea

Sometime around **300 BCE**, new people began arriving on the shores of northern Kyushu — the Japanese island closest to the Korean Peninsula. They came in boats, crossing the strait from mainland Asia, and they brought with them something that would transform Japan forever.

They brought **rice**.

Not wild rice gathered from a swamp, but carefully cultivated wet rice — planted in flooded fields called **paddies**, tended through the growing season, and harvested in autumn to feed families through the winter. Along with rice, these newcomers brought new tools — **bronze** for bells and weapons, **iron** for axes and farming implements — and a completely different way of life.

Archaeologists call this new culture **Yayoi**, named after the neighborhood in Tokyo where their distinctive pottery was first discovered. Yayoi pottery was different from Jomon ware — simpler, smoother, and more practical. Less art, more function. These were pots designed for storing rice, not for beauty.

## The Rice Revolution

The arrival of rice farming changed everything about life in Japan, and it happened with astonishing speed. Within just a few centuries, wet rice agriculture spread from Kyushu northeast across Shikoku and Honshu, transforming the landscape as it went.

Why was rice so revolutionary? Because a rice paddy can feed far more people than the same area of forest or coastline. A single hectare of well-maintained paddy can produce enough rice to feed a dozen people for a year. Hunting and gathering simply cannot match that.

But rice farming demands something that hunting and gathering does not: **cooperation**. Building rice paddies is backbreaking work. You have to flatten the land, build walls of earth to hold the water in, dig channels to bring water from rivers to the fields, and then plant, weed, and harvest together. One family cannot do it alone. It takes a whole village working in rhythm.

This need for cooperation changed how people lived together. Yayoi villages were larger and more organized than Jomon settlements. They had **granaries** — raised storehouses built on stilts to keep the rice dry and safe from rats. And because some families had more rice than others — because some land was better than other land, or some leaders were more skilled at organizing labor — something new appeared in Japan for the first time.

**Inequality.**

In the Jomon world, people seem to have been roughly equal. Everyone hunted, everyone gathered, and nature's gifts were shared. In the Yayoi world, some people had more. Chiefs and powerful families controlled the best rice land. They wore bronze and jade ornaments. When they died, they were buried with precious objects — bronze mirrors, iron swords, glass beads — while ordinary people were buried with nothing.

The age of equality was ending. The age of kings was beginning.

## What Happened to the Jomon?

This is one of the great questions of Japanese history, and scientists are still arguing about it.

Did the Yayoi people conquer the Jomon? Did they push them out? Or did the two groups slowly blend together over centuries — intermarrying, sharing tools and ideas, merging into a single people?

The answer seems to be: **a little of everything.**

In some places, the Jomon adopted rice farming and Yayoi tools and gradually became part of the new culture. In other places, especially in the remote mountains and in northern Honshu and Hokkaido, people continued the Jomon way of life for centuries longer, resisting or simply ignoring the changes happening to the south.

Modern genetic studies have shown that today's Japanese people carry DNA from both Jomon and Yayoi ancestors. The Jomon did not disappear. They are still there, woven into the fabric of Japan.

And one group in particular may carry a particularly strong Jomon heritage: the **Ainu** people of Hokkaido. The Ainu maintained a distinct hunter-gatherer culture, their own language, and their own traditions well into modern times. Many researchers believe they are the closest living descendants of the ancient Jomon.

> **Did You Know?** The Jomon people made elaborate clay masks, lacquered wooden combs, and even earrings made from polished stone. They also created some of the earliest known examples of lacquerware — objects coated with the sap of the lacquer tree, which hardens into a beautiful, waterproof finish. A lacquered comb found in Hokkaido has been dated to about 7,000 BCE!

## The Sound of Bronze Bells

One of the most mysterious objects left behind by the Yayoi people is the **dotaku** — a bronze bell, sometimes as tall as a child, decorated with pictures of animals, people, and scenes of daily life. Hundreds of dotaku have been found buried on hilltops and mountainsides, far from any village.

What were they for? Some scholars think they were used in harvest rituals — rung to call the spirits and ask for good crops. Others think they were symbols of power, buried as offerings to the gods. The pictures on their surfaces show deer, herons, dragonflies, people pounding rice, and hunters with bows — a window into a world that existed more than 2,000 years ago.

The dotaku are silent now. But if you could ring one, you might hear the sound that echoed across ancient Japan as a new civilization was taking shape — a civilization built on rice, organized into villages and clans, and reaching toward something bigger.

## Two Worlds Become One

The Jomon and the Yayoi represent two fundamentally different ways of being human. The Jomon lived *with* nature, taking what the land offered, moving with the seasons, leaving the forests and coastlines largely unchanged. The Yayoi lived *on* nature, reshaping the land to grow rice, digging channels, building walls, bending the earth to their will.

Both approaches have their wisdom. Both have their costs.

But in the end, the rice won. Farming could feed more mouths, build larger villages, and support more complex societies. From the Yayoi foundation, clans would grow into kingdoms, kingdoms into an empire, and a scattered collection of fishing villages and farming communities would slowly, over centuries, become the nation of Japan.

The Jomon gave Japan its oldest roots — a deep connection to nature, a tradition of craftsmanship, and perhaps that quiet, patient quality that would mark Japanese culture for millennia to come.

The Yayoi gave Japan its engine — the cooperative rice-growing society that would power everything from great temples to mighty armies.

Together, they are the First People. And their story is just getting started.
