# Unification and Peace

There is a famous Japanese saying about three of the most extraordinary men who ever lived:

*"Nobunaga made the rice cake, Hideyoshi shaped it, and Ieyasu ate it."*

In the last chapter, we watched Nobunaga begin to unite a fractured Japan before his shocking murder in 1582. Now we meet the two men who finished his work — and the one who would create a system of order so powerful that it brought 250 years of peace to a land that had known nothing but war.

This is the story of a peasant who became a ruler, a battle that decided the fate of millions, and the most patient man in Japanese history.

## The Peasant Who Became a Lord

When Nobunaga died, the question on everyone's mind was: who would take his place? The answer came from the most unlikely person imaginable.

**Toyotomi Hideyoshi** was born in 1537 to a peasant family. His father was a foot soldier — the lowest rank in the military. As a boy, Hideyoshi was scrawny, short, and so homely that people called him "Monkey" because of his face. In a society where your birth determined everything, Hideyoshi had been dealt the worst possible hand.

But Hideyoshi had something more valuable than noble blood: he had extraordinary intelligence, charm, and an almost supernatural ability to read people and situations.

As a young man, he entered Nobunaga's service as a sandal-bearer — a servant so lowly that he literally carried his master's shoes. Legend says that on cold mornings, Hideyoshi would warm Nobunaga's sandals by tucking them inside his own clothing. Whether or not this story is true, it captures something real about Hideyoshi: he understood that winning people's trust started with small acts of devotion.

Step by step, Hideyoshi rose. He proved himself brave in battle, creative in strategy, and brilliant at building alliances. Nobunaga, who cared about talent more than birth, promoted him again and again. By the time of Nobunaga's death, Hideyoshi was one of his most powerful generals.

## Revenge and Power

When news of Nobunaga's assassination reached Hideyoshi, he was far away, fighting a campaign in western Japan. But he moved with astonishing speed. He made a rapid peace with his current enemy, then marched his army back toward Kyoto in what became known as the "Great Return" — covering over 200 kilometers in just a few days, an extraordinary feat for an army on foot.

Thirteen days after Nobunaga's death, Hideyoshi caught the traitor Akechi Mitsuhide at the **Battle of Yamazaki** and destroyed him. Mitsuhide had ruled as Japan's most powerful man for exactly thirteen days. (The Japanese have a saying for someone whose glory is brief: "Mitsuhide's thirteen days.")

By avenging his master, Hideyoshi positioned himself as Nobunaga's true heir. Over the next eight years, through a combination of military victories, clever diplomacy, strategic marriages, and sheer force of personality, he brought all of Japan under his control.

Think about what that means. A man born as a peasant, with no family name, no noble connections, no money, and no advantages, rose to become the supreme ruler of an entire nation. It would be like a janitor becoming president — except in a society where such things were considered absolutely impossible.

> **Did You Know?** Because Hideyoshi was born a commoner, he could never officially become shogun — that title was reserved for men of warrior lineage. Instead, he took the title of *kampaku*, meaning "regent" to the emperor. He also gave himself the grand family name "Toyotomi," which means "bountiful minister." When you are the most powerful man in the country, you can name yourself whatever you like!

## Hideyoshi's Japan

Hideyoshi did far more than just conquer. He rebuilt, reorganized, and reshaped Japan from top to bottom.

He conducted a **national land survey** — sending officials to every corner of the country to measure and record every rice paddy, every field, every piece of productive land. This gave him an exact picture of Japan's wealth and allowed him to set fair (if heavy) taxes. No ruler in Japanese history had ever had such detailed knowledge of his domain.

He issued the **Sword Hunt** of 1588, ordering that all peasants must turn in their weapons — swords, spears, guns, everything. The official reason was that the metal would be melted down to build a great statue of the Buddha. The real reason was far more practical: an armed peasantry is a dangerous peasantry. By disarming the farmers, Hideyoshi made sure that only the samurai class carried weapons. The age of *gekokujo* — when peasants could rise to power through force — was deliberately being shut down.

He also froze the social classes. From now on, a samurai was always a samurai, a farmer was always a farmer, and an artisan was always an artisan. No more switching. No more rising from sandal-bearer to supreme ruler. Hideyoshi, the greatest social climber in Japanese history, pulled up the ladder behind him.

## Castles and Golden Rooms

Hideyoshi was a builder on a grand scale. His greatest creation was **Osaka Castle**, one of the most magnificent fortresses in Japanese history. Its stone walls were made from boulders so enormous that some weighed over a hundred tons — dragged into place by thousands of workers. The main tower rose high above the city, its upper floors covered in gold leaf that blazed like fire in the sunlight.

Inside, Hideyoshi decorated rooms with gold — gold walls, gold screens, gold tea ceremony utensils. He even built a portable golden tea room that he could take with him on his travels. This was partly personal taste (Hideyoshi loved flashy things) and partly politics. Every visiting lord who saw all that gold understood the message: *I am richer and more powerful than you. Do not even think about rebelling.*

## Hideyoshi's Great Mistake

For all his brilliance, Hideyoshi made one colossal error. In 1592 and again in 1597, he launched massive invasions of **Korea**, with the ultimate goal of conquering China. Hundreds of thousands of Japanese soldiers crossed the sea.

The invasions were a disaster. Though Japanese forces initially swept through Korea, they were pushed back by Korean resistance — including the legendary Korean admiral **Yi Sun-sin**, whose armored "turtle ships" devastated the Japanese navy — and by Chinese armies that came to Korea's aid. The wars dragged on, accomplishing nothing except enormous suffering on all sides.

Hideyoshi died in 1598, at the age of sixty-one, while the second Korean invasion was still grinding on. His troops came home. His five-year-old son, Hideyori, was left as his heir, protected by a council of powerful lords who had sworn to serve the boy.

But one of those lords had been waiting his entire life for this moment.

## The Patient Old Fox

**Tokugawa Ieyasu** was born in 1543 — the same year guns arrived in Japan. As a boy, he was taken hostage by a powerful clan and spent years as a prisoner, far from his family. He learned early that survival required patience, caution, and the ability to wait for the right moment.

As a young lord, Ieyasu allied with Nobunaga. He fought beside Hideyoshi. He watched, learned, and bided his time. While Nobunaga was bold and Hideyoshi was flashy, Ieyasu was quiet, steady, and methodical. He was like a tortoise in a race full of hares — slow and careful, but always moving forward, and still standing when the hares had exhausted themselves.

After Hideyoshi's death, the council of lords quickly split into two factions. On one side was Ieyasu, the most powerful daimyo in eastern Japan. On the other was a coalition of western lords led by **Ishida Mitsunari**, who was loyal to Hideyoshi's young son.

Everyone knew that war was coming. The only question was where and when.

## The Battle of Sekigahara

The answer came on October 21, 1600, in a misty valley called **Sekigahara** — a narrow plain surrounded by mountains in central Japan. It was the crossroads where the eastern and western roads met, and it would become the most important battlefield in Japanese history.

![The Battle of Sekigahara and the unification of Japan under Tokugawa rule](images/tokugawa-unification.png)

Approximately 160,000 warriors faced each other that morning — roughly 80,000 on each side. The fog was so thick that soldiers could barely see a hundred meters. At eight in the morning, the first gunshots cracked through the mist, and the battle began.

For hours, the fighting was brutal and confused. Units clashed in the fog, sometimes unsure if they were fighting friend or enemy. Ieyasu watched from his command post, waiting.

He was waiting because he had a secret weapon — not a gun or a sword, but something far more powerful: **betrayal**. In the weeks before the battle, Ieyasu had been secretly negotiating with several lords on the western side, promising them land and power if they switched sides at the critical moment.

At around noon, a western lord named **Kobayakawa Hideaki**, who commanded 15,000 troops on a hill overlooking the battlefield, suddenly turned his army against his own allies. The western coalition, attacked from the front and now from the side, collapsed. What had been an even battle became a rout.

By evening, Ieyasu had won the most decisive battle in Japanese history. Over 30,000 men lay dead. The western lords who had opposed him were executed or stripped of their lands. Ishida Mitsunari was captured and beheaded.

The Battle of Sekigahara decided that the Tokugawa family would rule Japan. And they would rule it for the next two and a half centuries.

> **Did You Know?** The battle lasted only about six hours, but it determined the course of Japanese history for 260 years. Sekigahara is sometimes called "the battle that made Japan." Even today, in Japanese, the phrase "a Sekigahara" is used to describe any decisive, all-or-nothing confrontation.

## The Tokugawa Shogunate

In 1603, the emperor officially granted Ieyasu the title of **shogun**. He established his capital at **Edo** — a small fishing village that would grow into one of the world's largest cities. Today, we call it Tokyo.

![The construction of Edo Castle, with workers building massive stone walls](images/edo-castle-construction.png)

Ieyasu spent the rest of his life building a system designed to ensure that no one would ever challenge the Tokugawa family. He was obsessed with one question: *How do I make this last?*

His answer was brilliant and ruthless.

## The Sankin-Kotai System

Ieyasu's cleverest invention was called **sankin-kotai** — "alternate attendance." Here is how it worked:

Every daimyo in Japan was required to spend every other year living in the shogun's capital of Edo. During the years when the daimyo returned to his home province, his wife and children had to stay behind in Edo — as hostages, though no one used that word.

Think about what this meant. Every year, hundreds of lords traveled back and forth across Japan with huge processions of samurai, servants, and baggage. The travel itself was enormously expensive. Maintaining two households — one in your home province and one in Edo — was even more expensive. And your family was always in the shogun's hands.

The sankin-kotai system was genius for several reasons. First, it kept the daimyo poor. A lord who spends half his income on travel and fancy Edo houses does not have money left over to raise a rebel army. Second, it kept the daimyo's families as permanent hostages, guaranteeing good behavior. Third, it meant the shogun always had hundreds of powerful lords living right under his nose, where he could watch them.

As a side effect, all that travel created a network of well-maintained roads, bustling inn towns, and a thriving economy along the routes. Japan became more connected than ever before.

## Frozen in Place

Ieyasu also formalized the rigid **social class system** that Hideyoshi had begun. Japanese society was divided into four official classes:

1. **Samurai** — the warrior-administrators at the top
2. **Farmers** — honored because they grew the rice that fed everyone
3. **Artisans** — craftspeople who made useful things
4. **Merchants** — at the bottom, because they were seen as producing nothing, only buying and selling what others had made

Below all four classes were outcast groups who performed jobs considered impure.

You were born into your class and you died in it. A farmer's son became a farmer. A samurai's daughter married a samurai. The social mobility of the Sengoku period — when a sandal-bearer could become supreme ruler — was gone forever.

Ieyasu also kept a careful eye on **foreign influence**. He allowed some trade with the Dutch and Chinese, but he was suspicious of Christianity and European colonialism. His successors would take this much further, eventually closing Japan to almost all outside contact — but that is a story for a later chapter.

## Three Who Made a Nation

Tokugawa Ieyasu died in 1616, at the age of seventy-three — remarkably old for that era. He had lived through the entire Age of Warring States, allied with the two men who preceded him, and outlasted them both.

Let us return to that saying about the three great unifiers and remember what each one contributed:

**Nobunaga made the rice cake.** He was the visionary who saw that Japan could be unified, smashed the old order, introduced guns, and conquered a third of the country through sheer brilliance and terror.

**Hideyoshi shaped the rice cake.** He was the charmer, the builder, the peasant genius who finished the military conquests, reorganized society, and proved that one man's determination could overcome any obstacle of birth.

**Ieyasu ate the rice cake.** He was the patient strategist who waited until the time was right, won the decisive battle, and then — most importantly — built a system of control so thorough that his family ruled for fifteen generations.

Together, these three men transformed Japan from a patchwork of warring kingdoms into a unified nation. The long, bloody Age of Warring States was truly over.

And what came next was extraordinary. For the next 250 years — from 1603 to 1868 — Japan experienced an era of almost unbroken peace. No civil wars. No invasions. No revolutions. In a world full of conflict, Japan became an island of stability where art, theater, literature, and culture could flourish in ways the warring daimyo could never have imagined.

The age of the sword was yielding to something new. But the swords did not disappear — they just hung on the wall, waiting, while Japan began to dream different dreams.

> **Did You Know?** Tokugawa Ieyasu was so determined to be remembered that he arranged to be worshipped as a god after his death. His magnificent shrine at **Nikko**, north of Tokyo, is one of the most elaborately decorated buildings in Japan — carved with hundreds of colorful animals, flowers, and mythical creatures. Among the carvings are the famous "three wise monkeys" — see no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil — which have become known around the world.
