# Japan Today

At 6:00 in the morning, Tokyo Station is already humming. Thousands of commuters move through the vast building with calm, practiced precision -- pouring through turnstiles, gliding down escalators, stepping onto platforms where a gleaming white train with a nose like a jet fighter sits waiting.

This is the *Shinkansen* -- the bullet train -- and when its doors close with a soft chime and it pulls away from the platform, you barely feel it move. Within minutes, the city is a blur outside your window. The train accelerates to 320 kilometers per hour -- that is almost as fast as a small airplane -- and the ride is so smooth that you could balance a coin on the armrest and it would not fall over.

![A sleek white bullet train speeding past Mount Fuji on a clear day](images/bullet-train-fuji.png)

The Shinkansen began service in 1964, making it the world's first high-speed rail system. Since then, it has carried more than ten billion passengers. And here is the fact that makes engineers around the world shake their heads in amazement: in over sixty years of operation, with trains running every few minutes on some lines, the Shinkansen has never had a single fatal accident involving a passenger. The average delay per train? About fifty-four seconds. Not fifty-four minutes. Fifty-four *seconds*.

If you want to understand modern Japan in a single image, the Shinkansen is a good place to start. It is fast, precise, beautiful, and deeply considerate of the people who use it. It is a machine, but it was made with the care of a craftsperson.

## The World's Biggest City

The bullet train brings you into the heart of the world's largest metropolitan area. Greater Tokyo is home to roughly 37 million people -- more than the entire population of Canada. If Tokyo were a country, it would be bigger than most.

![The famous Shibuya Crossing packed with pedestrians, surrounded by neon signs and giant screens](images/tokyo-shibuya-crossing.png)

Stand at the Shibuya Crossing on a Friday evening and watch what happens when the traffic lights turn red on all sides and the "walk" signals flash on at once. Up to 3,000 people step off the curbs simultaneously, streaming across the intersection from every direction in a swirling, crisscrossing flood of humanity. And somehow -- no one bumps into anyone. It is like watching a school of fish change direction, each person instinctively adjusting their path to flow around every other person. First-time visitors stand on the corner with their mouths open, stunned by the organized chaos of it.

Tokyo is not one city but a galaxy of neighborhoods, each with its own personality. In Akihabara, entire buildings are dedicated to electronics, video games, and anime merchandise. In Asakusa, the ancient Senso-ji temple stands amid narrow streets where vendors sell traditional snacks just as they have for centuries. In Harajuku, teenagers invent wild new fashions on the sidewalks. In Ginza, elegant department stores sell everything from handmade pottery to French pastries.

The city works with a precision that visitors find almost eerie. Trains run exactly on time. Streets are spotlessly clean even though there are remarkably few trash cans -- people carry their garbage home. Crime rates are among the lowest of any major city in the world. If you leave your wallet on a train, there is a genuinely good chance you will get it back with everything still inside.

> **Did You Know?** Japan has more than 5.5 million vending machines -- about one for every 22 people. You can buy not just drinks and snacks, but also fresh eggs, hot soup, flowers, umbrellas, and even good-luck charms. Some vending machines in train stations serve freshly brewed coffee that rivals what you would get at a cafe.

## Anime, Manga, and the Imagination Factory

Walk into almost any bookstore in the world -- in Paris, in Buenos Aires, in Cairo, in Seoul -- and you will find shelves stacked with Japanese manga. Turn on a streaming service in almost any country, and you will find Japanese anime. Characters like Goku, Naruto, Sailor Moon, and the creatures of Pokemon are recognized by children (and adults!) on every continent.

Japan's pop culture has become a global language. Manga -- Japanese comics -- are read by hundreds of millions of people worldwide. They cover every imaginable subject: adventure, romance, cooking, sports, science fiction, history, and even educational topics. Unlike many Western comics, manga is read from right to left, following the traditional Japanese reading direction.

Anime -- Japanese animated films and television series -- ranges from lighthearted shows for young children to complex, cinematic works that rival anything Hollywood produces. Director Hayao Miyazaki, co-founder of Studio Ghibli, created films like *Spirited Away*, *My Neighbor Totoro*, and *Princess Mononoke* that are considered among the greatest animated films ever made. *Spirited Away* won the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature in 2003 -- the first (and so far only) Japanese animated film to do so.

Video games are another area where Japan changed the world. Nintendo, Sony PlayStation, and companies like Sega, Capcom, and Square Enix have created some of the most beloved games in history: *Super Mario*, *The Legend of Zelda*, *Final Fantasy*, *Sonic the Hedgehog*, and *Pokemon*, to name just a few. The modern video game industry as we know it was largely built by Japanese creators.

> **Did You Know?** The word "manga" has been used in Japan since the late 1700s. The famous ukiyo-e artist Hokusai -- the man who painted *The Great Wave* -- used the word for his collection of sketches. So in a way, today's manga artists are carrying on a tradition that stretches back to the Edo period!

## Ancient Traditions in a Modern World

Here is what makes Japan truly extraordinary: all this dazzling modernity exists side by side with traditions that are centuries old, and neither one seems the least bit out of place.

![A scene showing ancient temple architecture right next to modern glass skyscrapers](images/ancient-modern-contrast.png)

In the middle of Tokyo's forest of skyscrapers, you can find the Meiji Shrine, surrounded by 170,000 trees in a dense forest that feels like it belongs in another century. Business executives in crisp suits step off the city sidewalk, pass through the towering wooden gate called a *torii*, and enter a world of gravel paths, silence, and sacred space. They clap their hands twice, bow their heads, and offer a prayer -- just as Japanese people have done for a thousand years. Then they walk back out and check their smartphones.

Tea ceremony -- *chanoyu* -- is still practiced throughout Japan. Some large corporations have tea rooms in their offices, where employees and clients share a quiet cup of matcha as a way of building trust and respect. The same careful, graceful movements that Sen no Rikyu perfected five hundred years ago are performed today in modern buildings of steel and glass.

Festivals -- *matsuri* -- still fill the streets throughout the year. During the summer Obon festival, neighborhoods come alive with drumming, dancing, paper lanterns, and food stalls. During the spring cherry blossom season, millions of people gather in parks to sit beneath the flowering trees, sharing food and conversation in a tradition called *hanami* that has continued, unbroken, for over a thousand years.

![People in traditional festival clothing carrying a portable shrine through streets decorated with lanterns](images/matsuri-festival.png)

Japanese cuisine, known as *washoku*, was recognized by UNESCO in 2013 as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. This is not just about sushi and ramen (though both are magnificent). Washoku is a philosophy of cooking that emphasizes fresh, seasonal ingredients, beautiful presentation, and a balance of flavors. A traditional Japanese meal is designed to be a feast for the eyes as well as the stomach -- each dish arranged on carefully chosen plates and bowls, the colors and textures reflecting the season.

The art of making things with extraordinary care -- what the Japanese call *monozukuri* -- runs through everything from a $50,000 handforged kitchen knife to a $2 convenience store rice ball. There is a pride in craftsmanship, a refusal to cut corners, that visitors to Japan notice almost immediately. Things *work*. Doors close smoothly. Packaging is precise. Even a simple bento box lunch from a train station is a small masterpiece of arrangement and flavor.

## Challenges Ahead

Modern Japan faces real challenges, and it would not be honest to pretend otherwise.

Japan has one of the oldest populations in the world. People in Japan live a very long time -- the average life expectancy is over eighty-four years, one of the highest on Earth. But the birth rate is low, meaning fewer babies are born each year. This means there are more and more elderly people and fewer and fewer young people to work and take care of them. It is one of the biggest issues Japan must solve in the coming decades.

Natural disasters remain a constant reality. Japan sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, just as it always has, and earthquakes, tsunamis, and volcanic eruptions are part of life. The devastating earthquake and tsunami of March 11, 2011, which struck the Tohoku region, killed nearly 20,000 people and triggered a nuclear disaster at the Fukushima power plant. But Japan's response -- the resilience of ordinary people helping their neighbors, the calm determination to rebuild -- showed the world the strength of the Japanese spirit.

Climate change brings new dangers: more powerful typhoons, rising sea levels, and extreme heat. Japan, with its long coastline and densely packed cities, is working hard to develop new technologies and strategies to face these challenges.

## The Spirit of Japan

There are two Japanese words that, together, capture something essential about this country's character.

The first is *ganbaru*. It means to persevere, to do your best, to keep going even when things are difficult. When a Japanese person says "*Ganbatte!*" to a friend facing a challenge, they are saying something like "You can do it! Give it everything you've got!" It is more than just encouragement -- it is a core value. The belief that effort matters, that trying your hardest is itself honorable even if you do not succeed, runs deep in Japanese culture.

The second is *omotenashi*. It means wholehearted hospitality -- anticipating what someone needs before they even know they need it, and providing it with warmth and grace. It is the hotel staff who remembers your name. The taxi driver who wears white gloves and opens your door. The shop owner who wraps your purchase as if it were a gift. Omotenashi is not about being fancy. It is about genuinely caring for the person in front of you.

These two ideas -- *ganbaru* and *omotenashi*, perseverance and hospitality -- are not new. They were there in the Jomon potter shaping clay by hand 10,000 years ago. They were there in the Nara monks building the Great Buddha. They were there in Lady Murasaki writing *The Tale of Genji* by candlelight. They were there in the samurai, the tea masters, the ukiyo-e artists, the Meiji students sailing across the ocean to learn, the survivors of Hiroshima folding paper cranes, and the engineers designing bullet trains that arrive within seconds of their scheduled time.

## Our Journey Ends -- and Begins

We started this book with fire -- the volcanic fire that built these islands out of the Pacific Ocean millions of years ago. We have traveled through thousands of years of history: through ancient forests where the Jomon people made the world's oldest pottery, through the courts of emperors and the castles of shoguns, through golden ages of art and terrible years of war, through destruction and rebuilding and transformation.

Japan's story is, in many ways, a story about what human beings can do when they combine creativity with determination, tradition with innovation, and beauty with resilience. It is a story that is still being written -- by the scientist in her laboratory, the chef in his kitchen, the child in her classroom, the artist at her drawing table.

If this book has done its job, then Japan is no longer just a place on a map to you. It is a living, breathing country with a history as dramatic as any novel and a present as fascinating as any science fiction story. And perhaps, someday, you will visit these islands of fire and emperors for yourself.

When you do, you will discover something that every visitor to Japan discovers: a country that is nothing like what you expected, and everything you hoped it would be.

> **Did You Know?** The Japanese word for "Japan" is *Nihon* (or *Nippon*), which means "origin of the sun." It refers to Japan's location east of mainland Asia -- from China's perspective, the sun rises from the direction of Japan. That is why Japan is often called "The Land of the Rising Sun." It is a name that has endured for over a thousand years, and it still fits perfectly -- because in Japan, something new is always dawning.
