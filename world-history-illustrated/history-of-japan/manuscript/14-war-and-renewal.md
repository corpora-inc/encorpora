# War and Renewal

This is the hardest chapter in our book. It is about a time when Japan, and the world, went through terrible darkness -- and then, slowly and painfully, found a way back to the light.

It is important to tell this story honestly, because the people who lived through it -- on all sides -- deserve to have their experiences remembered. And because understanding what happened is the only way to make sure it never happens again.

## The Path to War

In the 1930s, Japan was in trouble. The Great Depression had caused economic suffering around the world, and Japan was hit hard. Factories closed. Farmers could not sell their crops. Families went hungry. People were frightened and angry, and they looked for leaders who promised strength and certainty.

The military provided those leaders. Generals and admirals gained more and more power over the government, pushing aside the democratic politicians of the Taisho era. They argued that Japan needed to expand its empire to secure resources -- oil, iron, rubber -- that the island nation did not have on its own.

In 1931, the Japanese military invaded Manchuria, a region in northeastern China, and set up a puppet state there. In 1937, a full-scale war with China began. The fighting was brutal, and terrible things were done to civilians. Millions of Chinese people suffered enormously.

Japan signed alliances with Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy, forming what was called the Axis powers. Then, on December 7, 1941, Japanese planes attacked the American naval base at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii, bringing the United States into World War II.

The war that followed spread across the Pacific Ocean and Southeast Asia. Japan conquered vast territories -- the Philippines, Indonesia, Malaya, Burma, and many Pacific islands. But everywhere, the war brought tremendous suffering to the people living in those places. Families were torn apart. Cities were destroyed. Prisoners were treated with cruelty.

Inside Japan, ordinary people suffered too. Young men were sent to fight in faraway places, and many never came home. As the war dragged on, food became scarce. Children were evacuated from cities to the countryside. Freedoms disappeared under military rule.

## The Bombs That Changed Everything

By 1945, Japan was losing the war badly. American forces had recaptured most of the Pacific islands and were closing in on Japan itself. American bombers flew over Japanese cities night after night, dropping firebombs that turned wooden neighborhoods into infernos. A single raid on Tokyo in March 1945 killed an estimated 100,000 people and left a million homeless.

But the worst was yet to come.

On August 6, 1945, an American plane dropped a single bomb on the city of Hiroshima. It was a new kind of weapon -- an atomic bomb -- and it unleashed a power the world had never seen. In an instant, a blinding flash of light turned the center of the city to ash. A massive fireball rose into the sky, forming the shape of a mushroom. Buildings were flattened for kilometers in every direction. Tens of thousands of people were killed immediately. Tens of thousands more would die in the weeks and months that followed from burns and radiation sickness.

Three days later, on August 9, a second atomic bomb was dropped on the city of Nagasaki.

![The Hiroshima Peace Dome, the skeletal remains of a building that survived the atomic blast, now a memorial](images/hiroshima-peace-dome.png)

In Hiroshima, one building near the center of the explosion somehow remained partially standing -- a domed exhibition hall, its steel frame twisted and its walls shattered, but still upright. Today it is known as the Atomic Bomb Dome, or *Genbaku Dome*, and it has been preserved exactly as it was after the bombing. It stands as a silent witness, reminding every visitor of what happened and what must never happen again.

On August 15, 1945, Emperor Hirohito spoke to the Japanese people by radio -- the first time most of them had ever heard his voice. In formal, old-fashioned language, he told them that Japan was surrendering. Many people wept as they listened. The war was over.

> **Did You Know?** In Hiroshima, the shadows of people and objects were burned into stone and concrete by the flash of the bomb. These "atomic shadows" can still be seen at some locations in the city. They are a haunting reminder of the human lives that were there one moment and gone the next.

## Sadako and the Thousand Cranes

Among the survivors of Hiroshima was a two-year-old girl named Sadako Sasaki. She was at home, about a mile and a half from where the bomb exploded. She survived, and as she grew up, she seemed perfectly healthy. She loved running and was one of the fastest students in her school.

But when Sadako was twelve years old, she became very sick. Doctors told her she had leukemia -- a type of cancer -- caused by the radiation from the bomb that had fallen when she was just a toddler. Sadako was admitted to the hospital.

In Japan, there is an ancient legend that says if you fold one thousand origami cranes, you will be granted a wish. Sadako began folding paper cranes from any scrap of paper she could find -- medicine wrappers, gift paper, anything. Some of her cranes were tiny, no bigger than a fingernail.

![Children placing colorful paper cranes at the Peace Memorial in Hiroshima](images/peace-memorial-children.png)

Sadako folded more than a thousand cranes, but her wish for health was not granted. She died on October 25, 1955, at the age of twelve.

Her classmates, heartbroken, raised money to build a memorial in her honor. In 1958, a statue of Sadako was unveiled in Hiroshima's Peace Memorial Park. It shows her standing on top of a granite pedestal, arms outstretched, holding a golden crane above her head. At the base of the statue is a plaque that reads:

*"This is our cry. This is our prayer. Peace in the world."*

To this day, children from all over Japan -- and from countries around the world -- fold paper cranes and send them to Hiroshima, where they are displayed around Sadako's statue in great, colorful cascades. It is one of the most powerful symbols of peace on Earth.

## Rising from the Ashes

After the war ended, Japan lay in ruins. Most major cities had been heavily damaged by bombing. Millions of people were homeless. There was not enough food. The economy had collapsed. American forces, led by General Douglas MacArthur, occupied the country.

But even in the rubble, something remarkable was stirring.

![Workers rebuilding in postwar Japan, constructing new buildings among the ruins](images/postwar-rebuilding.png)

A new constitution was written in 1947. It was unlike any constitution the world had ever seen. Article 9 declared that Japan would forever renounce war as a way of settling disputes. The Japanese people, who had seen where militarism led, embraced this idea. They would build a new kind of nation -- one that proved its strength not through armies, but through hard work, education, and innovation.

Women gained the right to vote for the first time. Democracy was strengthened. Schools reopened. And the Japanese people threw themselves into rebuilding with an energy and determination that astonished the world.

What happened next is often called the "Japanese economic miracle." Starting from almost nothing, Japan rebuilt its industries and its cities. Workers poured their hearts into their jobs. Companies like Toyota, Sony, and Honda -- names you certainly know today -- started small and grew into giants. Japanese products, once dismissed as cheap copies, became famous for their quality and reliability.

By the 1960s, Japan was growing faster than any major economy in the world. The 1964 Tokyo Olympics -- the first Olympics ever held in Asia -- announced to the world that Japan was back. Visitors arrived to find a modern, bustling capital with a brand-new high-speed train, clean streets, and a people radiating pride and purpose.

By the 1980s, Japan had the second-largest economy on Earth, trailing only the United States. In just forty years, a nation that had been reduced to rubble had rebuilt itself into one of the world's most prosperous and technologically advanced countries.

## The Lesson

How was this possible? How does a country go from total devastation to global success in a single generation?

Part of the answer is practical: American aid, smart economic policies, a well-educated population, and a culture that valued hard work and cooperation.

But there is a deeper answer too. The Japanese people, having lived through the worst that war can bring, made a choice. They chose to channel their energy, their discipline, and their intelligence not into destruction, but into creation. They built cars instead of tanks. They made televisions instead of weapons. They designed bullet trains instead of battleships.

Japan's postwar story is not just a story about economics. It is a story about what happens when a nation looks honestly at its past, acknowledges the suffering that was caused, and decides to build a different kind of future.

Every year, on August 6 and August 9, bells ring in Hiroshima and Nagasaki. People gather in silence. They remember those who were lost. And they renew their promise: *Never again.*

The paper cranes keep arriving. The children keep folding them. And the prayer at the base of Sadako's statue still speaks for millions:

*This is our cry. This is our prayer. Peace in the world.*
