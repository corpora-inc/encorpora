# Emperors and Tombs

If you could fly high above Japan in the year 400 CE and look straight down, you would notice something strange on the flat plains near the coast. Scattered across the green landscape, surrounded by moats of still water, are enormous mounds of earth — not round like hills and not square like buildings.

They are shaped like **keyholes**.

Giant, perfect keyholes, as though some colossal being had dropped a key from the sky and left its imprint on the earth. Some of these mounds are small — the size of a house. But others are staggeringly huge. The largest one, in what is now the city of Sakai near Osaka, stretches nearly **500 meters long** and rises 35 meters high. It is surrounded by three moats and covered in trees. From the ground, it looks like a forested hill. Only from the air can you see its true shape.

These are the **kofun** — the great burial tombs of Japan's earliest rulers. And they are the reason this entire era, from about 250 CE to 538 CE, is called the **Kofun period**.

Who were the people powerful enough to command the building of such enormous monuments? Where did they come from? And how did they transform a land of scattered farming villages into the beginning of an empire?

The answers lie with a single, ambitious clan: the **Yamato**.

## The Rise of the Yamato

As the Yayoi period drew to a close and rice farming spread across Japan, the islands were not a single nation. They were a patchwork of hundreds of small kingdoms and chiefdoms, each ruled by a local leader. These chiefs controlled the rice paddies, the bronze and iron tools, and the people who worked the fields. They traded with each other, they competed, and sometimes they fought.

Chinese historians, writing in the third century CE, described Japan as a land of many small kingdoms. They mentioned one in particular — a place they called **Yamatai**, ruled by a queen named **Himiko**. According to the Chinese records, Himiko was a powerful shaman-queen who communicated with the spirits and ruled over a coalition of kingdoms. She lived in a grand palace, attended by a thousand servants, and rarely showed herself to her people.

Was Himiko a real person? Almost certainly yes — the Chinese had no reason to invent her. Was Yamatai the same as the Yamato clan that later unified Japan? Scholars have argued about this for over a century. The truth is that we simply do not have enough evidence to be sure.

What we do know is that by the late third century CE, one clan — the **Yamato** — began to grow more powerful than all the others. Based in the rich agricultural region of what is now Nara Prefecture in central Honshu, the Yamato controlled some of the best rice-growing land in Japan. They forged alliances with neighboring clans through marriage, trade, and — when necessary — force.

Over the next two centuries, the Yamato extended their influence across most of Honshu and into Kyushu and Shikoku. They did not conquer in the way that the Romans conquered — marching armies across the land and installing governors. The Yamato approach was subtler. They built a network of relationships, claiming authority over other clans while allowing local chiefs to keep much of their power. It was less an empire and more a family — a very large, very complicated, and sometimes very violent family.

And at the top of this family sat the Yamato ruler, who would eventually be known by a title that has survived to the present day: **Tenno** — the Emperor.

## Children of the Sun

The Yamato rulers did something extraordinarily clever. They claimed to be **descendants of Amaterasu**, the sun goddess herself.

Think about what that means. If you are the great-great-great-grandchild of the most powerful kami in all of creation — the goddess who lights the world — then your right to rule is not just political. It is *divine*. It comes from heaven. To challenge the emperor is to challenge the gods.

This claim of divine ancestry was eventually written down in two books compiled in the early 700s CE — the **Kojiki** and the **Nihon Shoki** — which told the story of how Amaterasu's grandson descended from heaven to rule Japan, and how his descendants became the imperial line.

According to these chronicles, the first human emperor was **Jimmu**, who supposedly founded the imperial dynasty in 660 BCE. Modern historians believe Jimmu is a legendary, not historical, figure — 660 BCE is far too early, and the details of his story are clearly mythological. But the *idea* of an unbroken imperial line stretching back into the age of the gods was immensely powerful.

It still is. The Japanese imperial family has continued in an unbroken line for at least 1,500 years — and by tradition, much longer. It is the **oldest hereditary monarchy in the world**.

> **Did You Know?** The current Emperor of Japan, Emperor Naruhito, is considered the 126th emperor in the traditional count. Whether or not the earliest emperors on the list actually existed, the imperial family has a documented, verifiable history stretching back to at least the sixth century CE — making it older than any other royal family on Earth.

## Building the Great Tombs

So how do we know the Yamato rulers were as powerful as they claimed to be? Because of what they left behind.

The kofun — those keyhole-shaped burial mounds — are monuments to power on a scale that is hard to imagine. Building the largest ones would have required **millions of hours of labor**. Workers dug moats, carried earth in baskets, piled it into precise shapes, covered the mounds with stones, and lined the moats with clay. The entire surface of the mound was originally covered in smooth river stones that gleamed in the sunlight like armor.

The largest kofun of all is the **Daisen Kofun**, traditionally attributed to Emperor Nintoku, who is said to have ruled in the early fifth century. Let's put its size in perspective:

- It is **486 meters long** — almost half a kilometer
- Its total area, including the moats, is larger than the **Great Pyramid of Giza**
- It is larger than the tomb of China's First Emperor (the one with the Terracotta Warriors)
- It took an estimated **15 years** and thousands of workers to build

And the Daisen Kofun is just the biggest. There are over **160,000 kofun** scattered across Japan. Most are small, the tombs of local chiefs and minor nobles. But hundreds are large enough to be genuinely impressive, and dozens are enormous.

Why the keyhole shape? No one is completely sure. The round part at the back may represent the sacred — the world of the spirits. The rectangular part at the front may represent the worldly — the realm of human power. Together, they might symbolize the emperor's role as a bridge between heaven and earth. Or the shape might have a practical purpose: the rectangular platform was where ceremonies were held, and the round mound was where the body was buried.

Whatever the reason, the keyhole kofun is unique to Japan. You will not find this shape anywhere else in the world.

## Guardians of Clay

Walk around the base of a large kofun, and you would have encountered something extraordinary: rows and rows of clay figures standing like silent soldiers on the terraced slopes of the mound. These are **haniwa**.

Haniwa (the word means "clay ring," from the cylinders that formed their bases) are hollow terra-cotta figures that were placed on and around the kofun. They come in an astonishing variety of forms. There are haniwa warriors in armor, holding swords and shields. Haniwa horses, saddled and ready to ride. Haniwa women in elaborate robes, their hair swept up in complex styles. Haniwa houses, boats, birds, dogs, deer, and even monkeys.

The faces of the haniwa are simple but remarkably expressive — wide eyes, round mouths, an air of gentle surprise or quiet dignity. They were not meant to be realistic portraits. They were meant to be **companions and protectors** for the dead ruler in the afterlife.

Some haniwa seem to be arranged in scenes — a procession of warriors, a group of musicians, a hunter with his falcon. Standing on the slopes of the kofun, they would have made the great tomb look like a miniature kingdom of clay — a court of silent figures attending their emperor for eternity.

> **Did You Know?** One famous haniwa shows a warrior in full armor with a tiny monkey sitting on his shoulder. Another shows a woman dancing with her arms raised joyfully. Haniwa may have been made for the dead, but they are full of life and personality. Today, haniwa are among the most beloved objects in Japanese art, and cute haniwa characters appear on everything from keychains to postage stamps.

## What Was Inside the Tombs

Inside the burial chambers of the great kofun, archaeologists have found treasures that tell us about the world of the Yamato rulers.

**Bronze mirrors** — dozens of them, some imported from China, others made in Japan — were placed beside the dead. Mirrors were sacred objects in ancient Japan, connected to Amaterasu (remember the mirror used to lure her from the cave). They may have symbolized the ruler's connection to the sun goddess.

**Iron swords and armor** show that these were rulers of a warrior society. Some swords are inscribed with Chinese characters — early evidence of writing in Japan.

**Magatama** — curved beads made of jade or jasper, shaped like a comma — were strung into necklaces and placed with the dead. The magatama is another of the three Imperial Regalia of Japan (along with the mirror and the sword), and its comma shape may represent the soul, a fetus, or a bear's claw — no one knows for sure.

**Gold jewelry**, **glass beads**, **horse tack**, and **ornate pottery** round out the picture of rulers who commanded vast resources and maintained connections across the sea.

## Connections Across the Sea

The Yamato rulers did not build their power in isolation. During the Kofun period, Japan had intense contacts with the Korean Peninsula and, through Korea, with China.

Korean immigrants — particularly from the kingdoms of **Baekje** and **Gaya** — brought transformative technologies to Japan. They brought advanced **iron-working** techniques, allowing the Japanese to produce better tools and weapons. They brought **horse-riding** culture — the great horse haniwa on the kofun tell us that horses became an important part of elite life during this period. They brought **silk weaving**, advanced **pottery** techniques, and, most importantly, they brought **writing**.

The Chinese writing system — thousands of complex characters, each representing a word or concept — arrived in Japan through Korean scholars and scribes. This was an enormous gift. Before writing, the Japanese could only pass down their history through spoken stories, which changed a little with each retelling. With writing, they could record laws, keep accounts, send messages across long distances, and eventually write down the myths and histories that had been told around fires for generations.

But adopting Chinese characters for the Japanese language was not easy. Chinese and Japanese are completely unrelated languages with different grammar, different sounds, and different structures. Imagine trying to write English using only Egyptian hieroglyphs, and you begin to understand the challenge. The Japanese would spend centuries wrestling with this problem, eventually creating their own writing systems — **hiragana** and **katakana** — from simplified Chinese characters. But that story comes later.

## The Shape of Early Japan

By the late Kofun period, the Yamato rulers had created something that was beginning to look like a state. They had a central court, a network of allied clans, a divine origin story, a writing system (borrowed from China), and a military force that included armored warriors on horseback.

But the Yamato state was still fragile. The emperor's power depended on keeping dozens of powerful clans in line, and those clans — the **Soga**, the **Mononobe**, the **Nakatomi**, and others — were constantly jockeying for influence at court. The strongest clans married their daughters to emperors, placed their allies in key positions, and sometimes resorted to assassination when diplomacy failed.

The most powerful of these clans, the **Soga**, would soon push Japan toward its next great transformation. They championed a new religion that had traveled across Asia along the Silk Road, jumped from India to China to Korea, and was now knocking on Japan's door.

That religion was **Buddhism**.

But Buddhism's arrival would not be smooth. It would spark a bitter conflict between those who wanted to embrace the new faith and those who wanted to protect the old ways of the kami. A prince would arise who believed Japan could hold both — and his vision would shape the country forever.

> **Did You Know?** The Daisen Kofun and several other great tombs are still officially considered imperial property and are managed by the Imperial Household Agency. Because of this, most of the largest kofun have never been fully excavated by archaeologists. What treasures and secrets might still lie inside them? We may never know — at least not until the rules change.

## A Legacy in Earth and Clay

The Kofun period may not be as famous as the age of samurai or the gleaming world of modern Tokyo. But it was the time when the foundation of Japan was laid.

During these three centuries, a patchwork of quarreling chiefdoms became something larger — a kingdom with a divine ruler, a written language, connections to the wider world, and a story about itself that stretched all the way back to the sun goddess and the age of the gods.

The great keyhole tombs still rise from the plains of central Japan, covered now in forests and surrounded by modern cities. From the ground, they look like wooded hills. But from above, their shapes are unmistakable — silent monuments to rulers who believed they were descended from the sun.

The haniwa still stand in museums — warriors and horses and dancing women, their hollow eyes gazing across the centuries, guarding their emperors in a sleep that has lasted more than a thousand years.

And the imperial line that began in those shadowy centuries continues today, a thread of continuity stretching from the age of myth to the age of the internet.

The Yamato built their power from rice and iron, from alliances and ambition, from myths and tombs. They looked to the mainland and borrowed what they could use. They looked to the heavens and claimed the sun as their ancestor.

Now they were ready for something even bigger. They were ready to build a capital.
