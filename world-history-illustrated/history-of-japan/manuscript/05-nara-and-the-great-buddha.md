# Nara and the Great Buddha

A young monk stands on the deck of a wooden ship, gripping the railing as waves crash against the hull. Salt spray stings his face. The coastline of Japan is just appearing through the mist — dark mountains rising from the sea, topped by clouds.

In his arms, wrapped carefully in silk cloth, he carries a small bronze statue of the Buddha — a figure sitting cross-legged, eyes half-closed, hands resting peacefully in his lap, a gentle smile on his lips.

The monk has traveled for months. From a monastery in Korea, across the treacherous strait, through storms and seasickness and fear. He carries with him not just a statue, but an entire way of understanding the world — a philosophy of compassion, wisdom, and the end of suffering that began with a prince sitting under a tree in India over a thousand years earlier.

He does not know it yet, but the small bronze figure in his arms is about to change Japan forever.

## A Religion Travels East

**Buddhism** was born in India around 500 BCE, when a prince named Siddhartha Gautama left his palace, saw the suffering of the world, and spent years meditating until he achieved **enlightenment** — a state of perfect understanding and inner peace. He became the **Buddha**, which means "the awakened one," and spent the rest of his life teaching others how to find that same peace.

The Buddha's teachings were simple but profound: all life involves suffering. Suffering comes from desire and attachment. There is a way to end suffering. That way is the **Eightfold Path** — a guide to living with wisdom, kindness, and mindfulness.

After the Buddha's death, his teachings spread across Asia like ripples in a pond. From India, Buddhism traveled along the Silk Road to China, where it arrived around the first century CE. Chinese scholars translated the Buddhist scriptures from Sanskrit into Chinese. Chinese artists created new styles of Buddhist art. Chinese monks built great monasteries and temples.

From China, Buddhism crossed the sea to Korea. And from Korea, in the middle of the sixth century CE — around the year **552** — it finally reached Japan.

The arrival of Buddhism set off a political earthquake at the Yamato court.

## The Great Debate

When Buddhist statues, scriptures, and monks first arrived at the Japanese court, the reaction was mixed. Some powerful clans were fascinated. Others were horrified.

The **Soga clan** — wealthy, politically ambitious, and deeply connected to Korean immigrants who had brought Buddhism with them — championed the new religion enthusiastically. They saw in Buddhism not just spiritual truth but political opportunity. If Japan adopted this sophisticated, international religion, it could take its place among the great civilizations of Asia.

The **Mononobe** and **Nakatomi clans** — traditional guardians of Shinto rituals and the worship of the kami — were fiercely opposed. Worshipping a foreign god, they warned, would anger the native kami and bring disaster upon Japan. The kami had protected Japan since the beginning. To abandon them for a foreign faith was dangerous and disloyal.

The debate turned violent. The Soga built a small Buddhist temple, and a plague broke out. The Mononobe blamed Buddhism, destroyed the temple, and threw the Buddhist statues into a canal. Then the plague got worse. The Soga said the kami were *not* angry — the plague was punishment for destroying the temple.

Back and forth it went, until the conflict erupted into open war. In **587 CE**, the Soga clan defeated the Mononobe in battle, and their champion — a young prince who would become one of the most important figures in Japanese history — rose to power.

His name was **Prince Shotoku**.

## Prince Shotoku's Vision

**Prince Shotoku** (574-622 CE) was not an emperor, but he was the next best thing — a regent who governed on behalf of his aunt, Empress Suiko. He was, by all accounts, brilliant. The ancient chronicles say he could listen to ten people speaking at once and understand every word. That is almost certainly an exaggeration, but it captures the reputation of a man whose mind seemed to have no limits.

Shotoku believed Japan needed to modernize — and he looked to China for the blueprint. The great Chinese empire of the Sui dynasty was the most advanced civilization in the world at the time. It had a centralized government, a professional bureaucracy, a written legal code, magnificent architecture, and Buddhism as a state-supported religion.

Shotoku wanted all of this for Japan.

He sent **embassies to China** — enormous delegations of scholars, monks, and officials who made the dangerous sea crossing to study Chinese government, religion, art, and technology firsthand. These missions brought back an avalanche of knowledge: Chinese-style government systems, Confucian philosophy, Buddhist scriptures, architectural techniques, calendar systems, and medical knowledge.

Shotoku also wrote the **Seventeen-Article Constitution** (604 CE) — not a constitution in the modern sense, but a set of moral principles for government officials. Its very first article declared: "Harmony is to be valued." This emphasis on *wa* — harmony — would echo through Japanese culture for the next fourteen hundred years.

And Shotoku built temples. The most famous, **Horyu-ji**, still stands near the city of Nara. Built around 607 CE, it is the **oldest surviving wooden structure in the world** — a building that has endured earthquakes, typhoons, and fires for nearly 1,400 years. Its elegant pagoda, rising in five tiers against the sky, is a testament to both Japanese craftsmanship and the Buddhist faith that inspired it.

> **Did You Know?** Prince Shotoku is so revered in Japan that his face appeared on Japanese banknotes for decades. He was on the 10,000-yen note — the most valuable bill in everyday use — from 1958 to 1986. Imagine a figure from the year 600 being famous enough to put on your money!

## Building the First Capital

In the decades after Shotoku, Japan continued its transformation. The Yamato court adopted Chinese-style government reforms, creating a centralized bureaucracy with ranks, titles, and laws. But there was a problem: Japan did not have a proper capital city.

The early emperors had moved their court every time a new ruler took the throne — partly because of Shinto beliefs about death pollution (a place where an emperor had died was considered impure) and partly because each new emperor wanted to demonstrate power by building a new palace. This constant moving was expensive and chaotic.

Looking at China's magnificent capital of Chang'an — the largest city in the world, laid out in a perfect grid of wide boulevards and grand palaces — the Japanese decided it was time to build a permanent capital of their own.

In **710 CE**, the Empress Genmei established Japan's first true permanent capital at **Nara**, in a flat basin surrounded by gentle green hills in the heart of the Yamato region.

Nara was designed as a miniature version of Chang'an. Its streets were laid out in a precise **grid pattern** — running north-south and east-west in perfectly straight lines, dividing the city into neat rectangular blocks. At the northern end stood the imperial palace. Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines filled the city. Government offices, markets, and the homes of nobles lined the broad avenues.

At its peak, Nara had a population of about **200,000 people** — enormous for eighth-century Japan. It was a cosmopolitan city, too. Korean scholars, Chinese monks, and even travelers from as far as Persia and India walked its streets. The Silk Road, which connected China to the Mediterranean world, extended its influence all the way to this small city in the Japanese hills.

## The Great Buddha

Now we come to the moment that still takes visitors' breath away, more than 1,200 years later.

In the year **743 CE**, Emperor Shomu issued an extraordinary decree. He commanded the construction of a **giant bronze statue of the Buddha** — a figure so large, so magnificent, that it would demonstrate Japan's devotion to Buddhism and protect the nation from the epidemics, famines, and natural disasters that had plagued the country.

Building it took **nine years**.

Think about what that means. Nine years of work by thousands of people — metalworkers, carpenters, sculptors, laborers, monks — all dedicated to creating a single statue. The bronze alone required more than **500 tons of metal** and over **400 pounds of gold** for the gilding. It nearly bankrupted the treasury.

The result was the **Daibutsu** — the Great Buddha of Nara.

The finished statue sits inside **Todai-ji** (the "Great Eastern Temple"), and both the statue and the building are staggering in scale.

The Great Buddha:
- Sits **15 meters tall** — about the height of a five-story building
- Has a face that is **5 meters long** — taller than most adults
- Has eyes that are over **1 meter wide**
- Has a hand with a palm large enough for a person to lie down on
- Weighs approximately **500 tons**

The wooden hall that houses the Buddha, called the **Daibutsuden**, is one of the **largest wooden buildings in the world**. Even the current building — which is actually a 1709 reconstruction and is only about two-thirds the size of the original — is 57 meters wide and 50 meters tall.

When the Daibutsu was dedicated in a grand ceremony in **752 CE**, thousands of people gathered. Monks chanted. Musicians from across Asia performed. An Indian monk was invited to "open the eyes" of the Buddha — painting in the pupils to symbolically bring the statue to life. A long silk cord stretched from the Buddha's brush all the way out to the assembled crowd, so that everyone who held it would share in the blessing.

It was the greatest public spectacle Japan had ever seen.

![The Great Buddha of Nara (Daibutsu) sitting in meditation inside the massive wooden hall of Todai-ji, with visitors looking tiny at its base](images/great-buddha-nara.png)

> **Did You Know?** The Great Buddha has been damaged and repaired many times over the centuries. Its current head is from the 1600s, and parts of its body have been repaired in different eras. But the core of the statue — the lotus pedestal and lower body — dates back to the original eighth-century construction. The building that houses it has burned down and been rebuilt twice. Each time, the people of Japan raised the funds and did the work to restore it.

## The Friendly Deer of Nara

If you visit Nara today, you will notice something unusual before you even reach the temples. The parks and streets around Todai-ji are filled with **deer** — more than a thousand of them — and they walk right up to you, bowing politely (really!) in exchange for special deer crackers called *shika senbei* that vendors sell throughout the park.

These are **sika deer**, and they have been living in Nara for over a thousand years. According to Shinto legend, a kami rode into Nara on the back of a white deer, and ever since, the deer have been considered sacred messengers of the gods. For centuries, killing a deer in Nara was punishable by death.

Today, the deer are designated as **national natural treasures**. They wander freely among ancient temples, stroll across busy roads (cars stop for them), and have learned that bowing to tourists produces snacks. They are one of Japan's most beloved attractions — a living link between the modern city and the ancient capital.

## Buddhism and Shinto: Clash and Harmony

When Buddhism first arrived, many people feared it would replace Shinto. The two systems seem very different. Shinto focuses on purity, nature, and the kami of this world. Buddhism focuses on enlightenment, the end of suffering, and liberation from the cycle of rebirth.

But something remarkable happened in Japan. Instead of one replacing the other, **Buddhism and Shinto blended together**.

The Japanese developed a concept called **shinbutsu-shugo** — the "fusion of kami and buddhas." According to this idea, the kami were not enemies of the Buddha. They were actually **local manifestations** of Buddhist deities. Amaterasu was not a rival to the Buddha — she was a Japanese form of a cosmic Buddhist truth. The kami and the buddhas were different faces of the same reality.

In practice, this meant that Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines were often built side by side — or even inside each other. A Shinto shrine might sit within the grounds of a Buddhist temple. A Buddhist statue might be placed inside a Shinto shrine. Monks chanted Buddhist sutras to honor the kami, and Shinto priests blessed Buddhist ceremonies.

This ability to hold two different traditions together — to say "both, not either" — is one of the most distinctive features of Japanese culture. Rather than choosing one truth and rejecting the other, the Japanese embraced both, finding harmony where others might have found conflict.

> **Did You Know?** The Todai-ji complex was so large and powerful that it had its own army of warrior monks. These **sohei** (monk-soldiers) would become a major force in Japanese politics for centuries, sometimes marching on the capital to demand that the government do what the temples wanted. Religion and politics were deeply entangled in ancient Japan.

## The Nara Period's Legacy

The Nara period was brief — only 74 years, from 710 to 784 CE — but its impact was enormous.

During these decades, Japan:

- Built its **first permanent capital** modeled on the greatest city in Asia
- Created the **Great Buddha**, one of the largest bronze statues in the world
- Compiled the **Kojiki** and **Nihon Shoki**, the foundational chronicles of Japanese mythology and history
- Assembled the **Man'yoshu**, the oldest surviving collection of Japanese poetry — over 4,500 poems by everyone from emperors to common soldiers, farmers, and frontier guards
- Established a **centralized government** with written laws, tax systems, and provincial administrators
- Built a network of **Buddhist temples** across the country, each one a center of learning, art, and community life

But the Nara period also revealed a danger. The great Buddhist temples and monasteries had become enormously wealthy and politically powerful. Monks and abbots influenced court decisions, and the temples owned vast estates. The emperor found himself hemmed in by religious institutions that were supposed to serve the state but were beginning to rival it.

In **784 CE**, Emperor Kanmu decided to move the capital away from Nara — away from the shadow of the great temples — to a new city where the emperor could rule without the monks looking over his shoulder.

That new capital would be called **Heian-kyo** — the "Capital of Peace and Tranquility."

Today, we know it as **Kyoto**.

And the age that was about to begin there — the Heian period — would be one of the most elegant, artistic, and culturally extraordinary eras in all of human history.

## Standing Before the Buddha

If you ever have the chance to visit Nara, do this: walk through the great wooden gate of Todai-ji, cross the courtyard, and step inside the Daibutsuden. Let your eyes adjust to the dim light.

And then look up.

The Great Buddha sits before you in eternal meditation, one hand raised in a gesture of reassurance, the other resting open on his knee. His half-closed eyes gaze down at you with an expression of infinite calm. His face, five meters tall, seems both ancient and timeless — not quite smiling, but radiating a peace so deep you can almost feel it in the air.

Around his head, a golden halo bristles with smaller Buddha figures. The lotus petals of his seat are carved with entire universes.

You are standing where people have stood for thirteen centuries, looking up at the same face, feeling the same sense of awe. Emperors and peasants, monks and warriors, children and grandparents — all of them have stood in this spot and felt themselves grow small in the presence of something vast.

The Jomon people shaped clay with their hands. The Yayoi grew rice in their paddies. The Yamato built tombs as big as mountains. And now, the people of Nara had built a Buddha tall enough to touch the sky — a monument not to war or conquest, but to compassion and the hope that suffering could be overcome.

The islands of fire and water were becoming something extraordinary.
