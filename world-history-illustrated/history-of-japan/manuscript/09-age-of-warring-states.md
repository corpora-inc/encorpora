# Age of Warring States

On a September afternoon in 1543, a Chinese trading ship drifted off course in a storm and washed up on a tiny island at the southern tip of Japan. On board were three Portuguese merchants — the first Europeans most Japanese had ever seen. The locals stared at these strange men with their big noses, curly hair, and odd clothes.

But it was not the men who changed Japan. It was what they carried.

The Portuguese had **guns** — matchlock muskets called *tanegashima*, named after the island where they landed. A local lord bought two of them for an enormous sum. Within ten years, Japanese blacksmiths had figured out how to make their own. Within fifty years, Japan had more guns than any country in Europe.

And in a land already torn apart by war, that changed absolutely everything.

## A Country at War with Itself

The period from 1467 to 1615 is called the **Sengoku Jidai** — the "Age of Warring States." It is one of the most dramatic, violent, and fascinating periods in all of world history.

Imagine Japan as a puzzle that has been smashed apart. Where there had once been a single government (the Ashikaga shogunate), there were now dozens of independent **daimyo** — powerful warlords, each controlling their own territory. Each daimyo had an army, a castle, and a burning ambition to conquer his neighbors.

It was a bit like if every state in a country suddenly declared independence and started fighting every other state. Alliances formed and shattered. Friends became enemies overnight. A clever peasant could rise to become a general; a powerful lord could lose everything in a single battle.

This was a time of constant danger — but also of extraordinary opportunity. In most periods of Japanese history, your birth determined your future. If you were born a farmer, you died a farmer. But during the Sengoku period, the old rules broke down. Talent and ruthlessness mattered more than family name.

The Japanese had a phrase for it: *gekokujo* — "the low overcome the high." Servants overthrew masters. Foot soldiers became generals. And one man, born the son of a minor warrior, would rise to begin the unification of all Japan.

But first, let's talk about the shadows.

## Ninja: The Truth Behind the Legend

![A ninja moving through shadows, dressed in dark clothing, carrying tools of espionage](images/ninja-shadow.png)

You have probably seen ninja in movies and cartoons — black-clad figures who can vanish in puffs of smoke, walk on water, throw deadly stars, and defeat entire armies single-handedly.

The real ninja were far more interesting than the movie version — though perhaps less flashy.

The proper name for a ninja is **shinobi**, which means "one who endures" or "one who steals away." They came mostly from the mountainous provinces of **Iga** and **Koga** in central Japan, rugged regions where independent-minded villagers had developed sophisticated skills in espionage, sabotage, and guerrilla warfare.

Shinobi were, above all, **spies and scouts**. In an age when information meant the difference between victory and death, a daimyo needed to know what his enemies were planning. Shinobi would sneak into enemy castles disguised as merchants, monks, or wandering performers. They would observe troop movements, map defenses, and slip away to report what they had learned.

They were also masters of **sabotage**. Before a battle, shinobi might sneak into an enemy camp and set fires, poison water supplies, or spread false rumors to cause panic. They were experts in using the landscape — hiding in forests, swimming through moats, climbing walls using hooks and ropes.

Did they wear black? Sometimes, on night missions. But more often, they wore whatever would help them blend in — a farmer's clothes, a merchant's robe, a priest's outfit. The whole point was to be invisible, and a person dressed head-to-toe in black stands out almost anywhere except in total darkness.

Did they throw shuriken (throwing stars)? Occasionally, but these were mainly used as distractions, not as primary weapons. A shinobi's most important tools were patience, cunning, and knowledge. They studied weather patterns, herbal medicines (including poisons), lock-picking, explosives, and the psychology of fear.

> **Did You Know?** One of the most famous ninja manuals is the *Bansenshukai*, written in 1676. It is not full of mystical secrets — it reads more like a military intelligence handbook, with chapters on espionage techniques, weather forecasting, the psychology of deception, and even advice on choosing the right type of sandal for silent movement. The real ninja art was the art of knowing things.

## Oda Nobunaga: The First Unifier

![Portrait of Oda Nobunaga, a fierce-looking warlord in ornate armor](images/oda-nobunaga-portrait.png)

In 1534, in the small province of Owari (modern-day Nagoya area), a boy was born who would be called the most dangerous man in Japan.

His name was **Oda Nobunaga**.

As a teenager, Nobunaga was wild and unpredictable. He wore strange clothes, danced in the streets, and ignored every rule of polite behavior. His own family called him "The Fool of Owari." When his father died and Nobunaga inherited leadership of the clan, many of his own retainers rebelled, certain that this bizarre young man would lead them to ruin.

They were spectacularly wrong.

Nobunaga was not a fool. He was a genius — cold, calculating, and absolutely ruthless. He crushed the rebels within his own clan, then turned his attention outward. In 1560, at the Battle of Okehazama, he faced an army of 25,000 soldiers with only about 3,000 of his own. Any sensible commander would have surrendered or fled.

Nobunaga attacked.

During a rainstorm, he led his tiny force in a surprise assault on the enemy commander's camp. The enemy general, Imagawa Yoshimoto, was celebrating his expected victory with sake and song when Nobunaga's warriors came screaming out of the rain. Yoshimoto was killed. His massive army, suddenly leaderless, fell apart.

It was one of the most stunning upsets in military history, and it made Nobunaga famous across Japan.

## Guns Change Everything

What made Nobunaga truly revolutionary was not just his courage — it was his willingness to throw away tradition and embrace new ideas.

When those Portuguese guns arrived in 1543, most samurai dismissed them. Firearms were loud, slow to reload, unreliable in rain, and — worst of all — *dishonorable*. A common foot soldier with a gun could kill a great samurai warrior who had trained his whole life. That did not seem fair.

Nobunaga did not care about fair. He cared about winning.

He bought thousands of guns and trained his foot soldiers — not high-born samurai, but ordinary peasants called **ashigaru** — to use them in organized lines. At the **Battle of Nagashino** in 1575, Nobunaga faced the legendary cavalry of the Takeda clan, some of the finest mounted warriors in Japan.

Nobunaga positioned his gunners behind wooden palisades and organized them into **rotating volleys** — while one line fired, the next line reloaded, and a third line prepared. This created an almost continuous wall of gunfire. When the Takeda cavalry charged, they rode straight into a storm of lead.

The result was devastating. The flower of Takeda's samurai were cut down. The age-old tradition of the mounted warrior charging gloriously into battle was, in a single afternoon, blown to pieces by peasants with guns.

> **Did You Know?** By the 1580s, Japan had more firearms than any country in Europe. Japanese blacksmiths were not simply copying Portuguese guns — they were improving them, making them lighter, more accurate, and more suited to Japanese conditions. Japan's gun-making skills were world-class.

## Fire and Faith

Nobunaga was not just a military innovator. He was a builder, a reformer, and — when he felt threatened — utterly merciless.

He built magnificent castles, including the stunning **Azuchi Castle** on the shores of Lake Biwa, which was considered the most beautiful building in Japan. It rose seven stories high and was decorated with gold leaf, painted screens, and carvings of dragons. It was the first castle designed not just for defense but to display power and culture.

He opened free markets, abolished toll barriers that slowed trade, and built roads to connect his growing territory. Merchants prospered under his rule, and towns grew rapidly.

But Nobunaga also had a dark side. When the powerful Buddhist monasteries on **Mount Hiei** opposed him, he burned them to the ground and killed thousands of monks, women, and children. When a community of Buddhist rebels held out against him for eleven years, he eventually destroyed them without mercy. These acts horrified even his own allies.

He was also fascinated by the **European missionaries** who had followed the Portuguese traders to Japan. Jesuit priests arrived bringing Christianity, Western science, clocks, maps, eyeglasses, and oil paintings. Nobunaga did not become Christian himself, but he enjoyed the company of the missionaries and gave them permission to preach. He may have seen Christianity as a useful counterweight to the powerful Buddhist temples he was trying to crush.

## The Stranger from the West

Among the many foreigners who came to Japan during this period, one stands out. In 1579, a tall African man arrived in Japan. His name — or the name the Japanese gave him — was **Yasuke**. He may have come from Mozambique or Ethiopia, and he traveled to Japan with an Italian Jesuit missionary.

When Yasuke was presented to Nobunaga, the warlord was fascinated. He reportedly did not believe Yasuke's skin was naturally dark and ordered him scrubbed with water to see if the color would wash off. When it did not, Nobunaga was impressed. He made Yasuke a samurai in his personal service — one of the first foreign-born samurai in recorded history.

Yasuke served Nobunaga for about a year, carried his weapons, and reportedly fought beside him. His story shows how the chaos of the Sengoku period could open doors that would have been firmly shut in more orderly times.

## Betrayal at Honno-ji

By 1582, Nobunaga controlled about a third of Japan and seemed unstoppable. He was on the verge of conquering the rest. But on the night of June 21, 1582, one of his own generals — **Akechi Mitsuhide** — turned traitor.

While Nobunaga rested at a temple called Honno-ji in Kyoto with only a small guard, Mitsuhide attacked with 13,000 soldiers. Nobunaga, surrounded and wounded, retreated into the burning temple. According to legend, he composed a final poem, then took his own life as the flames closed in. His body was never found.

Why did Mitsuhide betray him? No one knows for certain. Perhaps he was angry at a public insult. Perhaps he was ambitious. Perhaps he simply saw his chance and took it. Whatever the reason, the man who had come closest to unifying Japan was dead at forty-eight.

But Nobunaga's work was not finished. Two of his most brilliant followers would complete what he had begun — and the next chapter tells how a peasant and a patient old fox together forged a nation.

> **Did You Know?** There is a famous Japanese saying about the three great unifiers and a bird that will not sing: "If the bird will not sing, kill it" (Nobunaga). "If the bird will not sing, make it want to sing" (Hideyoshi). "If the bird will not sing, wait for it to sing" (Ieyasu). Each answer perfectly captures the personality of these three extraordinary men.
