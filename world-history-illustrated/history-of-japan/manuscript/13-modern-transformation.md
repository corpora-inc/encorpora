# The Modern Transformation

On October 14, 1872, a crowd of thousands gathered at Shimbashi Station in Tokyo, buzzing with excitement and nervousness in equal measure. Many of the men wore traditional kimono; others, following the new fashion, wore Western-style suits that still looked a little stiff and uncomfortable on them. Women held parasols against the autumn sun. Children wriggled through the crowd to get a better look.

Then they heard it -- a long, shrill whistle and a deep, rumbling *chuff-chuff-chuff* as a steam locomotive, painted glossy black with a bright red cowcatcher, rolled into the station pulling a line of passenger cars. The crowd gasped. Some people backed away. A few of the older men shook their heads in disbelief.

Just five years earlier, there had not been a single railroad track in all of Japan. Samurai had still worn swords in the streets. The shogun had still ruled from his castle. Now here was a steam train, built with help from British engineers, about to carry passengers from Tokyo to the port city of Yokohama -- a journey of twenty-nine kilometers that would take just fifty-three minutes.

![Japan's first steam train at Shimbashi Station, with crowds of curious onlookers in both kimono and Western dress](images/first-japanese-railroad.png)

The Meiji Emperor himself rode the train that day. As the locomotive picked up speed and the landscape blurred past the windows, the message was clear to everyone watching: Japan was racing into the future.

## Everything Changes

The speed of Japan's transformation in the Meiji era (1868-1912) is one of the most astonishing stories in all of human history. Imagine if your grandparents had grown up in a world of horse-drawn carts, candlelight, and handwritten letters -- and then you grew up with cars, electric lights, telephones, and the internet. That is roughly what happened in Japan, except it all took place in about thirty years.

The Meiji leaders understood that becoming a modern nation was not just about buying Western machines. They had to rebuild their entire society from the ground up. And so they did -- with a determination that still takes your breath away.

**The samurai class was abolished.** In 1876, samurai were forbidden from carrying swords in public. For a class of warriors that had defined Japan for seven hundred years, this was devastating. Many samurai were furious. In 1877, a group of them launched one last great rebellion, led by the legendary Saigo Takamori in the southern region of Satsuma. They charged into battle with swords and old-fashioned rifles. They were brave, but they faced a modern conscript army -- an army of ordinary farmers and merchants trained with modern weapons. The rebellion was crushed. The age of the samurai was truly over.

**A modern army and navy were built.** Japan adopted the Prussian military model for its army and the British model for its navy. Every young man was required to serve in the military -- not just samurai, but farmers, fishermen, and shopkeepers. For the first time, being a soldier was not about what family you were born into. It was about serving your country.

**The government was reorganized.** In 1889, Japan adopted the Meiji Constitution, creating a parliament called the *Diet* with two houses -- an elected House of Representatives and an appointed House of Peers. It was not a full democracy by modern standards -- the emperor still held great power, and not everyone could vote -- but it was a giant step forward. Japan became the first country in Asia to have a constitution and a parliament.

**The legal system was modernized.** Japan wrote new legal codes based on French and German models, which helped convince Western nations to eventually renegotiate those humiliating "unequal treaties" from the 1850s and 1860s. By 1899, Japan had won back full legal control over its own territory. The treaties were gone.

> **Did You Know?** When the samurai class was abolished, the government paid them a one-time settlement. Some samurai invested the money wisely and became successful businesspeople. Others spent it quickly and fell into poverty. A few became teachers, police officers, or journalists. The transition was painful, but many former samurai channeled their discipline and education into building the new Japan.

## Every Child Goes to School

Of all the changes the Meiji government made, perhaps the most important was this: they built schools, and they sent every child to them.

![Children in a Meiji-era classroom sitting at wooden desks, a teacher writing on a blackboard](images/meiji-schoolchildren.png)

In 1872, the government declared that all children -- boys *and* girls -- must attend school. This was revolutionary. In most countries at that time, education was only for the wealthy or for boys. But the Meiji leaders believed that a modern nation needed an educated population. Every citizen needed to be able to read, write, and do mathematics.

The government built thousands of schools across the country, from the biggest cities to the tiniest mountain villages. They trained teachers, wrote textbooks, and made attendance mandatory. At first, some farming families resisted. They needed their children to help in the rice paddies, not sit in a classroom. But the government was relentless, and within a generation, Japan had one of the highest literacy rates in the world.

The schools taught reading, writing, mathematics, science, history, music, and physical education. They also taught moral education -- lessons about duty, respect, hard work, and loyalty. Students learned about the wider world: the geography of other continents, the discoveries of Western scientists, the history of ancient Greece and Rome.

Japanese students were also sent abroad in large numbers. Young men and women traveled to the United States, Britain, Germany, and France to study engineering, medicine, law, art, and military science. They lived in foreign boarding houses, struggled with unfamiliar languages, and soaked up knowledge like sponges. When they returned home, they brought that knowledge with them and used it to build the new Japan.

One of these students was Tsuda Umeko, who was sent to the United States at the age of just six as part of a group of female students in 1871. She lived with an American family, learned English, and eventually returned to Japan to found a women's college. She believed passionately that Japanese women deserved the same education as men -- an idea that was radical at the time but helped shape Japan's future.

> **Did You Know?** Tsuda Umeko's face now appears on Japan's 5,000-yen banknote, introduced in 2024. She is one of the first women to appear on Japanese paper currency, honoring her role in advancing women's education.

## Western Technology, Japanese Spirit

The Meiji era had a famous slogan: *wakon yosai* -- "Western technology, Japanese spirit." This phrase captured something essential about how Japan approached its transformation. The goal was never to *become* Western. The goal was to *learn from* the West while keeping the heart and soul of what it meant to be Japanese.

So Japan built railroads, but the train stations often had traditional Japanese architectural touches. Officials wore Western suits to the office but changed into kimono when they came home. Schools taught Western science alongside Japanese calligraphy and moral education. The army marched in Western-style uniforms but carried the spirit of the old samurai code.

This balancing act was not always easy, and not everyone agreed on where to draw the line. Some people wanted Japan to embrace everything Western -- clothing, food, music, architecture, everything. Others felt Japan was losing its identity and abandoning what made it special. The tension between tradition and modernity, between being Japanese and being "modern," became one of the defining questions of the era. In many ways, it is a question Japan is still answering today.

The changes extended to daily life in ways large and small. The traditional lunar calendar was replaced with the Western calendar. The Japanese began eating beef -- something that had been rare for centuries due to Buddhist dietary customs. Western-style brick buildings began appearing alongside traditional wooden ones, especially after the great fires that regularly swept through cities made of wood and paper.

In 1890, the first telephone exchange opened in Tokyo. By 1895, Japan had a functioning postal system, telegraph network, and gas lighting in major cities. Factories sprang up, producing textiles, steel, ships, and weapons. Japan was industrializing at a pace that stunned the rest of the world.

## A New Power in Asia

Japan's transformation was not just internal. The newly modern nation was also stepping onto the world stage in dramatic fashion.

In 1894-1895, Japan fought and won a war against China -- the massive Qing Empire that had dominated East Asia for centuries. The world was shocked. How had this small island nation defeated the great Chinese empire? The answer was modernization. Japan's modern navy, armed with steel warships and trained in Western tactics, overwhelmed China's outdated fleet.

Then, in 1904-1905, Japan did something even more astonishing: it fought and defeated Russia, one of the great European powers. The Russo-Japanese War was the first time in modern history that an Asian nation had defeated a European one. The news sent shockwaves around the world. In colonized nations across Asia and Africa, people took notice. If Japan could stand up to a European power, perhaps they could too.

By 1910, Japan had become a colonial power itself, having annexed Korea -- a painful chapter that would cause deep suffering and lasting bitterness. Japan's rapid rise showed both the tremendous possibilities and the serious dangers of unchecked military ambition.

## The Taisho Interlude

When the Meiji Emperor died in 1912, Japan entered the Taisho era (1912-1926), named after the new emperor. This was a brief but hopeful period sometimes called "Taisho Democracy."

During the Taisho years, Japan experienced a flowering of democratic ideas. Political parties grew stronger. More people gained the right to vote -- by 1925, all men over twenty-five could vote (women would have to wait until after World War II). Newspapers and magazines multiplied, and people debated politics freely. Labor unions formed. Artists and writers experimented with new styles. Jazz music drifted through the dance halls of Tokyo and Osaka. Young people adopted Western fashions -- the modern boys and modern girls, or *mobo* and *moga* -- strolled through the Ginza district, bobbed hair and all.

It was an exciting time, full of optimism and energy. Japan seemed to be moving toward a more open, more democratic future.

But dark clouds were gathering. Military leaders were growing more powerful. Extreme nationalists argued that Japan needed to expand its empire to survive. The Great Depression of the 1930s would bring economic misery and push Japan toward a fateful and tragic path.

The great question of the Meiji era -- what kind of nation would modern Japan become? -- was still unanswered. And the answer, when it finally came, would shake the entire world.
