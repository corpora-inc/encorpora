# The Opening of Japan

On a warm July morning in 1853, fishermen in small wooden boats off the coast of Edo Bay looked up and saw something that made their blood run cold.

Four enormous ships were moving toward them -- but they had no sails. Instead, thick columns of black smoke poured from their funnels, staining the blue sky gray. The ships moved against the wind, cutting through the waves under their own power, as if pushed by some invisible, monstrous force. The hulls were dark iron. Cannons bristled from their sides. And on the deck of the lead ship, the USS *Susquehanna*, stood a stern American naval officer named Commodore Matthew C. Perry.

The fishermen had never seen anything like it. Neither had anyone else in Japan. People ran through the streets of the nearby villages, shouting, "The Black Ships! The Black Ships are coming!"

![Commodore Perry's steam-powered Black Ships arriving in Edo Bay as Japanese boats scatter](images/black-ships-arrival.png)

And just like that, Japan's two hundred and fifty years of peaceful isolation began to crack open like an egg.

## The Locked Country

To understand why those ships caused such panic, you need to know what Japan had been doing for the previous two centuries.

Back in the 1630s, the Tokugawa shoguns had made an extraordinary decision. They looked at what was happening in other parts of Asia -- European traders and missionaries arriving, followed by soldiers and colonizers -- and they said: *No. Not here.* They issued a series of orders that slammed Japan's doors shut to the outside world. This policy was called *sakoku*, which means "locked country."

Under sakoku, almost all foreign trade was banned. The only exception was a tiny artificial island called Dejima in the harbor of Nagasaki, where a handful of Dutch merchants were allowed to trade under strict watch. No Japanese person was allowed to leave the country. If they did leave and tried to come back, the punishment was death. Foreign books were restricted. Christianity, which missionaries had been spreading, was outlawed.

For over two hundred years, this policy held. Japan developed its own extraordinary culture -- the ukiyo-e prints, the kabuki theater, the poetry and tea ceremonies we explored in the last chapter -- largely without outside influence. But during those same two centuries, the rest of the world was transforming at a dizzying pace.

In Europe and America, the Industrial Revolution had created steam engines, railroads, telegraphs, and factories. Countries built powerful navies with iron-hulled steamships and explosive cannons. Britain had already conquered India and was forcing China to accept trade at gunpoint in the Opium Wars. The United States was expanding westward and looking across the Pacific Ocean for new trading partners.

Japan, with its wooden ships and samurai swords, had no idea what was coming.

## Perry's Demand

Commodore Perry carried a letter from the President of the United States, Millard Fillmore. The letter was polite on the surface but carried an unmistakable threat underneath. It requested -- really, demanded -- that Japan open its ports to American ships for trade, supplies, and the protection of shipwrecked sailors.

When Japanese officials rowed out to Perry's fleet and asked him to go to Nagasaki, where foreigners were supposed to go, Perry refused. He pointed at his cannons. He fired blank shots from his guns -- a "salute," he called it, though everyone understood the message. *These cannons could just as easily fire real shells at your cities.*

![Samurai officials in traditional dress face Perry's crew beside a massive steamship](images/samurai-meet-steamship.png)

Perry gave the Japanese a year to consider the letter and said he would return for an answer. Then he sailed away, leaving behind a country in total crisis.

The debate that erupted was ferocious. Some leaders wanted to fight. "We are the land of the samurai!" they argued. "We will drive the barbarians into the sea!" But others had done the math. They had heard what happened when China tried to resist the British -- the Qing dynasty was humiliated, forced to hand over Hong Kong and pay enormous sums of money. Japan's cannons were ancient. Its navy was a fleet of wooden boats. Against steam-powered warships with explosive shells, the samurai sword was useless.

When Perry returned in February 1854 -- this time with *seven* ships -- Japan signed the Treaty of Kanagawa, opening two ports to American ships. It was only the beginning. Over the next few years, the United States, Britain, France, Russia, and the Netherlands all forced Japan to sign what the Japanese bitterly called "unequal treaties." These treaties gave Western nations trading rights, low import taxes, and special legal privileges for their citizens on Japanese soil. Japan had almost no power to refuse.

> **Did You Know?** When Perry's ships arrived, Japanese artists who had never seen a steamship drew pictures of them from memory. Some of the drawings show the ships with dragon-like features, as if the artists were trying to make sense of these smoke-breathing monsters by comparing them to creatures from their own mythology.

## The Fall of the Shogun

The humiliation of the unequal treaties shattered the authority of the Tokugawa shogunate. For two and a half centuries, the shoguns had justified their rule by keeping Japan safe and stable. Now they had been forced to submit to foreign demands without firing a single shot. Across the country, a cry went up: *Sonno joi!* -- "Revere the Emperor, expel the barbarians!"

Young samurai, especially from the powerful southern domains of Satsuma and Choshu, began to rebel. At first they attacked foreigners and foreign ships. But after the British navy bombarded the port of Kagoshima in 1863 and a combined Western fleet destroyed the forts at Shimonoseki in 1864, these young warriors learned a painful lesson: Japan could not drive the foreigners out by force. Not yet. First, Japan needed to become strong. And to become strong, Japan needed to change.

These rebels -- brilliant, fierce, and barely out of their twenties -- began plotting not to expel the foreigners but to overthrow the shogun himself. They believed that the emperor, who had been a ceremonial figurehead for centuries, should be restored to real power. Under the emperor's authority, they would rebuild Japan from the ground up.

In January 1868, after a brief civil war, they succeeded. The last Tokugawa shogun, Yoshinobu, surrendered his power. A fifteen-year-old boy named Mutsuhito became the new ruler, taking the reign name *Meiji*, which means "Enlightened Rule."

![The young Meiji Emperor in formal attire, symbolizing the new era](images/meiji-emperor-portrait.png)

The era of the samurai was ending. The era of modern Japan was about to begin.

## The Meiji Restoration

The word "restoration" might make you think that Japan was going back to the way things used to be. But the Meiji Restoration was actually the opposite -- it was a revolution that launched Japan forward at breathtaking speed.

The young leaders who surrounded the Meiji Emperor had a motto: *Fukoku kyohei* -- "Enrich the country, strengthen the military." They looked at the Western powers that had humiliated them and made a daring decision. Instead of rejecting everything Western, they would study it, learn it, master it, and use it to make Japan so strong that no foreign power could ever bully them again.

They sent teams of scholars, officials, and students to every corner of the globe. One famous mission, the Iwakura Mission of 1871, took nearly fifty top leaders and students on an eighteen-month tour of the United States and Europe. They visited factories, shipyards, schools, parliaments, and military bases, filling notebooks with everything they saw.

"We must pick the best from every country," they decided. The army would be modeled on Germany's. The navy on Britain's. The legal system on France's. The schools on America's. The postal service on Britain's. They took what worked and adapted it to Japan.

The speed of this transformation was unlike anything the world had ever seen. In the space of a single lifetime, Japan would go from a feudal country of samurai and shoguns to a modern industrial power with railroads, telegraphs, universities, a constitution, and a parliament.

It was as if an entire nation had said, all at once, with one voice: *We will not be conquered. We will learn. We will change. We will survive.*

> **Did You Know?** The Meiji Emperor's personal transformation mirrored the nation's. Early portraits show him in traditional Japanese imperial robes. Later portraits show him in a Western-style military uniform with medals, a European-style haircut, and a full beard. He became a living symbol of Japan's new direction.

## A Nation at the Crossroads

The opening of Japan was one of those moments in history where everything changes, and nothing can ever go back to the way it was. In just fifteen years -- from Perry's arrival in 1853 to the Meiji Restoration in 1868 -- Japan went from being a "locked country" to a nation determined to remake itself entirely.

Was something lost in this transformation? Absolutely. The old world of samurai, shoguns, and two-hundred-year peace was gone forever. Many people mourned what was disappearing -- the traditions, the slower pace of life, the sense of a world that made sense.

But something extraordinary was also being born. Japan was about to prove that a small island nation, with few natural resources and no colonial empire, could transform itself into one of the most powerful and innovative countries on Earth -- not over centuries, but over decades.

The question now was: what kind of nation would modern Japan become?

The answer would be written in steam, steel, and the determined footsteps of a people who refused to be left behind.
