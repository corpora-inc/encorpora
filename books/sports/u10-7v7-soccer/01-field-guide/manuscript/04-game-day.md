# Game Day

Arrive at the field with a plan. Know your starting seven and their positions before you leave the house. Have a rough idea of when you will substitute and who will come in for whom. Write the plan in a notebook. Then be ready to throw the plan away, because the game will not go the way you expect.

## The Starting Lineup

The sweeper starts every game. The sweeper is your anchor. Build the rest of the lineup around that player. Put each starter in the position where their strengths help the team the most. The Cannon takes corner kicks. The Speedster plays the wing. The Wall starts in goal. The Lefty goes on the left side. The defenders are kids who are solid with the ball and willing to do the unglamorous work of stopping attacks.

Announce the starting lineup before the game. Tell every player their position clearly. "You are left defender. You are right defender. You are sweeper. You are left wing. You are center attacker. You are right wing. You are in goal." No surprises. The players who are not starting should know when they are coming in and roughly where they will play.

## The Substitution Problem

Substitution at U10 is a harder problem than most coaches realize. You have ten to fourteen kids, seven positions, sixty minutes of game time, and two competing goals. You want every kid to play a meaningful amount. You also want to put the best lineup on the field during the important moments. These goals conflict. There is no perfect solution.

Here is what works in practice. Pick your starting seven. Plan to substitute one player at a time. Divide each thirty-minute half into three ten-minute sections in your head. Each ten-minute mark is a checkpoint. Look at your bench. Look at the field. Decide if anyone needs to come out.

Watch for signs. A player who is breathing hard needs a rest. A player who is limping needs to come out immediately. A player whose head is down and whose effort has dropped needs a break and a conversation on the sideline. Do not wait for the player to ask. Most nine-year-olds will not ask to come out even when they need to.

Use dead balls as substitution windows. Throw-ins, goal kicks, corner kicks, and any stoppage in play are opportunities to wave a player over and send a substitute in. Dead balls happen frequently at U10. You will have plenty of chances to rotate.

Key players need rest too. Even the sweeper needs to sit. A good pattern is to let the sweeper play the entire first half, rest the first ten minutes of the second half, and come back in for the final twenty minutes. The sweeper gets a meaningful break. The team still has the sweeper for most of the game. Adjust this pattern based on the score, the heat, and how the player is feeling.

The score matters. If the team is ahead by three goals, give every substitute extended time. Let the starters rest. Let the backups gain experience. No one benefits from running up the score with the best lineup. If the game is close, keep the strongest lineup in for longer stretches. If the team is losing badly, shift the goal from winning to development. Let kids try new positions. Let the backup goalkeeper get time. Use a blowout loss as a practice opportunity.

You will not get the substitutions perfect. Some kid will play a few minutes less than they should have. Some combination will not work and you will need to adjust. That is fine. The goal is reasonable fairness over the course of the season, not perfect equality in every single game.

## Sideline Coaching

Your voice from the sideline should be a calm signal, not a running commentary. Nine-year-olds cannot process instructions while playing the game. A kid who is listening to the coach is not watching the ball. A kid who is watching the ball is not listening to the coach.

Limit your sideline coaching to three things. First, position reminders. Short and specific. "Push up." "Get wide." "Get back." These are corrections to the team's shape. Say them once and let the player adjust. Second, encouragement. Genuine and brief. "Good tackle." "Nice pass." "Great hustle back." Specific praise reinforces the behavior you want to see. Third, set piece organization. When a corner kick or free kick is coming, remind the players of the plan. "Corner. Cannon, you take it. Attackers, get in the box. Sweeper, stay back."

Do not coach every touch. Do not yell instructions about what to do with the ball. Do not criticize mistakes during the game. The game belongs to the players. Your preparation during the week is what matters. The game is the test. Let them take it.

## Halftime

Halftime at U10 is five to eight minutes. Water first. Let the kids drink for a full minute before you say anything. They are hot and tired and they will not hear you until they have had water.

Then make one point. Pick the single most important adjustment for the second half. "We need to get back on defense faster." Or: "The left side is wide open, find the Lefty Striker." Or: "Great job on defense, keep the shape tight." One thing. Not three things. Not five things. One.

Then make one positive comment. "The passing in the first half was the best we have had all season." Or: "The Speedster made three great runs." Something real and specific. End on the positive.

Make your planned substitutions during the halftime break. Tell the new players their positions. The second half should start with everyone knowing their job.

## After the Game

Keep the post-game talk under two minutes. Acknowledge the result honestly. If the team won, say the team won. If the team lost, say the team lost. Do not pretend a loss was a win. Do not pretend a win was meaningless.

Say one positive thing about the game. Say one thing to work on at the next practice. Then look forward. "We practice Tuesday. Bring water. See you there." The kids want to go get a snack. Let them go.
