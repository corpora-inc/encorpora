# Game Day

Arrive at the field with a plan. Know your starting seven and their positions before you leave the house. Have a rough idea of when you will substitute and who will come in for whom. Write the plan in a notebook. Then be ready to throw the plan away, because the game will not go the way you expect.

## The Starting Lineup

Build the lineup around the sweeper position. Whoever starts at sweeper sets the tone. You need at least two or three players who can handle sweeper so you can rotate, rest, and cover for injuries. Everyone should practice sweeper during the week. On game day, pick the player whose touch and decision-making fit the moment. Put each starter in the position where their strengths help the team the most. Big legs take corner kicks. Speedsters play the wing. Walls start in goal. Lefties go on the left side. The defenders are kids who are solid with the ball and willing to do the unglamorous work of stopping attacks.

Announce the starting lineup before the game. Tell every player their position clearly. "You are left defender. You are right defender. You are sweeper. You are left wing. You are center attacker. You are right wing. You are in goal." No surprises. The players who are not starting should know when they are coming in and roughly where they will play.

## The Substitution Problem

Substitution at U10 is a harder problem than most coaches realize. You have ten to fourteen kids, seven positions, sixty minutes of game time, and two competing goals. You want every kid to play a meaningful amount. You also want to put the best lineup on the field during the important moments. These goals conflict. There is no perfect solution.

Here is what works in practice. Pick your starting seven. Plan to substitute one player at a time. Divide each thirty-minute half into three ten-minute sections in your head. Each ten-minute mark is a checkpoint. Look at your bench. Look at the field. Decide if anyone needs to come out.

Watch for signs. A player who is breathing hard needs a rotation. A player who is limping needs to come out immediately. A player whose energy is fading needs a break. Be proactive about substitutions — players do not always ask for rest even when they need it.

Use dead balls as substitution windows. Throw-ins, goal kicks, corner kicks, and any stoppage in play are opportunities to wave a player over and send a substitute in. Dead balls happen frequently at U10. You will have plenty of chances to rotate.

Every player needs rest, including the sweeper. When the starting sweeper sits, another trained sweeper steps in. A good pattern is to rotate sweepers at halftime or at the ten-minute marks. The team always has a capable sweeper on the field. This is why developing multiple sweepers matters. Adjust the rotation based on the score, the heat, and how the players are feeling.

The score matters. If the team is ahead by three goals, give every substitute extended time. Let the starters rest. Let the backups gain experience. No one benefits from running up the score with the strongest lineup. If the game is close, keep the strongest lineup in for longer stretches. If the team is down by a wide margin, shift the focus to development. Let kids try new positions. Let the backup goalkeeper get time. Use it as a learning opportunity.

You will not get the substitutions perfect. Some kid will play a few minutes less than they should have. Some combination will not work and you will need to adjust. That is fine. The goal is reasonable fairness over the course of the season, not perfect equality in every single game.

## Sideline Coaching

Your voice from the sideline should be a calm signal, not a running commentary. Nine-year-olds cannot process instructions while playing the game. A kid who is listening to the coach is not watching the ball. A kid who is watching the ball is not listening to the coach.

Position reminders, encouragement, and set piece organization are the most useful things you can say from the sideline. Keep it short and specific. "Push up." "Get wide." "Great hustle back." When a corner kick or free kick is coming, remind the players of the plan.

The game belongs to the players. Your preparation during the week is what matters. The game is the test. Let them take it.

## Halftime

Halftime at U10 is five to eight minutes. Water first. Let the kids drink for a full minute before you say anything. They are hot and tired and they will not hear you until they have had water.

Keep it brief. Talk to the team about what matters most for the second half. Pull individual players aside if they need something specific. End on something positive and real.

Make your planned substitutions during the halftime break. Tell the new players their positions. The second half should start with everyone knowing their job.

## After the Game

Keep the post-game talk short. Acknowledge the result honestly. Highlight what went well, point toward what to work on next, and look forward. The kids want to go get a snack. Let them go.
