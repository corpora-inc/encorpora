# Practice

The best practice looks like soccer. Kids learn by playing, not by standing in lines waiting for a turn. Every drill should feel like a real game situation as much as possible. Small-sided scrimmages teach more than cone exercises. Game-like pressure teaches more than unopposed passing patterns.

But there is a place for structured touch work, especially early in the season. Some kids have never played before. They need to learn how to stop the ball, pass the ball, and dribble the ball before they can play in a system. You cannot skip the basics just because game-like drills are more fun. Spend the first weeks building the foundation. The investment pays off for the rest of the season.

## Touches for Beginners

Before a kid can play in the 1-2-3, the kid needs to be able to control a pass and send one back. Individual ball work is the fastest way to build this skill. Each player gets a ball. Dribble in a square. Do ten toe taps. Pull the ball back with the sole of the foot. Roll the ball side to side. These exercises are simple and they produce a massive number of touches in a short time.

Pairs passing is the next step. Two players face each other about five yards apart. Pass and receive with the inside of the foot. Pass and receive with the right foot only. Pass and receive with the left foot only. Move the distance out to ten yards. Add a first touch to the side before passing back. The goal is accuracy and a clean first touch. Every pair gets hundreds of passes in ten minutes.

Do not spend the entire practice on individual work. Ten to fifteen minutes at the start of practice is enough. Then move into game-like drills where the touches happen under pressure.

## Practice Dead Balls

Every dead ball the team will face on Saturday should be rehearsed at least once during the week. Corner kicks, goal kicks, throw-ins, and free kicks all have specific plans. The kids need to know where to stand and what to do before the ball is in play.

Set up a corner kick. Put the Cannon on the corner. Put the attackers in the box. Put the sweeper at the top of the box for rebounds. Take ten corners. Rotate who takes the corner so the Cannon gets a rest and another player gets experience. Then switch to defensive corners. Put the other team on the corner and practice marking, clearing, and transitioning to the counter-attack.

Set up goal kicks. The goalkeeper rolls the ball to a defender. The defender passes to the sweeper. The sweeper looks up and plays forward. Practice this sequence until the kids can do it without thinking. A clean goal kick sequence starts the attack from the back and puts the team in control.

Throw-ins seem simple, but U10 players struggle with the technique. Both feet on the ground. Both hands on the ball. The ball comes from behind the head and goes over the head. Practice the throw-in technique. Then practice the movement off the throw-in. The player receiving the throw should check toward the ball to create separation from the defender. A good throw-in retains possession. A bad throw-in gives the ball away.

## The 6v4 Shape Game

This is the signature drill of the 1-2-3 system. The drill teaches the formation shape, passing lanes, and attacking transitions in a single exercise. You need ten players to run the drill at full scale. Adjust the numbers if your roster is smaller.

Set up the drill on half the field. Place a full-size goal at one end with a goalkeeper. Six players line up on offense in the 1-2-3 shape. The sweeper stands at the midfield line in the center. Two defenders stand in front of the sweeper, one on each side. Three attackers spread across the front line. On the other side, three defenders and a goalkeeper prepare to defend the goal. Place two small cone goals on the wings near midfield. The defending team can score by clearing the ball into one of these small goals.

The drill has two phases. In the first phase, the six offensive players pass the ball freely among themselves. No defenders apply pressure yet. Every offensive player must touch the ball at least once before the phase ends. The players move the ball around the 1-2-3 shape. The sweeper passes to a defender. The defender passes to an attacker. The attacker passes across to another attacker. The ball circulates until all six players have had a touch. This phase teaches the passing lanes and the shape of the formation.

In the second phase, the ball goes live. The three defenders begin to pressure the offense. The offense continues passing and moving, looking for a chance to score on the full-size goal. The defenders try to win the ball and clear it into one of the small wing goals. Play continues until the offense scores, the defense clears the ball, or the ball goes out of bounds.

Rotate the teams every five minutes. The six offensive players become three defenders plus a goalkeeper, and three of the former defenders move to offense. Everyone gets reps on both sides. The drill moves quickly and stays engaging because there is always a goal to play for.

## The 60-Minute Structure

A standard practice runs sixty minutes. Every minute should be active. Kids should be moving and touching the ball from start to finish.

The first ten minutes are arrival and warm-up. Players arrive, get a ball, and start individual work or pairs passing. No standing around waiting for everyone to show up. The early arrivals get extra touches. When the full group is ready, gather in a circle. State the plan for practice in two sentences. "Today we are working on corners and the 1-2-3 shape game. Let us go."

The next fifteen minutes are the first drill block. Pick one drill that targets the specific skill or situation you want to improve. If corners were a disaster last game, spend this block on corner kicks. If the team struggled with passing out of the back, spend this block on goal kick sequences. Focus on one thing. Repetition builds confidence.

The next fifteen minutes are the second drill block. Run the 6v4 Shape Game or another game-like drill. The second block should always have a competitive element. Goals, scoring, winning and losing within the drill. Competition brings energy and urgency that non-competitive drills cannot match.

The final fifteen minutes are a scrimmage. Full-speed, game-like conditions. Let the kids play. Coach lightly during the scrimmage. Stop play briefly to make a point if you see a teachable moment, then restart quickly. The scrimmage is where the kids put everything together.

The last five minutes are the closing circle. Gather the team. One thing they did well. One thing to work on. Remind them of the next practice or game. Break. Done.

## Let Game Day Inform Practice Day

The most useful practice targets the specific problem the team faced in the last game. If the team gave up three goals on corner kicks, practice corner kick defense. If the sweeper kept getting caught upfield, practice the sweeper getting back. If the attackers could not get open, practice movement off the ball.

Keep a notebook at every game. Write down what went wrong and what went right. Use those notes to plan the next practice. The connection between Saturday's game and Tuesday's practice is what drives improvement week over week. A practice that addresses real problems the kids actually experienced is ten times more valuable than a generic drill session.
