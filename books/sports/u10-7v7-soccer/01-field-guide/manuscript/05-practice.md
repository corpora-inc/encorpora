# Practice

The best practice looks like soccer. Kids learn by playing, not by standing in lines waiting for a turn. Every drill should feel like a real game situation as much as possible. Small-sided scrimmages teach more than cone exercises. Game-like pressure teaches more than unopposed passing patterns.

But there is a place for structured touch work, especially early in the season. Every player develops at a different pace. Stopping the ball, passing the ball, and dribbling the ball are the foundation of everything else. Spend the first weeks building that foundation. Everything else gets easier once the basics are solid.

## Touches for Beginners

Before a kid can play in the 1-2-3, the kid needs to be able to control a pass and send one back. Individual ball work is the fastest way to build this skill. Each player gets a ball. Dribble in a square. Do ten toe taps. Pull the ball back with the sole of the foot. Roll the ball side to side. These exercises are simple and they produce a massive number of touches in a short time.

Pairs passing is the next step. Two players face each other about five yards apart. Pass and receive with the inside of the foot. Pass and receive with the right foot only. Pass and receive with the left foot only. Move the distance out to ten yards. Add a first touch to the side before passing back. The goal is accuracy and a clean first touch. Every pair gets hundreds of passes in ten minutes.

Do not spend the entire practice on individual work. Ten to fifteen minutes at the start of practice is enough. Then move into game-like drills where the touches happen under pressure.

## Practice Dead Balls

Every dead ball the team will face on Saturday should be rehearsed at least once during the week. Corner kicks, goal kicks, throw-ins, and free kicks all have specific plans. The kids need to know where to stand and what to do before the ball is in play.

Set up a corner kick. Put a big leg on the corner. Put the attackers in the box. Put the sweeper at the top of the box for rebounds. Take ten corners. Rotate who takes the corner so everyone gets experience. Then switch to defensive corners. Put the other team on the corner and practice marking, clearing, and transitioning to the counter-attack.

Set up goal kicks. The goalkeeper rolls the ball to a defender. The defender passes to the sweeper. The sweeper looks up and plays forward. Practice this sequence until the kids can do it without thinking. A clean goal kick sequence starts the attack from the back and puts the team in control.

Throw-in technique takes practice. Both feet on the ground. Both hands on the ball. The ball comes from behind the head and goes over the head. Practice the throw-in technique. Then practice the movement off the throw-in. The player receiving the throw should check toward the ball to create separation from the defender. A good throw-in retains possession. A bad throw-in gives the ball away.

## The 6v3 Shape Game

This is the signature drill of the 1-2-3 system. It teaches the formation shape, passing, spacing, defensive organization, dead balls, and attacking transitions in a single exercise. The drill works best with ten or eleven players. Expand or contract the numbers depending on who shows up.

### Setup

Use half the field. Place a full-size goal at one end with a goalkeeper. Place two small cone goals on the wings near midfield. Six players line up on offense in the 1-2-3 pyramid shape. The sweeper stands at the midfield line in the center. Two defenders stand in front of the sweeper, one on each side. Three attackers spread across the front line. Three defenders set up in front of the goal.

### Phase One: Passing Without Pressure

The six offensive players pass the ball freely among themselves. One-touch and two-touch passes all over the half. The defenders shift, communicate, and organize their shape but do not pressure the ball yet.

This phase gives the offense basic touch work in the actual formation. It also teaches the defense to move as a unit, read the play, and cover space without rushing the ball.

### Phase Two: Ball Goes Live

The coach calls "Go" or "Ball's live" and the drill becomes a real game. The offense continues passing and moving, looking for a chance to score on the full-size goal. The defenders pressure the ball, try to win it, and clear it into one of the small wing goals.

Six against three, the offense has a numbers advantage. The offense needs to learn how to use it. Pass, stretch the defense, find the open player, and create a dangerous run to goal. The defense learns that only one defender pressures the ball while the other two cover passing lanes and dangerous space. Communication is everything on both sides.

### Scoring

Offense scores by putting the ball in the full-size goal. Defense scores by putting the ball in a small wing goal, which simulates clearing to an open attacker on the counter. A ball across midfield is a restart. A ball out of bounds any other way is a chance to practice the dead ball situation — throw-in, corner kick, goal kick, or free kick. The drill naturally creates reps for every dead ball scenario without needing a separate exercise.

### Rotation

Rotate the teams regularly. Offensive players become defenders and goalkeeper, defenders move to offense. Everyone gets reps on both sides. The drill moves quickly and stays engaging because there is always a goal to play for.

## The 60-Minute Structure

A standard practice runs sixty minutes. Every minute should be active. Kids should be moving and touching the ball from start to finish.

The first ten minutes are arrival and warm-up. Players arrive, get a ball, and start individual work or pairs passing. No standing around waiting for everyone to show up. The early arrivals get extra touches. When the full group is ready, gather in a circle. State the plan for practice briefly. "Today we are working on corners and the shape game. Let us go."

The next fifteen minutes are the first drill block. Pick one drill that targets the specific skill or situation you want to improve. If the team wants to sharpen corner kicks, spend this block on corners. If passing out of the back needs work, spend this block on goal kick sequences. Repetition builds confidence.

The next fifteen minutes are the second drill block. Run the 6v3 Shape Game or another game-like drill. The second block should always have a competitive element. Goals, scoring, winning and losing within the drill. Competition brings energy and urgency that non-competitive drills cannot match.

The final fifteen minutes are a scrimmage. Full-speed, game-like conditions. Let the kids play. Coach lightly during the scrimmage. Stop play briefly to make a point if you see a teachable moment, then restart quickly. The scrimmage is where the kids put everything together.

The last five minutes are the closing circle. Gather the team. Talk about what went well and what to work on. Remind them of the next practice or game. Break. Done.

## Let Game Day Inform Practice Day

Every game reveals what the team can optimize next. Corner positioning can tighten. The sweeper can refine when to commit forward and when to stay patient. The attackers can find better space and time their runs more precisely. Watch the game with an eye for these opportunities.

Keep a notebook at every game. Write down what worked and what can improve. Use those notes to plan the next practice. The connection between Saturday's game and Tuesday's practice is what drives development week over week.
