# Know Your Players

This chapter could not have been written before coaching real kids. Tactics look clean on a whiteboard. Real nine-year-olds are complicated. They have different bodies, different skills, different personalities, and different emotions. The differences between players on a U10 team are enormous. One kid has been playing since age four. Another kid is touching a soccer ball for the first time. One kid is a head taller than everyone else. Another kid is still learning to handle the frustration of a tough play.

These differences are not a problem to solve. They are the reality you deploy. Every kid on the team has something they do well. The coach's job is to find that thing and put the kid where it matters most.

## Match Strengths to Roles

Practice everyone in every position. Let every kid try sweeper, defense, attack, and goalkeeper during the week. Exposure builds understanding. A kid who has played sweeper understands what the sweeper needs from the defenders. A kid who has played goalkeeper understands why the defenders need to clear the ball to the sides.

But on game day, deploy strengths. The kid with a reliable touch and sharp decision-making starts at sweeper. The kid with the biggest leg takes corner kicks and goal kicks. The fastest kid plays on the wing and runs at defenders. The toughest kid with great reflexes plays goalkeeper. The kid who is good with the ball but does not want to be the center of attention plays defense. The only left-footed player specializes on the left wing.

This is not about labeling kids or limiting them. Every player should develop every skill. But on Saturday, the team needs to win. The team wins when each player is in the position where their strengths help the most. A fast kid on the wing creates more danger than a fast kid at sweeper. A kid with a big leg on corner kicks creates more chances than that same kid standing on the front line.

## The Personalities

Working with personalities is harder than drawing formations. You can explain the 1-2-3 in two minutes. Getting a sensitive nine-year-old to keep playing after a bad touch takes real patience and real skill. Here are the types you will encounter on almost every U10 team.

> **Bosses** — Players who understand the game and want to direct traffic on the field.

Bosses see what needs to happen and want everyone else to see it too. That instinct is valuable. The challenge is helping bosses communicate in a way that lifts teammates up. Bosses work well at sweeper, where they can read the whole field and organize from the back. The other players listen because bosses usually know what is happening. Coach bosses on delivery — help them grow from directing to leading. A real leader helps teammates make the next play better.

> **Lefties** — Left-footed players who see the field differently.

Most kids are right-footed. A left-footed player is a rare weapon. Lefties create passing and shooting lanes that right-footed players cannot reach. Put lefties on the left wing. They can cut inside on their strong foot and shoot. They can cross the ball with natural curl toward the goal.

The connection between the sweeper and a lefty becomes a signature play. The sweeper collects the ball, looks left, and sends a long ball to the lefty running up the wing. The lefty receives on the strong foot and attacks the goal. Practice this combination every week until the players can execute it without thinking.

> **Speedsters** — The fastest players on the team.

Speed combined with good positioning is the most dangerous combination on the field. Getting open means moving into space where a teammate can find you with a pass. Getting open means checking your shoulder to see where the defenders are. Getting open means timing your run so the ball arrives when you are already in stride.

Put speedsters on the wing. Teach them to stay wide and stretch the defense. When the sweeper has the ball, speedsters should be making a run into open space behind the opposing defenders. A speedster's edge multiplies with good positioning.

> **Walls** — Tough, fearless players who belong in goal.

Walls do not flinch when a shot comes in. Walls dive for balls that other kids would dodge. Every team needs at least one.

Be strategic about when walls rest. If the team is winning by a comfortable margin, rotate the goalkeeper and give another player time in goal. Walls want to play the field sometimes too. Find moments where the team can afford to rotate without giving up the advantage in goal.

> **Sensitives** — Skilled players who feel every moment of the game deeply.

Sensitives have good feet and make nice plays. They care a lot, and that investment in the game is an asset.

Sensitives perform best with specific, positive feedback. "Great first touch." "Nice pass to the wing." "You won that ball back." Give feedback privately rather than in front of the team. Sensitives have real talent that shows when they feel comfortable and supported.

> **Big Legs** — Players with the strongest kicks on the team.

Big legs can blast a ball forty yards. They can take a corner kick that reaches the goal. They can strike a goal kick past midfield.

Big legs perform best with a clear, defined role. Put them on every set piece where leg strength matters. A player with a specific job stays focused and effective. Corner kicks, goal kicks, and free kicks belong to the big legs.

## Squash the Beefs

Nine-year-olds have conflicts. Two players will have a disagreement during practice or during a game. Frustration after a tough goal can create tension. A player might feel left out of the passing. Feelings get hurt.

Address conflicts immediately. Do not let them fester. Pull the two kids aside. Acknowledge what happened. Then redirect. "You are both on this team. You both help the team in different ways." Name the contributions specifically. "You are our fastest player. You are our best passer. We need both of those things to win." Get the handshake or the fist bump and move on.

Acknowledge differences openly as a team. Every kid knows who the fastest kid is. Every kid knows who has the hardest shot. Every kid knows who is still developing their skills. Pretending everyone is identical does not fool anyone. What works is showing every kid that their specific contribution matters. The kid who always hustles back on defense is keeping the team in the game. Say that out loud. Make it real.

Building chemistry takes time. Over the course of a season, the kids start to understand each other. Bosses learn to encourage teammates. Speedsters learn to find open space. Sensitives build confidence. Big legs stay engaged longer. It is a process. Trust the process and keep showing up.
