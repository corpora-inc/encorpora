# Know Your Players

This chapter could not have been written before coaching real kids. Tactics look clean on a whiteboard. Real nine-year-olds are complicated. They have different bodies, different skills, different personalities, and different emotions. The differences between players on a U10 team are enormous. One kid has been playing since age four. Another kid is touching a soccer ball for the first time. One kid is a head taller than everyone else. Another kid is still learning to handle the frustration of a tough play.

These differences are not a problem to solve. They are the reality you deploy. Every kid on the team has something they do well. The coach's job is to find that thing and put the kid where it matters most.

## Match Strengths to Roles

Practice everyone in every position. Let every kid try sweeper, defense, attack, and goalkeeper during the week. Exposure builds understanding. A kid who has played sweeper understands what the sweeper needs from the defenders. A kid who has played goalkeeper understands why the defenders need to clear the ball to the sides.

But on game day, deploy strengths. The kid with the best touch and the highest soccer IQ plays sweeper. The kid with the biggest leg takes corner kicks and goal kicks. The fastest kid plays on the wing and runs at defenders. The toughest kid with the best reflexes plays goalkeeper. The kid who is good with the ball but does not want to be the center of attention plays defense. The only left-footed player specializes on the left wing.

This is not about labeling kids or limiting them. Every player should develop every skill. But on Saturday, the team needs to win. The team wins when each player is in the position where their strengths help the most. A fast kid on the wing creates more danger than a fast kid at sweeper. A kid with a big leg on corner kicks creates more chances than that same kid standing on the front line.

## The Personalities

Working with personalities is harder than drawing formations. You can explain the 1-2-3 in two minutes. Getting a sensitive nine-year-old to keep playing after a bad touch takes real patience and real skill. Here are the types you will encounter on almost every U10 team.

**The Boss.** This is your best player. The Boss understands the game better than anyone else on the team. The Boss is vocal and wants to direct traffic on the field. That instinct is valuable. The Boss sees what needs to happen and wants everyone else to see it too. The challenge is helping the Boss communicate in a way that lifts teammates up instead of putting them down.

Give the Boss the sweeper role. Let the Boss run the team from the back. The other players should listen to the Boss because the Boss usually knows what is happening on the field. The Boss has the right instincts. The Boss just needs coaching on delivery. Help the Boss grow from directing to leading. A real leader does not point out mistakes. A real leader helps teammates make the next play better.

**The Lefty Striker.** Most kids are right-footed. A left-footed player is a rare weapon. The Lefty Striker sees the field differently and creates passing and shooting lanes that right-footed players cannot reach. Put the Lefty Striker on the left wing. The Lefty can cut inside on their strong foot and shoot. The Lefty can cross the ball with natural curl toward the goal.

The connection between the sweeper and the Lefty Striker becomes a signature play. The sweeper collects the ball, looks left, and sends a long ball to the Lefty Striker running up the wing. The Lefty receives on the strong foot and attacks the goal. Practice this combination every week until the players can execute it without thinking.

**The Speedster.** The Speedster is the fastest kid on the team. Speed is a gift, but speed alone does not score goals. The Speedster needs to learn how to get open. Getting open means moving into space where a teammate can find you with a pass. Getting open means checking your shoulder to see where the defenders are. Getting open means timing your run so the ball arrives when you are already in stride.

Put the Speedster on the right wing. Teach the Speedster to stay wide and stretch the defense. When the sweeper has the ball, the Speedster should be making a run into open space behind the opposing defenders. Speed plus good positioning is devastating. Speed without positioning means a lot of running without results.

**The Wall.** The Wall is the toughest kid on the team. The Wall does not flinch when a shot comes in. The Wall dives for balls that other kids would dodge. The Wall is your starting goalkeeper.

Be strategic about when the Wall rests. If the team is winning by a comfortable margin, let the Wall take a break and give another player time in goal. If the game is close, keep the Wall in. If the team is already losing badly, let the Wall play the field and give another goalkeeper some experience. The Wall wants to play the field sometimes too. Find moments where the team can afford to give the Wall a break without giving up the advantage in goal.

**The Sensitive One.** The Sensitive One is skilled with the ball. The Sensitive One has good feet and can make nice plays. The Sensitive One also feels every moment of the game deeply. A missed pass or a tough play can weigh on the Sensitive One and make it hard to stay engaged. This player cares a lot, and that caring cuts both ways.

Never correct the Sensitive One in front of the team. Pull the Sensitive One aside privately for feedback. Encourage them constantly with specific praise. "Great first touch." "Nice pass to the wing." "You won that ball back." Build them up steadily so they have a reserve of confidence to draw from when a tough moment arrives. The Sensitive One has real talent that comes out when the player feels supported and safe.

**The Cannon.** The Cannon has the biggest leg on the team. The Cannon can blast a ball forty yards. The Cannon can take a corner kick that actually reaches the goal. The Cannon can strike a goal kick past midfield.

The Cannon is also an emotional player. When things are going well, the Cannon is one of the best players on the field. When things get frustrating, the Cannon can lose focus and energy. The key is keeping the Cannon engaged. Put the Cannon on every set piece where leg strength matters. Give the Cannon a role that feels important, because it is important. When the Cannon starts to drift, give them the next corner kick and watch the confidence come right back.

## Squash the Beefs

Nine-year-olds have conflicts. Two players will have a disagreement during practice or during a game. Frustration after a tough goal can create tension. A player might feel left out of the passing. Feelings get hurt.

Address conflicts immediately. Do not let them fester. Pull the two kids aside. Acknowledge what happened. Then redirect. "You are both on this team. You both help the team in different ways." Name the contributions specifically. "You are our fastest player. You are our best passer. We need both of those things to win." Get the handshake or the fist bump and move on.

Acknowledge differences openly as a team. Every kid knows who the best player is. Every kid knows who the fastest kid is. Every kid knows who is still developing their skills. Pretending everyone is equal does not fool anyone. What works is showing every kid that their specific contribution matters. The kid who is not the most skilled but always hustles back on defense is keeping the team in the game. Say that out loud. Make it real.

Building chemistry takes time. After five games, the kids on my team are starting to understand each other. The Boss is learning to encourage teammates. The Speedster is learning to find open space. The Sensitive One is building confidence. The Cannon is staying engaged longer. It is a process. Trust the process and keep showing up.
