## Chapter 5 — The Maqam

When you play a piano, you press white keys and black keys. There are twelve notes in every octave, and each step is the same size. We call this equal temperament.

The oud does not work that way. The oud uses a much older system, called maqam.

A maqam is a way of choosing notes, and there are many maqamat. Each one has its own mood. Some are happy, some are sad, some sound a little mysterious.

The big secret of maqam is that it has more notes than a piano. Between the white keys and the black keys, there are smaller steps that we call quarter tones.

A quarter tone is half of a half step. It is a tiny note that lives in between the notes you know. On a piano, you cannot play it. On an oud, you slide your finger to find it.

A maqam is built out of small groups of notes. Each small group is called a jins. Most ajnas have four notes. You can think of a jins as a little Lego brick made of music, and you stack them to build a maqam.

The maqam is melodic, not harmonic. That means the music moves one note at a time, instead of stacking notes into chords like a guitar.

When an oud plays a slow, winding solo, the player is moving inside a maqam. They are picking notes from the brick, then sliding to the next one. That solo has a name. We call it a taqsim.

A good taqsim can make you laugh, and it can make you cry. It is the oud at its most beautiful.
