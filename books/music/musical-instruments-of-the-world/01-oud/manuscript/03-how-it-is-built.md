## Chapter 3 — How an Oud Is Built

Building an oud takes a long time. A good maker may spend many weeks on a single instrument.

The bowl is made of thin strips of wood, usually between 15 and 25 of them. Each strip is bent and glued to the next one. Together they make the deep, round back of the oud.

The wood matters. Walnut gives a warm sound, mahogany gives a darker sound, and mulberry gives a bright, ringing sound. The maker chooses the wood for the voice they want.

The flat front, the soundboard, is made of spruce. Spruce is light and strong, and it lets the wood vibrate easily. That vibration is what we hear as music.

In the soundboard there are small round holes called rosettes. They are often carved in beautiful patterns. The rosettes let the sound out of the bowl.

The strings used to be made of gut, a kind of dried animal sinew. Today, most strings are made of nylon, with a thin metal wire wound around the lowest ones.

The player picks the strings with a risha. The word risha means feather. The first plectrums were made from eagle feathers. Today they are made of plastic, bone, or tortoiseshell.
