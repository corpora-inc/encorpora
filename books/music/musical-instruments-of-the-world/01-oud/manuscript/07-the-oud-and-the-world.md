## Chapter 7 — The Oud and the World

The oud did not stay in the Arab world. As it traveled, it changed shape, it changed its name, and it changed its songs.

When the oud reached medieval Spain, European players liked the wooden bowl and the deep sound. But they wanted frets, like the small instruments they already knew. So they added frets, flattened the back a little, and called the new instrument the lute.

For five hundred years, the lute was the favorite instrument of Europe. Kings played it. Poets played it. The greatest composers wrote pieces for it.

Then the lute changed again. Players added more strings, flattened the back even more, and put a smaller, lighter body on it. By about the year 1800, in Spain, this new instrument had a name we all know. It was the guitar.

So the oud, the lute, and the guitar are all members of the same family. They are cousins, and they share a grandfather. That grandfather is the oud.

Today, the oud and the guitar play together. Anouar Brahem plays the oud with jazz guitarists. Munir Bashir played for orchestras. Young players in Beirut, Istanbul, Paris, and New York are mixing oud with rock, with hip hop, and with film scores.

The oud is more than an instrument. It is a bridge. It carries a story from Mesopotamia, through Persia, through the Arab world, into Spain, into Europe, and back out into every country in the world.

Whenever you hear a guitar, somewhere far underneath, you can hear the soft, deep voice of the oud.
