# Moments

Let me tell you three small stories. Not the big ones. The small ones, which are the real ones.

The first is about a single pea. It was my first spring with my own little plot. I had planted a row of peas because a friend told me peas were forgiving. For two weeks, nothing. I checked every morning. I stopped checking. I went away for a weekend, came home tired and a little sad, and walked out to the bed without expecting anything. There they were. A whole row of pale green hooks, lifting the soil like tiny shoulders. I sat down right there in the dirt, in my traveling clothes, and just looked at them. I have seen many things grow since. Nothing has ever felt as much like magic as that first quiet line of peas.

The second is about my grandmother. She was eighty when she taught me to deadhead her roses. She did not call it teaching. She handed me a small pair of scissors and stood beside me and told me where to cut. Right there, just above where the leaves come out in fives. Yes, like that. Good. We worked along the row together for an hour, slowly, without much talking. The bees moved between us. I remember the warmth of the sun on my arms and the quiet snip of the scissors. I remember the way she nodded once, when I got it right without being told. She has been gone for some time now. Every time I cut a spent flower, she is there. The garden is full of people who taught us things. They never really leave it.

The third is about a tomato in late summer. The vines were heavy and tired. The leaves had started to yellow at the bottom. I went out at the end of a long, hard week, just to stand for a minute. I picked one tomato off the vine. Still warm from the sun. I bit into it standing there, juice running down my wrist. Salt on my tongue from sweat, the smell of the leaves on my hands. I did not think about anything. I did not need to. The whole long week stopped existing for the length of one tomato. I do not know how to explain what a sun-warm tomato tastes like in your own garden on your own quiet evening. You have to grow one. There is no shortcut.

These are the moments that bring people back. Not the big harvest. Not the photograph. The small private ones that no one else will quite understand. The first sprout. The hand of a person you love beside yours in the dirt. The taste of something you grew yourself, eaten where you grew it.

You will have your own. They will surprise you. A bumblebee asleep in a squash flower at dawn. The sound of rain on dry leaves after a long week. The day a child you love pulls up a carrot and laughs because it is real. The first frost, and the way the garden becomes still and silver.

These moments are not rewards for the work. They are the work. The weeding and the watering and the waiting are how you stay close enough to be there when the small thing happens. And the small thing always happens, if you keep showing up.
