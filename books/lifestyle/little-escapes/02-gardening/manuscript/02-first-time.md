# First Time

Let me put you in the garden.

You are kneeling on a folded towel beside a small patch of soil. The sun is gentle on the back of your neck. A friend has handed you a paper packet of seeds and a little trowel. They give you the patient smile of someone who knows what is about to happen.

Your friend tells you, calmly, that you cannot really do this wrong. You nod. You have nodded so many times in the last hour that your neck is starting to hurt.

Then you push the trowel into the earth.

The soil parts like soft cake. It is darker than you expected, and cooler, and it smells like rain and old leaves and something almost like bread. You crumble a clod between your fingers. Tiny roots, a pale pebble, the curl of a worm moving away from the light. You hold a piece of the world in your hand, and the world keeps moving inside it.

Your friend nods. They say, a little deeper. They say, now the seed.

You tip the packet, and one small dark grain rolls into your palm.

It is so small. That is the first surprise. The thing that will become a whole plant, with leaves and stems and fruit, fits under your thumbnail with room to spare. You stare at it for a second longer than you mean to.

Then you press it down into the dark. You cover it. You pat the soil flat, like tucking in a child. You sit back on your heels. Your friend hands you a watering can. You pour gently, and the water sinks straight in. The patch goes the color of strong coffee.

You look around. The garden is still very quiet, but it is different now. You have done a small thing that the soil will remember.

You are a gardener. Not in your head. You, your hands, your knees, your slow breath, are a gardener.

Your friend taps you on the shoulder. They walk you to the next row. You plant another seed. And another. The work has a rhythm now, like rocking a baby, like kneading bread. The sun moves a little. Your back warms. A bird lands on a fence and watches without comment.

The world goes quiet.

The phone is gone. The list is gone. The hurry is gone. You are kneeling now, with dirt under your nails. In front of you, a small flat bed of brown earth holds a dozen invisible promises. The garden is suddenly very, very still. You can hear the bees in the next yard. You can hear yourself breathing. Your friend laughs softly and asks how you are doing. You cannot answer for a moment, because you are smiling, or near tears, or both.

The first watering takes about five minutes. They are five of the most peaceful minutes of your day.

When you stand up, your knees crack and your back complains, and you brush the soil off your hands and stop. The grass is real again. The afternoon is solid again. You turn around and look back at the patch of bare earth. And you understand, for the first time, what your friend has been trying to tell you all these years.
