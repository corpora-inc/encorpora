# Why It Stays

People sometimes ask me, after a few years, whether I am tired of it yet. Whether the novelty has worn off. Whether I am ready to move on to something new. I always smile, because I understand the question, and because I know the answer surprises them.

Gardening is not a phase, because a garden is not a project. A project ends. A garden does not. Each year, the soil asks you to come back, and each year, you find you want to. The seasons keep turning, and you turn with them. You plant in the cool spring. You tend in the long summer. You harvest in the slow fall, and you rest in the quiet winter. Then it begins again, and you are slightly different, and so is the garden.

It stays because it teaches you, gently, the things you most need to learn. It teaches you that you are not in charge of everything. The weather will do what it will do. The frost will come when it comes. The pest will arrive on its own schedule. You can be careful, and clever, and kind, and still lose the squash. The garden does not care about your plans. And somehow, the longer you garden, the more this becomes a comfort, not a frustration.

It stays because it gives you something to look forward to that is not on a screen. In the dead of winter, you sit at a kitchen table with a warm cup and a stack of seed packets. You plan. You imagine the beans climbing. You imagine the basil dense and fragrant. The hope alone is worth the price of the seeds.

It stays because it slows your thinking down to a speed your body actually likes. The mind, left alone with a row of weeds, sorts itself out. Problems you carried into the garden walk out of you on their own. You did not solve them. You just gave them somewhere quiet to sit. By the time you wash your hands at the kitchen sink, half of them have wandered off without saying goodbye.

It stays because it connects you to people. Other gardeners, anywhere, are immediately your friends. A neighbor over a fence. A stranger at a market with a tray of seedlings. Gardening is one of the oldest conversations humans have. You have joined it, and you will never run out of people to talk with.

And it stays because, year after year, it gives you back your sense of time. A garden does not let you forget that life moves in seasons. There is a time for planting and a time for resting. There is a time for tending and a time for letting go. You feel this in your hands, in your back, in your knees. Your body knows the year because your garden made you live it.

Picture us on a slow walk in early spring. You ask why I still kneel in the dirt after all this time. I will give you my answer. The garden gave me a life that fits me. Slower. Smaller. Greener. Closer to the ground. And I would not trade it for anything the world is selling.

That is why it stays. Not because I love it more each year, although I do. Because it loves me back, in its quiet way, every single season.
