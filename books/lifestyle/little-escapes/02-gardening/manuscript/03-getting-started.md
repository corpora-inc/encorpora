# Getting Started

Here is the honest truth about beginning a garden. You need less than the magazines tell you, and more than your friends warn you about. The hard part is not the money. The hard part is the waiting.

Start small. Smaller than you think. A single pot on a sunny windowsill is a real garden. A wooden box on a balcony is a real garden. A patch of yard the size of a bath towel is a real garden. People fail because they begin with a kingdom and find they cannot rule it. Begin with a corner you can love.

You will need a few things. A trowel, a watering can, gloves if your hands are tender, and good soil. Of these, the soil matters most. Cheap soil grows cheap plants. Spend a little extra on a bag that smells rich and dark when you open it.

You will also need light. Walk around your space at three different hours, morning, noon, and late afternoon. Notice where the sun lingers. Plants do not negotiate about light. If a corner is shady, choose plants that love shade. If a wall is hot, choose plants that love heat. The garden is a conversation with the sun.

Water is the next teacher. New gardeners almost always drown their plants. The instinct, when something looks sad, is to pour. The truth is that most sad plants are sad because they are sitting in cold wet feet. Push a finger into the soil before you water. If it is damp at the second knuckle, walk away.

Choose easy things first. Lettuce and herbs and beans forgive almost everything. Tomatoes will reward your attention generously, but they will also test it. Leave the rare and difficult plants for next year. This first season is for confidence, not for showing off.

Read the back of a seed packet, slowly. It will tell you almost everything you need. When to plant. How deep. How far apart. Seed packets are written by people who have made every mistake on your behalf. Trust them, mostly.

Expect failure. Half the things you plant in your first year will not work. A seedling will get nibbled in the night. A pot will dry out on a hot day while you were at work. A tomato will split for no reason you can name. None of this is your fault. All of it is the fee for joining the club.

The entry barrier, honestly, is your patience. You can buy a pot in an afternoon. You cannot buy a third week. The lettuce takes as long as the lettuce takes. There is no faster lettuce. This is the real test, and most people pass it without realizing they were being tested.

You do not need a teacher, but a kind neighbor helps. Find the person on your street with the messiest, happiest garden. They will be flattered if you ask one good question. Most of what gardeners know was given to them by another gardener over a fence. You are now part of that long chain.

Buy a notebook. Write the date you planted. Write what came up and what did not. Next year, you will read it and laugh at how much you learned without noticing. That notebook is the second most useful tool you will own. The first is your own slowed-down breath.
