# Why It Stays

I have been jumping for a long time now. Long enough that I do not remember the count. Long enough that the question, why are you still doing this, has stopped meaning anything to me.

I want to leave you with a few things.

The first is that skydiving is not a phase. People worry, when they see a friend take it up. They worry it is a rebellion, a midlife thing, a way of running from something. It is not. The people who stay are not running. They have, almost without meaning to, built a small life inside the sky. They show up on Saturdays. They pack their parachutes. They hug their friends. They go home at sunset, tired in the good way. On Monday they go back to work, and they are noticeably kinder than they used to be.

The sky changes you, but slowly. You become more present. You become less afraid of small things, because you have practiced being honest with bigger things. You laugh more easily. You tell people you love them more often. You have stood in a doorway, twelve thousand feet up, and remembered that nothing is promised.

If you ever find yourself at dinner with a skydiver, here are the things you can ask.

You can ask how they got into it. Every skydiver remembers their first jump in vivid detail, and they will be happy to tell you. You can ask how many jumps they have done. They will know the number, give or take a few, and they will not pretend it is more than it is. You can ask what their favorite kind of jump is. Some will say a sunset. Some will say a big formation. Some will say a quiet solo on a calm evening.

You can ask if they were scared the first time. Of course they were. Everyone is. They will probably mention the wind in the door, the count, and how the world looks just before you step out. Listen carefully. They are giving you a small gift.

If you want to make a skydiver light up, ask them to describe the moment the parachute opens. They will close their eyes for half a second. Then they will try. They will use a different word every time. Maybe quiet. Maybe gentle. Like waking up. Like coming home. Nobody describes it the same way twice.

Do not be afraid to admit that you have never jumped. Skydivers love that question. Skydivers also love telling you that you should try, and then telling you, very seriously, that they will go with you. Most of us have brought a friend to their first jump. It is one of the great pleasures of the sport.

There is no big lesson at the end of this. Only this. Somewhere in the world right now, a small group of calm and patient people are walking out to a plane. The field is sunlit. The grass is warm. They are checking each other's gear. They are smiling. In thirty minutes, they will be in the sky, doing the quietest thing they know how to do.

Maybe one day you will be one of them. Maybe one day you will not. Either way, the sky is there. It has always been there. It is waiting, patiently, in case you ever feel like leaning.
