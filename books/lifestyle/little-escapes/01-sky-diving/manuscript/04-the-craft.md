# The Craft

There is a craft hidden inside the falling, and it is the part of skydiving that keeps you for life.

When you watch an experienced skydiver in freefall, you do not see somebody falling. You see somebody flying. They turn slowly, on purpose, with their fingertips. They slide forward across the sky for half a mile by tipping their shoulders forward two inches. They stop, mid-fall, and hold a stillness that looks impossible.

The body is the airplane. That is the first secret of the craft. Once you understand that, everything else follows.

The basic shape of a stable freefall is called the arch. You push your hips forward and let your arms and legs trail behind, like a banana. The arch is what keeps you face-down to the earth without you having to think about it. New jumpers learn the arch in a foam pit, then in a wind tunnel, then in the sky. Old jumpers still talk about the arch the way old runners talk about their stride.

Once you have a stable arch, the rest of freefall opens up. You learn to fall slowly by spreading wide, turning yourself into a kite. You learn to fall fast by tucking small, turning yourself into a stone. You learn to track, which is the closest thing to flying a human body can do. A good tracker can cover a quarter of a mile across the sky in twenty seconds. Arms swept back, body angled, moving like a paper airplane that knows where it is going.

The other half of the craft is the canopy. The parachute is not a passive thing that floats you down. It is a wing, and you fly it. Pulling on the right toggle dips the right wing and turns you right. Pulling on both at the same time slows you down and lets you land soft. You read the wind, you pick the spot, you plan your circuit. You flare at the last second, so your feet kiss the ground instead of slamming into it. All of that is canopy flight, and it is its own discipline.

And then there is packing.

Every skydiver packs their own parachute. Every single time. You lay the canopy out on a long mat. You smooth the lines. You fold the cells. You cocoon the fabric, ease it into the bag, route the bridle, close the container. It takes about fifteen minutes, and you do it slowly, because your life is going to depend on this very piece of fabric.

Packing is meditation. Many skydivers say it is the part of the day they love most. The mat, the lines, the slow folding. The shoulder of a friend packing beside you. The radio in the corner playing a song you have heard too many times. There is no rushing the pack. The sky does not care about your schedule.

The mindset of the craft is patience. It is humility. It is the willingness to debrief every single jump, even the boring ones. Skydivers love to talk after a jump. They will sit on a picnic table and walk through every second, body position by body position. What worked. What was sloppy. What they want to try next time.

That is why people stay. Not because the falling is exciting. The falling is the easy part. People stay because there is always something to learn. There is always a friend to learn it with. And there is a sky waiting for you at noon tomorrow.
