# First Time

Let me put you in the plane.

You are sitting on a low metal bench, harnessed to an instructor behind you. The plane climbs in slow, lazy circles. The little square window beside you gets bluer and bluer. You can feel your heart in your throat, but you are not panicking. You are just very, very awake.

The instructor leans close to your ear. He tells you, calmly, that everything you need to do, he will tell you when. You nod. You have nodded so many times in the last hour that your neck is starting to hurt.

Then the door opens.

The wind comes in like a wall, loud and cold and alive, and suddenly the plane feels very small. You scoot to the edge. Your toes hang in nothing. You look down, and the ground is so far away that it does not look like ground anymore. It looks like a quilt. Roads as thin as thread. A river the color of dull silver. Tiny green and brown fields stitched together by hedges.

Your instructor counts. He says one. He says two.

On three, the plane is gone.

The first three seconds are the hardest. Your body believes it is falling, because it is, and your body has never been told this is okay. You make a sound. Everyone makes a sound. There is no need to be embarrassed about the sound.

Then something extraordinary happens. The fall stops feeling like falling. The wind comes up to meet you and holds you, the way water holds you when you float on your back. Your arms and legs spread out without you asking them to. Your face feels like it is being pulled in many directions at once. You cannot tell if you are smiling or screaming. It does not matter.

You look around. The earth is still very far below you, but it is closer now, and you can see the colors better. Cars on a highway, slow as ants. A lake, perfectly still. Cloud shadow drifting across a forest.

You are flying. Not in a plane. You, your body, are flying.

Your instructor taps you on the shoulder. You pull the cord. There is a soft yank, like someone gently grabbing the back of your shirt. Then a second yank, harder, that lifts you upright.

The world goes quiet.

The wind is gone. The roar is gone. The fall is gone. You are hanging now, in a harness, under a wide rectangle of fabric. The sky is suddenly very, very still. You can hear the bird below you. You can hear yourself breathing. Your instructor laughs in your ear and asks how you are doing. You cannot answer for a moment, because you are crying, or laughing, or both.

The canopy ride takes about five minutes. They are five of the most peaceful minutes of your life.

When your feet touch the ground, you take three short running steps and stop. The grass is real again. The world is solid again. You turn around and look up at the sky. And you understand, for the first time, what your friend has been trying to tell you all these years.
