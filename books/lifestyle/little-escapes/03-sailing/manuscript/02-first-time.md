# First Time

Let me put you in the boat.

You are sitting on a low wooden bench, your hand on a smooth tiller behind you. The boat rocks gently against the dock. The mast taps a soft, hollow note above your head. You can smell wet rope and warm wood and a little salt. Your friend is at the bow, untying the last line.

The friend tells you, calmly, that everything you need to do, they will tell you when. You nod. You have nodded so many times in the last hour that your neck is starting to hurt.

Then the line drops.

The boat slides away from the dock so quietly you almost miss it. There is no engine sound. There is no rush. There is just a soft slap of water under the hull, and the dock getting smaller, and the wind on one side of your face. You did not press a button. You did not pull a cord. You are simply moving, because the air decided to push you.

Your friend pulls in the sail. The cloth goes from loose and flapping to tight and shaped, and the boat leans, and your stomach leans with it. You make a small sound. Everyone makes a small sound. There is no need to be embarrassed.

Then something extraordinary happens. The lean stops feeling scary. The boat finds an angle and holds it, and you realize the wind is doing the work, and the water is doing the work, and all you have to do is sit and not fall off. Your shoulders drop. Your jaw, which you did not know you were clenching, lets go.

You look around. The shore is still close, but it is changing shape. Houses you have walked past for years look different from the water. Trees lean in the same wind that is pushing you. A bird, low and slow, is going almost exactly your speed.

You are sailing. Not in a movie. You, your hand on the tiller, are sailing.

Your friend points up at a small ribbon at the top of the mast. They tell you that ribbon is your eyes. They tell you the wind is over your left shoulder, and if it moves to your right shoulder, the sail will need to come across. You nod, but you only half understand. That is fine. The boat is teaching you faster than your friend can.

The wind drops for a moment. The sail goes soft. The boat slows. The world goes very quiet. You can hear the water against the hull, a long, low whisper. You can hear yourself breathing. Your friend smiles and does not say anything. They know this part. They are letting the silence work on you.

Then the wind comes back. The sail fills. The boat leans again. And you laugh, out loud, because you did not know a thing made of wood and cloth could feel this alive.

The first sail takes about an hour. It is one of the most peaceful hours of your life.

When the boat slides back to the dock and your feet touch the boards, your legs feel funny, as if the ground is the thing that moves now. You turn around and look out at the water. And you understand, for the first time, what your friend has been trying to tell you all these years.
