# Moments

I want to tell you about three small days, because they are the days I keep coming back to when someone asks me why I still go out.

The first was a morning so calm that the water looked like a mirror. There was almost no wind. We launched anyway, because we had brought sandwiches and we are stubborn. For the first hour, we drifted. The sail hung loose. The boat barely moved. We did not talk. We just sat, and watched the sky, and ate slowly. My friend lay back across the bow and closed his eyes. I trailed my fingers in the water. A small fish came up and looked at my hand and went away again. Then, very softly, the wind came back. The sail filled. The boat began to move. We had not done anything. The wind had simply arrived, and decided we were ready, and taken us home. I think about that morning more than any other day on the water.

The second was the opposite. It was a windy afternoon, the kind where the water is already showing little white tops before you leave the dock. I went out alone, which I should not have done quite that early in my sailing life, but I did. The boat heeled hard, almost too hard, and for an hour I was completely focused, hand tight on the tiller, eyes on the sail, body leaned out over the side to keep the boat flat. I was a little scared. I was very alive. When I finally turned for shore, my arms were shaking, and I was grinning so widely my cheeks hurt. I sat on the dock afterward and could not stop laughing. I had not spoken to anyone in three hours. I had spoken to the wind the entire time.

The third was an evening sail with my father. He had not been on a boat since he was a young man. He sat on the bench, hands folded, watching everything. He did not say much. I worked the tiller and the sail and tried not to make a fuss. The light got long and gold across the water. A bird flew low across our path. My father turned to me and said, very quietly, that he had forgotten this. He had forgotten that the world could be this quiet. We sailed back in the soft last light, and he kept one hand on the side of the boat the whole way, the way you keep a hand on a sleeping child. He died a few years later. I have that hour with me forever.

These are the moments I mean. None of them are big. None of them would make a good story at a loud dinner. The first time someone asks me about sailing at a party, I do not tell these stories. They sound too quiet. They sound like nothing happened.

But everything happened. The wind happened. The water happened. The hour happened, and it happened with someone I loved, or alone, or in a silence I will never get back. Sailing collects these hours and gives them to you, one at a time, and you do not always notice while they are happening.

You only notice later, when you find yourself missing the boat on a Tuesday afternoon, for no reason you can name.
