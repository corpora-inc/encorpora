# Part Two: Ipiutak

## Chapter 3 — The City at the Edge of the World

Point Hope, Alaska, is a narrow gravel spit jutting into the Chukchi Sea, roughly two hundred kilometers above the Arctic Circle. It is one of the most remote inhabited places in North America. The nearest city is Kotzebue (KOT-zeh-byoo), 130 kilometers to the southeast, accessible only by bush plane or, in winter, by snowmobile across the frozen sea. The village of Tikigaq (tih-KEE-gahk), as Point Hope is known in Inupiaq (ih-NOO-pee-ahk), sits at the very tip of the spit, where the land narrows to a point barely a hundred meters wide, surrounded on three sides by the Arctic Ocean.

Tikigaq has been continuously inhabited for over two thousand years. It is one of the oldest continuously occupied sites in the Arctic. The reason is simple: the geography creates a natural hunting station. The point extends into a zone where ocean currents converge, bringing marine mammals close to shore. Bowhead whales, beluga whales, walruses, and seals pass the point on their seasonal migrations, and hunters stationed on the tip can intercept them. The spring whale hunt, in particular, is the defining event of Tikigaq's annual cycle -- it has been for millennia.

In the late 1930s, two archaeologists arrived at Point Hope and discovered something extraordinary beneath the modern village. Froelich Rainey, an American from the University of Alaska, and Helge Larsen (HEL-geh LAR-sen), a Dane from the Danish National Museum, had come to investigate reports of unusually rich archaeological deposits. What they found exceeded anything they had expected.

Beneath and adjacent to the modern village, Rainey and Larsen uncovered the remains of a massive settlement -- far larger than any known prehistoric Arctic site. They mapped over six hundred semi-subterranean house pits, arranged in roughly parallel rows along the old beach ridges. The houses extended for over a mile along the coastline. At its peak, the settlement had been home to several thousand people -- a population larger than most modern Arctic villages and comparable in size to Fairbanks, Alaska, when the excavations took place.

Rainey and Larsen named the site Ipiutak, after the Inupiaq name for a nearby area. The radiocarbon dates placed the main occupation between approximately 100 BCE and 800 CE -- a span of nearly a millennium. The peak of occupation appears to have been around 200-500 CE, roughly contemporary with the Roman Empire's decline and the early centuries of the Byzantine period in Europe.

The scale of Ipiutak was astonishing for an Arctic site. Arctic populations are typically small because the carrying capacity of the Arctic environment -- the amount of food available in a given area -- is limited by the extreme climate and the seasonal availability of resources. A city of several thousand people at the edge of the Arctic Ocean implied either an exceptionally productive local environment, an unusually effective subsistence strategy, or both. At Point Hope, the convergence of marine mammal migration routes created exactly the kind of resource concentration that could support a large population: whales, walruses, and seals passing in predictable numbers at predictable times, year after year.

The houses at Ipiutak were rectangular, semi-subterranean structures -- pits roughly four to five meters on a side, dug into the gravel to a depth of about half a meter, with walls of driftwood logs and roofs of driftwood beams covered with sod. Each house had a long entrance tunnel extending from one wall -- a feature common in Arctic architecture, designed to trap cold air in the tunnel (which slopes downward from the house floor to the exterior) and prevent it from entering the living space. The tunnel functions as a cold trap: since cold air sinks and warm air rises, the living space above the tunnel entrance remains warmer than the air outside, even without supplemental heating.

The arrangement of the houses in parallel rows suggests planned settlement -- some degree of community organization that dictated where houses could be built. The rows follow the old beach ridges, which would have been the highest and driest ground available, but the regularity of the spacing suggests more than simple convenience. A settlement of this scale, with hundreds of houses organized in a coherent pattern, implies social structures capable of managing a large population: leaders, rules about land use, systems for distributing resources, and mechanisms for resolving disputes.

The artifacts from Ipiutak's living areas are consistent with a sophisticated maritime hunting culture. Harpoon heads, lance points, arrow points, adze blades, scrapers, and other tools of stone, bone, ivory, and antler fill the archaeological record. The toolkit is diverse and specialized, with different tool types for different prey species and different seasons. The harpoon heads, in particular, show a variety of forms adapted to different targets: small toggling heads for seals, larger heads for walruses, specialized points for different hunting conditions.

The stone tools at Ipiutak are beautifully made. The flint-knapping (stone-shaping) tradition represented at Ipiutak is among the finest in the Arctic -- thin, symmetrical blades and finely retouched points that demonstrate a high level of craft skill. The raw material was chert and obsidian, some of which was imported from sources hundreds of kilometers away, indicating trade connections with communities in the Alaskan interior and possibly across the Bering Strait in Siberia.

What was absent from Ipiutak's living areas was as notable as what was present. Rainey and Larsen found no pottery. This was surprising because contemporary cultures elsewhere in Alaska used pottery -- the Norton tradition, which overlapped with Ipiutak in time and partially in space, was a pottery-using culture. The absence of pottery at Ipiutak, combined with the presence of a highly developed flint-knapping tradition, gave the site a peculiarly archaic quality -- as if the Ipiutak people had chosen to maintain older technological traditions while their neighbors adopted innovations.

There was also, intriguingly, no clear evidence that the Ipiutak people hunted whales -- despite living at one of the best whale-hunting locations in the Arctic. The faunal remains (animal bones) from the living areas are dominated by seal and caribou bones, with walrus bones present in smaller quantities. Whale bones are rare or absent from the habitation layers. The Ipiutak people lived at the perfect spot for whale hunting but appear to have focused their subsistence on smaller prey. Whether this reflects a cultural choice (a taboo against whale hunting, or a lack of whale-hunting technology), a temporal pattern (whales may have been less abundant during Ipiutak's peak), or a bias in the archaeological sample is debated.

The relationship between Ipiutak and the contemporary Norton tradition -- a pottery-using culture that occupied many of the same regions of western Alaska during the same centuries -- adds another layer of complexity. Norton sites are found at many locations along the Norton Sound coast and in the Yukon-Kuskokwim (KOOS-koh-kwim) Delta, often in close proximity to Ipiutak-type sites. The two traditions share some tool types but differ markedly in others: Norton people made pottery, Ipiutak people did not. Norton people hunted whales, Ipiutak people apparently did not. The two traditions may represent different ethnic groups, different economic specializations, or different social classes within a single regional system. The relationship between them is one of the unresolved problems of Alaskan archaeology.

The houses at Ipiutak, despite their modest individual size, collectively represent an enormous investment of labor and material. Each house required driftwood logs for walls and roof beams -- a valuable material in a region where trees do not grow and all wood must be scavenged from the beach or traded from forested regions to the south. The availability of driftwood at Point Hope is relatively good -- logs carried by the Yukon and other rivers into the Bering Sea are deposited by currents along the coast -- but building six hundred houses still required a sustained accumulation of wood over many years. The settlement's longevity (nearly a millennium) suggests that Ipiutak was not a single construction event but a continuous process of building, rebuilding, and expansion.

The middens at Ipiutak -- the refuse deposits adjacent to the houses -- contain a rich record of daily life. Seal bones predominate, particularly ringed seal and bearded seal (ugruk, OOG-rook). Caribou bones are also abundant, indicating that the Ipiutak people pursued a dual economy: marine hunting in winter and spring, caribou hunting in summer and autumn. The combination of marine and terrestrial resources would have provided a varied diet: seal meat and blubber (rich in fat and vitamins), caribou meat (lean protein), fish, birds, and their eggs. The diversity of the faunal assemblage suggests a well-balanced subsistence strategy, even if it did not include whale hunting.

The true surprise of Ipiutak, however, was waiting in the burial ground.


## Chapter 4 — The Death Masks

The Ipiutak cemetery lay on a beach ridge adjacent to the habitation area. Rainey and Larsen excavated 138 burials, and the objects they found with the dead were unlike anything previously known from the Arctic.

The most famous Ipiutak artifacts are the death masks. Carved from walrus ivory, the masks are composite constructions: a face piece consisting of eyes, nose, and mouth carved separately and originally assembled on a backing that has since decayed (probably wood or leather). The eyes are made from ivory orbs inlaid with jet (a hard, black, polished stone) to create irises, giving the masks an uncanny, staring quality. The noses are sometimes carved as bird beaks -- stylized raptor or owl forms that transform the human face into something avian, something between human and bird. Ivory nose plugs, also in bird-beak form, were inserted into the nasal openings.

![Ipiutak ivory death mask with jet-inlaid eyes and bird-beak nose plug](../images/placeholders/05-ipiutak-death-mask.png)

The effect is haunting. The masks were placed over the faces of the dead, transforming the deceased into something other than human -- a being with the staring eyes of ivory and jet and the beak of a predatory bird. The masks have been interpreted as shamanic equipment: devices that enabled the dead to travel in the spirit world, to assume animal forms, to move between realms. The bird imagery is consistent with shamanic traditions across the circumpolar world, in which the shaman's spirit takes the form of a bird to travel to the sky world or the underworld. The dead at Ipiutak were equipped for a journey.

The burial goods also included carved ivory objects of extraordinary complexity: openwork carvings (ivory pieces with patterns cut through them, creating lattice-like designs), spiral and pretzel-shaped ornaments, chains carved from a single piece of ivory (each link separate but interlocking, carved without breaking the ivory), and animal figurines. Some objects are purely decorative; others appear to be functional tools of uncertain purpose. The openwork carvings, in particular, have no known parallels in Arctic art -- they are unique to Ipiutak.

The chains carved from single pieces of ivory deserve special attention. To carve a chain from a single piece of ivory -- each link separate, movable, and interlocking, without breaking the continuous piece of material -- requires extraordinary spatial visualization, patience, and manual skill. The carver must work in three dimensions, removing material from between links that are still connected to the main piece, gradually freeing each link while keeping it attached to its neighbors. A single slip of the carving tool and the chain breaks. The Ipiutak ivory chains represent virtuoso craftsmanship -- a display of skill for its own sake, or perhaps for the sake of the dead.

Some burials contained small carved ivory objects that Rainey and Larsen described as "animal carvings with inset eyes." These included miniature bears, seals, and what appear to be mythological creatures -- composite beings that combine features of different animals. The inset eyes are made of jet or, in some cases, of small concave ivory discs set into drilled sockets. The effect gives the carved animals an animated, watchful quality, as if the sculptures were meant to see -- to function as observers or guardians in the burial context.

One remarkable burial contained what Rainey and Larsen called a "petrified human hand" -- a carved ivory object shaped like a human hand with elongated fingers, possibly a ritual implement or a representation of a spiritual being's hand. The object was found near the skull of the deceased, alongside mask components and other carved ivory pieces. The combination of mask, carved hand, and animal figurines suggests that the burial was a carefully staged composition -- a tableau of objects with spiritual significance arranged around the body according to a ritual logic that we can observe but cannot fully reconstruct.

The artistic style of the Ipiutak burial goods is what truly sets the site apart. The designs feature flowing curvilinear lines, spirals, and interlocking patterns that have no precedent in the Arctic archaeological record. They do, however, have striking parallels in the art of cultures far to the west -- across the Bering Strait, in Siberia, and beyond.

The curvilinear style of Ipiutak art has been compared to the Scytho-Siberian animal style -- the artistic tradition of the nomadic cultures of the Eurasian steppe, which flourished from roughly the seventh century BCE to the third century CE. The Scytho-Siberian style is characterized by dynamic animal forms, flowing lines, spirals, and a preference for depicting animals in motion -- running, fighting, being hunted. The Scythians (SITH-ee-uhnz) and their cultural relatives spread this style across the steppe from the Black Sea to Mongolia, and elements of it reached Siberia's Pacific coast, well within the range of trans-Bering contact.

![Comparison of Ipiutak curvilinear art with Scytho-Siberian animal style motifs](../images/placeholders/06-ipiutak-scythian-comparison.png)

The connection between Ipiutak and the Scytho-Siberian tradition is debated. The similarities are real -- the curvilinear designs, the animal imagery, the emphasis on transformation and movement -- but the mechanisms of transmission are unclear. Did Ipiutak artists have direct contact with Siberian cultures? Did they share a common artistic ancestor, a circumpolar tradition that expressed itself differently in different regions? Or is the resemblance partly coincidental -- the result of similar artistic impulses operating independently on both sides of the Bering Strait?

Owen Mason, a specialist in Ipiutak archaeology, has argued that the connections are best understood as part of a broader circumpolar interaction zone -- a network of cultural contacts linking communities around the Arctic rim, facilitated by the relative ease of travel across the Bering Strait (which is only eighty-five kilometers wide at its narrowest point, with islands -- the Diomede Islands -- midway that reduce the open-water crossing to less than forty kilometers in each direction). During periods of winter freeze, the strait can be crossed on foot over the ice. The Bering Strait was a bridge, not a barrier, and cultural exchange across it was continuous for millennia.

The Ipiutak people themselves remain enigmatic. They lived at Point Hope for nearly a millennium, built a settlement of unprecedented size for the Arctic, produced burial art of extraordinary sophistication, and then -- like the Dorset -- faded from the archaeological record. The later occupation of Point Hope was by Thule whale-hunting cultures whose toolkit and artistic style were entirely different from Ipiutak's. The transition between Ipiutak and Thule at Point Hope is not well understood: whether the Ipiutak population was replaced, absorbed, or simply evolved into a different cultural configuration is unclear.

The Ipiutak cemetery raises a further question. The elaborate burial goods -- the masks, the chains, the carved animals -- were confined to a subset of burials. Many Ipiutak graves contained modest or no grave goods. The differential treatment suggests social stratification: some individuals (perhaps shamans, leaders, or members of elite families) received elaborate funerary treatment, while others did not. In a community of several thousand people, the existence of social hierarchy is not surprising, but the nature and basis of that hierarchy -- was it based on spiritual power, hunting skill, lineage, or some other criterion? -- is unknown.

The local Inupiaq community of Tikigaq maintains its own oral traditions about the ancient settlement. Elders have described the old people of Point Hope as "different from us" -- people who lived before the whale-hunting tradition that defines modern Tikigaq. The oral traditions do not claim that the modern community descends from the Ipiutak people; they acknowledge a discontinuity, a gap between the ancient settlement and the later occupations. The Ipiutak people were there before. They were not us.

The question of why Ipiutak disappeared is entangled with larger questions about cultural change in western Alaska during the first millennium CE. The period from roughly 500 to 1000 CE saw significant shifts in Arctic Alaska: the development of whale-hunting technology, the emergence of the Birnirk (BEER-nirk) culture (an immediate precursor to the Thule), and the increasing importance of whaling as an economic and social activity. The Ipiutak way of life -- focused on seal and caribou hunting, with elaborate burial ritual and a distinctive art style -- may have been displaced by a new way of life centered on whale hunting, which brought different technologies, different social organization, and different cultural values. The transition was not necessarily violent; it may have been a gradual shift in emphasis, with whaling communities growing in population and influence while non-whaling communities declined.

Alternatively, the Ipiutak people may have participated in the transition themselves -- adopting whale-hunting technology, reorganizing their social structure around the whale-hunting crew, and gradually abandoning the cultural traits (the elaborate burial art, the pottery-free toolkit, the distinctive stone-working tradition) that defined Ipiutak as a distinct culture. In this interpretation, the Ipiutak did not disappear; they changed. The people remained, but the culture transformed.

The ambiguity is itself significant. Archaeological cultures are defined by their artifacts -- their tools, their art, their architecture. When the artifacts change, the archaeologist declares a new "culture," even if the same population continues to live in the same place. The end of Ipiutak as an archaeological culture does not necessarily mean the end of the Ipiutak people. It may mean only that they stopped making the objects by which we identify them.

The death masks of Ipiutak are now housed in the American Museum of Natural History in New York City and the Danish National Museum in Copenhagen. They remain among the most compelling objects in Arctic archaeology -- faces of ivory with eyes of jet, staring across two thousand years of Arctic darkness, equipped for a journey whose destination we can only guess.
