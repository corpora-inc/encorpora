# Part Two: The Sea Peoples

## Chapter 3 — The Egyptian Record

Almost everything we know about the Sea Peoples comes from Egyptian sources. The Egyptians fought the Sea Peoples twice -- once under Merneptah around 1208 BCE and once under Ramesses III around 1177 BCE -- and both pharaohs commemorated their victories in monumental inscriptions that have survived.

The most important record is at Medinet Habu (meh-DEE-net HAH-boo), the mortuary temple of Ramesses III on the west bank of the Nile at Luxor (ancient Thebes). Medinet Habu is one of the best-preserved temple complexes in Egypt, and its exterior walls are covered with enormous relief carvings and hieroglyphic inscriptions describing Ramesses III's military campaigns. The Sea Peoples reliefs occupy prominent positions on the temple's north exterior wall, depicting both a land battle and a naval battle in vivid detail.

The naval battle scene is one of the most dramatic works of ancient Egyptian art. Egyptian warships, manned by archers, engage enemy vessels in close combat. The Sea Peoples' ships are depicted differently from Egyptian vessels -- they have high prows and sterns, sometimes with bird-head ornaments, and their warriors wear distinctive feathered headdresses or horned helmets. The Egyptian archers rain arrows on the enemy from the decks of their ships, while grappling hooks are used to pull enemy vessels alongside for boarding. The sea is shown full of capsized ships and drowning warriors. The scene is propaganda -- a pharaoh's celebration of his own military triumph -- but it is also evidence, providing visual information about the appearance, equipment, and fighting methods of the Sea Peoples.

The reliefs reward close study. The Egyptian ships are standardized -- oar-powered warships with a single mast and a crew of archers and marines positioned along the gunwales. The Sea Peoples' vessels vary in design: some have the bird-head prows, others have high curving sterns, and at least one type appears to have a sail rather than oars as its primary means of propulsion. The warriors aboard the enemy ships carry round shields and long swords or spears, and their headdresses fall into two distinct types -- the feathered crown (a ring of upright feathers or reeds radiating from a headband) and the horned helmet (two curved projections rising from a close-fitting cap). The feathered headdress is associated specifically with the Peleset and the Tjeker. The horned helmet appears on the Sherden. These visual distinctions suggest that the Egyptians recognized different peoples among the attackers and depicted them accordingly.

The tactical details in the relief are specific enough to suggest eyewitness reporting. The Egyptian fleet appears to have used a coordinated strategy: archers on the shore and in the ships created overlapping fields of fire, while grappling hooks prevented the enemy vessels from maneuvering freely. The battle was fought in the mouths of the Nile Delta, where the shallow waters and narrow channels gave the advantage to the Egyptians, who knew the local conditions. The Sea Peoples' deep-water vessels, designed for open-sea sailing, would have been at a disadvantage in the confined waterways of the Delta. Ramesses III may have chosen the battlefield deliberately, luring the attackers into waters where their ships could not maneuver.

The land battle scene is equally revealing. The Sea Peoples are shown traveling in ox-carts with their families -- women and children riding in solid-wheeled wagons alongside the warriors. This detail is significant. A military raiding force does not bring its families. The presence of women and children suggests that the Sea Peoples were not merely conducting a military campaign but migrating -- moving their entire population from one place to another, seeking new land to settle. The ox-carts, the families, and the scale of the operation all suggest a mass migration rather than a military raid.

The hieroglyphic inscriptions at Medinet Habu provide the names of the attacking peoples and a narrative of the campaign. The key passage reads: "The foreign countries made a conspiracy in their islands. All at once the lands were removed and scattered in the fray. No land could stand before their arms: from Hatti, Kode, Carchemish, Arzawa, and Alashiya on, being cut off at one time. A camp was set up in Amurru. They desolated its people, and its land was like that which has never come into being. They were coming forward toward Egypt, while the flame was prepared before them."

This passage describes a wave of destruction moving from north to south -- from the Hittite homeland (Hatti), through the cities of the Levantine coast (Kode, Carchemish), through western Anatolia (Arzawa), through Cyprus (Alashiya), and into the Levant (Amurru), before reaching Egypt itself. The description is consistent with the archaeological evidence of sequential destruction across the Eastern Mediterranean. The Medinet Habu inscription is not merely a military record; it is a description of the collapse of the Bronze Age world as witnessed by the one great power that survived it.

The inscriptions name specific groups among the Sea Peoples. At Medinet Habu, the groups listed include the Peleset (PEL-eh-set), the Tjeker (CHEH-ker), the Shekelesh (SHEK-eh-lesh), the Denyen (DEN-yen), and the Weshesh (WEH-shesh). The earlier inscription of Merneptah lists additional names: the Sherden (SHER-den), the Lukka (LOO-kah), and the Ekwesh (EK-wesh). These are Egyptian renderings of foreign names, and their identification with known historical peoples is one of the central puzzles of Sea Peoples studies.


## Chapter 4 — Who Were They?

The names recorded by the Egyptians have been the subject of intense scholarly debate for over a century. Scholars have attempted to match the Egyptian names to known peoples, regions, or later historical groups -- with varying degrees of confidence.

The Peleset are the most confidently identified. After their defeat by Ramesses III, the Peleset settled on the southern coast of the Levant -- the region that became known as Philistia (fih-LISS-tee-ah) and eventually gave its name to Palestine. The Peleset are the Philistines of the Hebrew Bible -- the people who fought against the Israelites in the stories of Samson and David. The five Philistine cities -- Ashkelon (ASH-keh-lon), Ekron (EK-ron), Gath (GATH), Ashdod (ASH-dod), and Gaza (GAH-zah) -- formed a pentapolis, a league of five cities that controlled the fertile coastal plain and competed with the emerging Israelite polities in the highlands to the east.

Archaeological excavations at these cities have revealed a material culture that is distinctly different from the Canaanite culture of the surrounding region and that shows strong similarities to Aegean (Greek) material culture -- Mycenaean-style pottery, Aegean-style cooking practices, and architectural features that resemble Aegean prototypes. Ekron, in particular, became a major industrial center during the Iron Age, with an olive oil production facility containing over a hundred olive presses -- one of the largest industrial installations in the ancient Near East. The Philistines were not merely warriors. They were builders, farmers, and industrialists who transformed the coastal plain into one of the most productive agricultural regions in the Levant. The Philistines appear to have been of Aegean origin -- migrants from the Mycenaean world or the islands of the eastern Mediterranean who settled in the Levant after the collapse.

Recent ancient DNA analysis has provided additional evidence. A 2019 study led by Michal Feldman analyzed genomes from a Philistine cemetery at Ashkelon dating to the twelfth and eleventh centuries BCE. The earliest Philistine individuals showed significant European genetic ancestry -- consistent with a migration from the Aegean region. The European genetic signal diminished in later generations, indicating that the Philistine migrants intermarried with the local Canaanite population and were gradually absorbed. The genetic evidence supports the archaeological evidence: the Philistines originated somewhere in the Aegean or eastern Mediterranean and migrated to the Levant during the Bronze Age collapse.

The Philistine settlement archaeology is revealing in its details. The earliest Philistine levels at Ashkelon, Ekron, and other sites show a dramatic break with the preceding Canaanite culture. The pottery changes abruptly -- from local Canaanite forms to Mycenaean-style decorated ware, produced locally but following Aegean models. Cooking practices shift: pig bones appear in large quantities in Philistine levels (pigs were raised and consumed in the Aegean but were uncommon in Canaanite dietary practice). Loom weights change from the Canaanite type to an Aegean type, suggesting different weaving traditions. Hearth styles shift from the Canaanite tabun (tah-BOON, a cylindrical clay oven) to the Aegean-style open hearth placed in the center of the main room. Even the architecture changes: early Philistine buildings include features resembling Aegean megaron-style halls, with a central hearth and columned entrance.

These are not the traces of a military conquest. A conquering army does not change the cooking pots, the loom weights, and the hearth placement of the conquered population. These are the traces of a population replacement -- families arriving with their own domestic traditions and rebuilding their daily lives in a new land. The Philistines brought their culture with them: their pottery styles, their food preferences, their textile techniques, their architectural traditions. Over the following generations, these Aegean traditions gradually blended with local Canaanite practices, producing a hybrid Philistine culture that was neither purely Aegean nor purely Levantine.

The Sherden are sometimes identified with Sardinia -- the island in the western Mediterranean. The name resembles "Sardinia" (Sardinian: Sardigna), and some scholars have proposed that the Sherden were from or settled on the island. However, the Sherden appear in Egyptian records as mercenaries in the Egyptian army before the Sea Peoples crisis, suggesting that their relationship with Egypt was more complex than simple invasion. Some Sherden fought for Ramesses III; others fought against him. They may have been a mercenary people who served multiple masters.

The Shekelesh are sometimes identified with the Siculi -- the people who gave their name to Sicily. The identification is based on the similarity of the names, but there is no archaeological evidence to confirm it. The Lukka are often identified with Lycia (LIH-shee-ah), a region in southwestern Anatolia (modern Turkey), where the Lukka lands are mentioned in Hittite texts as a troublesome, semi-independent territory. The Denyen have been connected to the Danaans (dah-NAY-ahns) -- one of Homer's names for the Greeks -- though this identification is speculative. The Ekwesh have been connected to the Ahhiyawa (ah-hee-YAH-wah), a western Anatolian or Aegean power mentioned in Hittite texts that some scholars identify with the Achaeans (ah-KEE-ahnz), another Homeric name for the Greeks.

The Tjeker settled on the coast of the Levant, like the Peleset, and are mentioned in the Egyptian story of Wenamun (WEN-ah-moon) -- an account of an Egyptian official's journey to Byblos around 1075 BCE, in which the Tjeker are described as controlling the port of Dor on the coast of modern Israel. The Weshesh are the least identifiable of the groups -- their name has not been convincingly matched to any known people or region.

The identifications range from fairly confident (the Peleset as Philistines) to highly speculative (the Denyen as Danaans, the Shekelesh as Siculi). The fundamental problem is that the Egyptian names are transliterations of foreign words into hieroglyphic writing, which does not represent vowels consistently and may not preserve the original pronunciation of the names. Matching Egyptian transliterations to names known from other sources -- Greek, Hittite, or later historical records -- involves assumptions about pronunciation, language change, and geographical connections that cannot be verified independently.

A deeper question underlies the identification problem: were the Sea Peoples a coordinated alliance, a loose confederation, or simply a collection of disparate groups swept up in the same historical crisis? The Egyptian records describe them as a conspiracy -- "the foreign countries made a conspiracy in their islands" -- but this may be Egyptian propaganda rather than accurate description. Egyptian royal inscriptions routinely portrayed foreign threats as vast, coordinated conspiracies against the cosmic order that Egypt represented -- a literary convention designed to magnify the pharaoh's victory. A confederation of diverse peoples coordinating a simultaneous attack across the entire Eastern Mediterranean would require a level of organization that seems implausible for the period -- no shared language, no central command structure, no logistical system capable of coordinating movements across thousands of kilometers.

More likely, the "Sea Peoples" were a label applied by the Egyptians to a diverse collection of groups -- migrants, refugees, raiders, and displaced populations -- who arrived at Egypt's borders from different directions and for different reasons, united by nothing more than the crisis that had set them in motion. Some may have been organized military forces. Others may have been displaced farming communities seeking new land. Still others may have been opportunistic raiders taking advantage of the chaos to attack weakened targets. The Egyptian scribes, encountering unfamiliar peoples arriving simultaneously from multiple directions, grouped them under a single category and imposed a narrative of coordinated invasion that may have had little relationship to reality on the ground.

Assaf Yasur-Landau (AH-sahf yah-SOOR lahn-DOW), in his 2010 study *The Philistines and Aegean Migration at the End of the Late Bronze Age*, has argued that the Philistine migration was a genuine population movement -- families traveling with their possessions, seeking new land to settle -- comparable to later historical migrations like the Anglo-Saxon migration to Britain or the Volkerwanderung (migration of peoples) that contributed to the fall of the Roman Empire. The ox-carts depicted at Medinet Habu support this interpretation. These were not raiders returning to a homeland after a campaign. They were people moving permanently, carrying everything they owned.


## Chapter 5 — The Theories

The question of what caused the Bronze Age collapse -- and what role the Sea Peoples played in it -- has generated a rich and contentious scholarly literature. Multiple theories have been proposed, and the current consensus (if it can be called that) is that no single cause explains the collapse. Instead, multiple factors interacted to produce a cascading failure.

The invasion theory is the oldest and most straightforward. In this view, the Sea Peoples were a powerful military force -- a coalition of warrior peoples from the western Mediterranean and the Aegean -- who attacked and destroyed the Bronze Age civilizations of the Eastern Mediterranean through military conquest. The theory was dominant for much of the twentieth century, and it has the advantage of fitting the Egyptian evidence: the Medinet Habu reliefs depict a military confrontation, and the inscriptions describe the Sea Peoples as destroyers of nations. Robert Drews, in his 1993 book *The End of the Bronze Age*, argued that the collapse was caused by a revolution in military tactics -- the replacement of chariot-based warfare (which the Bronze Age powers had perfected) with infantry-based warfare using new weapons and tactics that the established powers could not counter. In Drews's view, the Sea Peoples were the first practitioners of this new form of warfare, and their military superiority explains the rapidity and comprehensiveness of the collapse.

The invasion theory has weaknesses. Many of the destroyed sites show no clear evidence of military assault -- no arrow points embedded in walls, no mass graves, no defensive measures hastily erected. Some destructions appear to have been caused by fire, but fire can result from earthquake, accident, or deliberate abandonment as well as from military attack. And the theory does not explain why the destruction was so geographically comprehensive: if the Sea Peoples were a military force from a specific region, why did they attack every major power in the Eastern Mediterranean simultaneously? The chariot-based armies of the Late Bronze Age were formidable military forces -- the Battle of Kadesh alone involved thousands of chariots on each side. It is difficult to explain how a collection of migrating peoples, however skilled in infantry warfare, could have simultaneously defeated every major military power in the region. Drews's theory of a military revolution is elegant, but the archaeological evidence for a specific change in warfare tactics at exactly this moment is thin.

The earthquake theory notes that the Eastern Mediterranean is one of the most seismically active regions in the world. The Anatolian, Aegean, and Dead Sea fault systems are capable of producing devastating earthquakes, and geological evidence suggests that the period around 1200 BCE was a period of heightened seismic activity. Amos Nur, a Stanford geophysicist, proposed in 2008 that an "earthquake storm" -- a sequence of large earthquakes occurring over a period of decades along connected fault systems -- could explain the pattern of destruction. The concept of an earthquake storm is based on the observation that large earthquakes along a fault system can trigger subsequent earthquakes at adjacent points along the same fault, creating a cascading sequence of seismic events over a period of years or decades. Modern examples exist: the North Anatolian Fault in Turkey experienced a series of major earthquakes between 1939 and 1999, progressively migrating westward along the fault toward Istanbul.

Several of the destroyed sites (including Mycenae, Tiryns, and various Levantine cities) show destruction layers that are consistent with earthquake damage: collapsed walls, sheared stone blocks, and debris patterns that suggest seismic rather than military destruction. At Tiryns, the lower town shows evidence of earthquake destruction followed by rebuilding, followed by a second destruction -- a sequence consistent with repeated seismic events. At Mycenae, the massive Cyclopean walls show evidence of structural damage that is more consistent with ground shaking than with military assault.

The earthquake theory explains some of the evidence but not all of it. Earthquakes destroy buildings, but they do not destroy civilizations. A society that experiences an earthquake rebuilds -- unless other factors prevent recovery. The earthquake theory works best as a contributing factor rather than a sole cause: earthquakes may have damaged cities and disrupted infrastructure, making the affected societies more vulnerable to other pressures (invasion, drought, trade disruption) that completed the collapse.

The climate change theory draws on paleoclimatic evidence suggesting that the Eastern Mediterranean experienced a prolonged drought during the late thirteenth and early twelfth centuries BCE. Pollen cores from sediment in the Sea of Galilee, the Dead Sea, and other locations show evidence of reduced rainfall during this period. The evidence is compelling: a study by Dafna Langgut and colleagues published in 2013, analyzing pollen from a core drilled in the Sea of Galilee, found a sharp decline in olive and oak pollen beginning around 1250 BCE -- indicating reduced precipitation and agricultural stress across the region. The decline persisted for roughly 150 years, overlapping precisely with the period of the collapse.

A prolonged drought would have had cascading effects across the Bronze Age system. Reduced agricultural yields would have stressed food supplies, potentially triggering famine in regions that depended on dry farming (growing crops without irrigation). Egypt, which depended on the Nile flood rather than rainfall, might have been less directly affected -- but the Hittite Empire, Mycenaean Greece, and the rain-fed agricultural zones of the Levant would have suffered severely. Grain shortages in Anatolia and Greece could have triggered the very population movements that the Egyptian records describe: peoples displaced by failing harvests migrating southward toward the still-productive lands of the Nile Delta and the irrigated zones of the Levant. The Hittite records contain letters requesting emergency grain shipments from Egypt -- evidence that food stress was already affecting the system before the final collapse.

The drought may have been the underlying pressure that set populations in motion: peoples displaced by failing harvests migrating in search of more productive land, becoming the "Sea Peoples" in the process.

The systems collapse theory, most fully articulated by Eric Cline in his 2014 book *1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed*, argues that no single factor caused the collapse. Instead, the Bronze Age world was a complex system -- a network of interdependent states, trade routes, and economic relationships -- that was vulnerable to cascading failure. In a complex system, small disruptions can have large consequences when the components of the system are tightly coupled. If the tin supply from Afghanistan was interrupted (by political instability along the trade route, by drought, or by conflict), the bronze workshops of the Levant and Greece could not produce the tools and weapons that their economies depended on. If the grain supply from Egypt was disrupted, the cities of the Levant that depended on Egyptian grain imports would face famine. If the trade routes that carried copper from Cyprus were cut, the entire system would be starved of its most important industrial resource.

Cline argues that the Bronze Age collapse was a "perfect storm" -- a convergence of earthquakes, drought, trade disruption, internal rebellions, and external attacks that, individually, might have been survivable but collectively overwhelmed the system's ability to adapt. The Sea Peoples, in this view, were not the cause of the collapse but one of its symptoms -- populations displaced by the breakdown of the system, migrating in search of new homes and contributing to the destruction as they went. The collapse was not caused by a single catastrophe but by the cumulative failure of an interconnected world.

Cline draws an analogy to a modern concept from complexity theory: the idea that complex systems can experience sudden, catastrophic failure when multiple stresses exceed the system's resilience threshold. Below that threshold, the system absorbs shocks and continues to function. Above it, the system collapses rapidly and comprehensively -- a phase transition from order to chaos. The Bronze Age world, in Cline's analysis, was a complex system operating near its resilience threshold: it could survive a drought, or an earthquake, or a disruption of the tin trade, or a military invasion -- but it could not survive all of them at once. The simultaneous occurrence of multiple stresses pushed the system past its breaking point, and the collapse, once it began, was self-reinforcing: each destroyed city, each severed trade route, each displaced population made the next destruction more likely.

The systems collapse theory is the most widely accepted framework among current scholars, though it is not universally embraced. Some archaeologists argue that it is too vague -- that saying "everything caused everything" is not really an explanation. Others argue that specific local factors (invasion in some places, earthquake in others, drought in still others) are more important than the systemic framework. The debate continues, driven by new evidence from excavations, paleoclimate studies, and the analysis of ancient texts.

What is clear is that the Bronze Age collapse was real, rapid, and comprehensive. The world that existed in 1250 BCE -- interconnected, prosperous, literate, and diplomatically sophisticated -- was gone by 1150 BCE. The causes were multiple. The consequences were enormous. And the nameless peoples who moved through the wreckage, settling in the ruins of destroyed cities and building new communities on the ashes of the old, remain, after three thousand years, among the most mysterious actors in human history.
