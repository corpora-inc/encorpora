# A Note to the Reader

Around 1200 BCE, the world ended. Not the whole world -- but the world that mattered to the people living in it. The Eastern Mediterranean in the thirteenth century BCE was an interconnected system of great powers: Egypt, the Hittite Empire, Mycenaean Greece, the trading cities of the Levantine coast, Cyprus, Assyria, Babylon. These states traded with each other, fought with each other, and corresponded with each other in multiple languages across thousands of kilometers. They were linked by trade routes that carried tin from Afghanistan, copper from Cyprus, gold from Egypt, and amber from the Baltic. It was, by the standards of its time, a globalized world.

Within roughly fifty years, starting around 1200 BCE, this world collapsed. The Hittite Empire was destroyed. Mycenaean civilization fell. The great trading city of Ugarit was burned to the ground. Troy was destroyed. Dozens of cities across the Levant were razed. Egypt survived -- barely -- and recorded what hit them.

On the walls of his mortuary temple at Medinet Habu, the pharaoh Ramesses III (RAM-eh-seez) carved images of a massive naval and land battle fought around 1177 BCE. The inscriptions name the attackers: the Peleset (PEL-eh-set), the Tjeker (CHEH-ker), the Shekelesh (SHEK-eh-lesh), the Denyen (DEN-yen), the Weshesh (WEH-shesh). These are the Sea Peoples -- and we do not know who they were.

This is the story of the nameless invaders who ended the Bronze Age.
