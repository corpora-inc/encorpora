# Part One: The Anonymous Metropolis

## Chapter 1 — No Name, No Language, No Script

Begin with the silence.

Most of the great cities of the ancient world left their names behind. Babylon means "Gate of God" in Akkadian. Athens is named for its patron goddess. Rome's origin story -- Romulus, the wolf, the seven hills -- is one of the most famous tales in Western civilization. Even cities whose scripts remain undeciphered left records in the scripts of their neighbors: the Etruscans are named in Greek and Latin sources; the Harappan cities appear in Mesopotamian trade records.

Teotihuacan left nothing. No inscription in any language names the city or its people. No neighboring civilization recorded their name in a way that scholars can confidently link to the archaeological site. The Maya, who interacted with Teotihuacan extensively, referred to it with a glyph that scholars read as *puh* -- "Place of Reeds" -- a term that may be a proper name or may be a generic title used for any great city. The Aztecs, who arrived in central Mexico roughly seven centuries after Teotihuacan's fall, gave it the Nahuatl name we use today, but the Aztecs had no more direct knowledge of the city's builders than a modern tourist does.

The result is one of the strangest situations in the study of the ancient world. We are dealing with a city that, at its peak around 450 CE, was among the six or seven largest on the planet. Its influence extended across Mesoamerica. Its art, architecture, and religious symbols appeared at sites over a thousand kilometers away. Foreign communities built neighborhoods within its walls. Its military or diplomatic agents intervened in the politics of the Maya lowlands. It was, by any measure, one of the most important places in the Western Hemisphere for over five centuries.

And we do not know its name.

![View down the Avenue of the Dead toward the Pyramid of the Moon](../images/placeholders/01-avenue-of-the-dead.png)

The language question is equally frustrating. Teotihuacan's population of 125,000 to 200,000 people certainly spoke a language -- probably more than one, given the multi-ethnic character of the city. Scholars have proposed several candidates. Nahuatl, the language of the later Aztecs and one of the most widely spoken language families in Mesoamerica, is one possibility, though the linguistic evidence for Nahuatl speakers in central Mexico this early is debated. Totonac (toh-toh-NAHK), a language family spoken in the Gulf Coast region of Veracruz, has been proposed by some scholars based on phonological clues in place names. Otomí (oh-toh-MEE), a language family still spoken in central Mexico, is another candidate. Mixe-Zoquean (MEE-heh soh-KEH-an), the language family associated with the Olmec, has been proposed for the city's earlier phases. The honest answer is: we do not know, and without a deciphered writing system or clear linguistic evidence, we may never know.

The writing question is the most tantalizing. Did the Teotihuacanos have a writing system? The answer is: probably, but the evidence is ambiguous and hotly debated.

The city's art is rich in symbols. Murals, pottery, stone carvings, and other objects carry what appear to be glyphs -- small, standardized symbols that recur across the site and across media. Karl Taube, one of the leading scholars of Teotihuacan iconography, has identified a set of signs that appear to function as a notation system: symbols for place names, personal names, calendrical dates, and possibly speech or sound. Some of these signs resemble elements of later Mesoamerican writing systems, particularly the Aztec system, which was partially logographic (using pictures to represent words or concepts).

The problem is scale. The Maya had a fully developed writing system -- capable of recording historical narratives, royal genealogies, astronomical observations, and mythological texts in complete sentences. The Zapotecs of Monte Albán had a writing system that recorded dates, names, and conquered places. Even the Olmec may have had an early writing system (the Cascajal Block, discussed in Volume 3). Teotihuacan's notational system, if it exists, appears to be more limited -- or perhaps less well preserved. The city's primary medium of visual communication was the mural, and Teotihuacan's murals are among the richest in the ancient Americas. But murals are pictorial, not necessarily textual. A mural depicting a ritual scene can communicate meaning without using writing in the technical sense.

Some scholars argue that the absence of confirmed writing at Teotihuacan reflects a genuine cultural choice -- that the city's leaders deliberately avoided the kind of personalized inscriptions that characterized the Maya world, where rulers proclaimed their names, their victories, and their genealogies in carved stone. Teotihuacan's art is notably impersonal: it depicts gods, rituals, and symbols, but almost never depicts an identifiable individual human being. If Teotihuacan's political system was collective rather than individual -- if the city was governed by a council or a corporate group rather than a single king -- then the absence of personal inscriptions would be consistent with the political structure. You do not carve your name in stone if the system does not permit individual glorification.

Others argue that Teotihuacan almost certainly had writing and that the evidence simply has not survived. The city's buildings were plastered and painted -- ideal surfaces for writing -- but plaster deteriorates over 1,500 years. Bark-paper codices, the standard medium for Mesoamerican books, are perishable and would not survive in the Valley of Mexico's climate. The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

The population of Teotihuacan is itself worth pausing over. The estimates range from 125,000 to 200,000 at the city's peak, around 450 CE. Even the conservative end of this range places Teotihuacan among the largest cities in the world at that time. Constantinople, the capital of the Eastern Roman Empire, had perhaps 400,000 to 500,000. Alexandria in Egypt was comparable. The great cities of the Gupta Empire in India -- Pataliputra, Ujjain -- may have been of similar size. In the Americas, nothing else was close. The Classic Maya cities, impressive as they were, typically housed populations in the tens of thousands. Teotihuacan was an order of magnitude larger than its nearest Mesoamerican rival.

The location mattered. The city sits in a high, semi-arid valley -- the northeast arm of the Basin of Mexico, at an altitude of 2,300 meters. The valley is not obviously destined for a great city. It receives modest rainfall. The soil is volcanic, well-drained but not naturally rich. The nearest major body of water, Lake Texcoco, lies thirty kilometers to the southwest. What the valley does have is springs -- natural water sources fed by the volcanic geology of the region -- and obsidian. The Pachuca obsidian deposits, one of the richest sources of high-quality obsidian in Mesoamerica, lie northeast of the valley. Control of this resource may have been one of the economic foundations of the city's rise.

The anonymity of Teotihuacan is not merely an academic puzzle. It shapes everything we can and cannot say about the city. We cannot tell its history in the way we tell the history of Rome or the Maya kingdoms -- as a sequence of named rulers, documented wars, and recorded events. We tell it instead through architecture, art, archaeology, and analogy -- building a picture of the city from the physical evidence it left behind, filling the gaps with cautious inference from better-documented civilizations nearby. The story of Teotihuacan is a story told in objects and buildings rather than words.


## Chapter 2 — The Grid

The first thing that strikes a modern visitor to Teotihuacan is the order. The city is organized on a grid -- a precise, planned layout that extends across more than twenty square kilometers, one of the earliest and most complete examples of urban planning in the ancient Americas.

The central axis of the grid is the Avenue of the Dead -- a monumental boulevard running roughly north-south for 2.5 kilometers, and possibly as long as five kilometers if unexcavated extensions are included. The avenue is approximately forty meters wide, flanked on both sides by platforms, pyramids, and public buildings that step back from its edges in a carefully orchestrated sequence of architectural forms. The name "Avenue of the Dead" is another Aztec imposition -- the Aztecs, seeing the platforms lining the road, mistook them for tombs. They were almost certainly not tombs. They were platforms for temples, administrative buildings, and other public structures. But the name has stuck.

![Plan of Teotihuacan showing the Avenue of the Dead and the grid layout](../images/placeholders/02-city-grid-plan.png)

The Avenue of the Dead is not a road in the modern sense. It was not primarily a transportation corridor. It was a ceremonial axis -- a line drawn through the heart of the city, connecting its most important buildings and creating a sequence of visual experiences for anyone walking its length. From the south, looking north, the avenue frames the Pyramid of the Moon perfectly against the backdrop of Cerro Gordo (SEHR-roh GOR-doh), the mountain that rises behind the city's northern edge. The visual effect is deliberate: the pyramid echoes the shape of the mountain, as if the builders were completing in stone what nature had begun in rock. The mountain and the pyramid merge into a single form, connecting the built environment to the natural landscape, the human world to the world of the sacred.

The urban grid extends outward from the Avenue of the Dead in both directions. Streets run at right angles, creating a pattern of rectangular blocks. Within these blocks, the dominant residential form is the apartment compound -- a walled enclosure containing multiple rooms arranged around interior courtyards, housing anywhere from twenty to one hundred people. The city contained over two thousand of these compounds, each one a self-contained residential unit with its own cooking areas, storage rooms, ritual spaces, and drainage systems.

The apartment compounds are remarkable for their uniformity and their internal complexity. Each compound was enclosed by a solid exterior wall -- windowless on the outside, presenting a blank face to the street. Inside, rooms opened onto interior courtyards that provided light, air, and communal space. The courtyards often contained small shrines or altars where household rituals were performed. Drainage systems channeled rainwater from the courtyards into underground channels that ran beneath the streets. The buildings were constructed from a combination of local volcanic stone (tezontle -- teh-SOHN-tleh -- a lightweight, porous rock) and a concrete-like material made from volcanic gravel and lime, then finished with a smooth plaster surface that was often painted with murals.

The uniformity of the compounds suggests centralized planning. Someone -- or some institution -- decided that residential buildings would follow a standard form, and the decision was implemented across the entire city. This is urban planning on a scale that Europe would not achieve until the Roman colonial cities, and that the Americas would not see again until the Spanish colonial grid towns of the sixteenth century.

The city even altered its hydrology to serve the grid. The San Juan River, which flows through the valley, was channeled into a straight, stone-lined canal that follows the grid's orientation -- diverted from its natural course to conform to the city's geometry. The river was made to obey the plan. This is engineering on a monumental scale: not merely building on the landscape but reshaping the landscape itself, bending rivers to match streets, subordinating geography to geometry. The statement is clear: at Teotihuacan, human will took precedence over natural form.

René Millon (mee-YOHN), the American archaeologist who spent decades mapping the entire city in a project completed in 1973, produced the definitive plan of Teotihuacan. The Millon map covers the entire urban area at a resolution sufficient to identify individual apartment compounds, and it remains the foundation of all spatial analysis of the city. What the map reveals is the sheer density of habitation: Teotihuacan was not a ceremonial center with scattered surrounding villages, like some Mesoamerican sites. It was a true city -- a densely packed urban environment where people lived, worked, worshipped, and died in close proximity. The residential compounds are packed together, block after block, filling the grid with an urban density that would be recognizable to a resident of any modern city.

The grid also encoded multiculturalism. Millon identified several neighborhoods that appear to have housed ethnic enclaves -- communities of foreigners living within Teotihuacan's walls.

The Oaxacan barrio, in the western part of the city, contained apartment compounds with architectural features and pottery styles characteristic of the Zapotec culture of Oaxaca, over three hundred kilometers to the southeast. The residents used Zapotec-style tomb construction for their dead -- a practice found at Monte Albán but not in standard Teotihuacan burials. They made pottery in Zapotec forms. They appear to have maintained their cultural identity while living within a foreign city, much as immigrant communities in modern cities maintain distinctive foodways, religious practices, and architectural preferences.

The "Merchants' Barrio," on the eastern side, contained goods in Maya and Gulf Coast styles -- pottery, figurines, and other objects that link this neighborhood to the tropical lowlands hundreds of kilometers to the east and south. Whether the residents were Maya traders living in Teotihuacan, Teotihuacano merchants who specialized in lowland trade, or a mixed community is debated. The neighborhood's material culture is distinct from the rest of the city, suggesting a degree of cultural autonomy. The pottery includes "Maya-style" vessels -- round-sided bowls with polychrome decoration in forms that are common at Maya sites in Guatemala and Belize but rare in the Valley of Mexico. Someone in this neighborhood was either importing Maya pottery or making it from memory, maintaining a connection to a tropical world that lay hundreds of kilometers and several ecological zones away.

A third enclave -- the "Michoacán barrio" in the western part of the city -- has been tentatively identified based on pottery styles associated with western Mexico. If confirmed, it would extend the range of Teotihuacan's cosmopolitan reach even further, drawing people from the Pacific coast into the life of the metropolis.

These ethnic enclaves tell us something important about Teotihuacan: it was a cosmopolitan city, drawing people from across Mesoamerica. The Zapotec, the Maya, the Gulf Coast peoples -- representatives of multiple civilizations lived within its walls, maintaining their own traditions while participating in the life of the metropolis. This kind of ethnic diversity, in a city of over 100,000 people, implies sophisticated mechanisms of social organization -- systems for managing cultural difference, resolving disputes between communities, and integrating diverse populations into a functioning urban whole.

The murals that decorated the apartment compounds provide glimpses into the lives and beliefs of the city's residents. The best-preserved examples -- at Tepantitla (teh-pahn-TEET-lah), Tetitla, Atetelco, and other excavated compounds -- depict processions of priests, jaguars and coyotes in elaborate regalia, flowering trees dripping with water, and supernatural beings moving through landscapes of rivers and mountains. The colors are vivid: reds, blues, greens, and yellows, applied in flat areas bordered by strong black outlines. The style is distinctive -- frontal, stylized, and rhythmic, with figures arranged in registers or processions rather than in naturalistic scenes. Teotihuacan's muralists created a visual world that is immediately recognizable: once you have seen a Teotihuacan mural, you will never mistake one for the art of any other civilization.

![Cutaway view of a Teotihuacan apartment compound showing interior courtyards and murals](../images/placeholders/11-apartment-compound.png)

One mural from Tepantitla is particularly famous: a scene sometimes called "Tlalocan" (the paradise of the rain god), showing small human figures swimming, playing, and gathering flowers in a landscape of rivers and plants, while a large deity figure presides above. The scene has been interpreted as a depiction of the afterlife, a ritual gathering, or a mythological narrative. Whatever its specific meaning, it conveys an impression of abundance, vitality, and delight -- a vision of the good life as the Teotihuacanos understood it.

The grid, the compounds, the murals, and the ethnic enclaves together create a picture of a city that was orderly, dense, diverse, and visually rich -- a true urban civilization, with all the complexity that the word implies. Teotihuacan was not a collection of temples surrounded by scattered farming villages. It was a city where 125,000 people lived together, worked together, worshipped together, and -- as the murals suggest -- imagined together. That we do not know their name makes the achievement more remarkable, not less. They built one of the greatest cities in the ancient world and left us to figure out who they were from the buildings they left behind.


## Chapter 3 — The Pyramids

Three pyramids dominate the skyline of Teotihuacan. They are among the largest structures ever built in the ancient Americas, and each carries its own mystery.

The Pyramid of the Sun is the largest. Standing roughly sixty-five meters tall with a base measuring approximately 225 meters on each side, it is the third-largest pyramid in the world by volume -- smaller than the Great Pyramid of Giza and the Great Pyramid of Cholula (also in Mexico), but larger than any other pyramid in Egypt or the Americas. The pyramid was built in a single massive construction phase, probably in the first or second century CE, making it one of the earliest major structures at the site. Its construction required an estimated one million cubic meters of fill -- earth, rubble, and adobe brick, faced with cut stone and finished with painted plaster. The labor force required for this construction has been estimated at several thousand workers over a period of years.

![The Pyramid of the Sun with people climbing its steps for scale](../images/placeholders/03-pyramid-of-the-sun.png)

The pyramid's most extraordinary feature was discovered in 1971, when an archaeologist named Jorge Acosta noticed water seeping from the base of the pyramid's western staircase. Investigation revealed a natural cave and tunnel system running beneath the pyramid -- a lava tube, formed by ancient volcanic activity, that extends roughly one hundred meters into the earth beneath the structure. The cave had been modified and enlarged by human hands, its walls smoothed and its interior divided into chambers. Offerings had been placed inside, though the cave was badly looted before systematic archaeological investigation began.

The discovery transformed understanding of the Pyramid of the Sun. The cave was not an afterthought -- it was the reason the pyramid was built where it was. In Mesoamerican cosmology, caves were entrances to the underworld. They were places where the boundary between the human world and the supernatural world was thin, where gods and ancestors could be contacted, where the first humans emerged from the earth at the beginning of time. The Aztec word *Chicomoztoc* (chee-koh-MOHS-tohk) -- "Seven Caves" -- refers to the mythological origin place of the Nahua peoples. The presence of a natural cave beneath the Pyramid of the Sun suggests that the entire city of Teotihuacan may have been founded because of this cave. The sacred geography came first; the city followed.

If this interpretation is correct, then Teotihuacan's urban form was dictated by the location of a hole in the ground. The cave marked the spot as sacred. The pyramid was built over the cave to monumentalize the sacred site. The Avenue of the Dead was laid out relative to the pyramid. The grid of the city was aligned to the avenue. The entire twenty-square-kilometer metropolis, with its 2,000 apartment compounds and its 125,000 residents, was oriented by the position of a natural lava tube beneath a valley floor. Architecture followed geology, and urban planning followed religion.

The orientation of the grid itself contains a puzzle. The Avenue of the Dead does not run exactly north-south. It is offset from true north by approximately 15.5 degrees east. The entire grid follows this offset. Why? Several explanations have been proposed. The most intriguing is astronomical: the offset may align the avenue with the setting point of the Pleiades star cluster, which held calendar significance in Mesoamerican cultures. The Pleiades' appearance and disappearance marked agricultural cycles and were associated with important ritual dates. If the avenue was aligned to the Pleiades, then the city's grid was a stellar map -- its streets pointing toward a specific position in the sky, connecting the human city to the celestial order. Other scholars have proposed that the offset reflects the direction of water flow in the valley, or the position of the cave beneath the Pyramid of the Sun, or some other geographic feature. The question remains open, but the precision of the offset -- consistent across the entire urban grid -- indicates that it was deliberate, not accidental.

The pyramid was originally covered in painted plaster. When it was first built, it would not have looked like the gray stone ruin that visitors see today. It would have been a vivid, colored surface -- probably red, possibly decorated with mural imagery -- shining in the highland sun. A painted pyramid, rising sixty-five meters above a valley floor, visible from kilometers away, marking the sacred center of a city of 125,000 people: the visual impact would have been overwhelming. Today we see the skeleton. The original was dressed in color.

The "Pyramid of the Sun" is, like every other name at Teotihuacan, a misnomer. The Aztecs gave it this name because they believed the sun was created here. The pyramid may have had nothing to do with the sun. Its cave, its orientation, and its relationship to water sources suggest associations with the earth, the underworld, and the origins of the world. We do not know its original name. We call it what the Aztecs called it because we have no alternative.

The Pyramid of the Moon, at the northern end of the Avenue of the Dead, is smaller than the Pyramid of the Sun but arguably more visually striking. It was built in stages over several centuries, with at least seven construction phases identified by excavation. Each phase involved burying the previous structure inside a larger one, like a set of nesting dolls. The earliest phase dates to approximately 100 CE; the latest to perhaps 350 CE.

The pyramid's relationship to Cerro Gordo -- the mountain behind it -- is one of the most celebrated examples of sacred landscape architecture in Mesoamerica. Viewed from the south, along the Avenue of the Dead, the pyramid appears to merge with the mountain. The human-made structure echoes the shape of the natural one, creating a visual continuity between the built and natural worlds. This was intentional. The pyramid's proportions and position were calculated to produce this effect, aligning the human and the cosmic in a single visual field.

Excavations inside the Pyramid of the Moon, led by Saburo Sugiyama and Rubén Cabrera Castro beginning in 1998, revealed a sequence of increasingly elaborate dedicatory burials in the earlier construction phases. The burials included sacrificed individuals -- both humans and animals -- accompanied by rich offerings: obsidian figurines, greenstone objects, pyrite mirrors, and the remains of eagles, pumas, wolves, and rattlesnakes. One burial contained a seated male figure, his hands bound behind his back, surrounded by the skeletons of animals arranged in symbolic positions. The animal symbolism is significant: eagles are associated with the sun and the upper world; pumas and wolves with warfare; serpents with the earth and the underworld. The burials embedded these cosmological associations into the fabric of the pyramid itself, transforming the structure from a mere platform into a repository of sacred power.

The third pyramid -- the Temple of the Feathered Serpent (also called the Pyramid of the Feathered Serpent, or in older literature the Temple of Quetzalcoatl, though the latter uses an Aztec name that may not have applied in Teotihuacan's time) -- is smaller than the other two but contains the most terrifying discovery in the city. It stands at the southern end of a large enclosed plaza called the Ciudadela (syoo-dah-DEH-lah, "The Citadel," another colonial-era name), and its facade was once covered in elaborate stone carvings of feathered serpents and a goggle-eyed figure -- possibly a rain deity, possibly a war deity, possibly a representation of time itself -- alternating in projecting stone heads along the pyramid's stepped profile.

![The carved facade of the Temple of the Feathered Serpent](../images/placeholders/04-feathered-serpent-temple.png)

The feathered serpent imagery links Teotihuacan to one of the most widespread religious symbols in Mesoamerican civilization -- the plumed serpent, known to the Aztecs as Quetzalcoatl (keht-sahl-KOH-ahtl) and to the Maya as Kukulkan (koo-kool-KAHN). The serpent combines two realms: the feathers of a bird (the upper world, the sky) and the body of a snake (the lower world, the earth). The combination is a visual statement of cosmic wholeness, the union of above and below. The feathered serpent at Teotihuacan is one of the earliest monumental depictions of this symbol, suggesting that the city may have been an important center for the development of the theology that the feathered serpent represents.

The Ciudadela plaza in which the Temple sits is itself a significant structure. The enclosed space -- roughly 400 meters on a side, surrounded by raised platforms -- could have held tens of thousands of people. If the temple was the focus of public ritual, then the Ciudadela was the theater in which the ritual was performed. The scale suggests events designed to be witnessed by a large portion of the city's population -- mass ceremonies, public sacrifices, communal observances of the calendar. The Ciudadela may have been Teotihuacan's equivalent of a Roman forum or an Athenian agora: a civic space where the community gathered to enact its collective identity.

The relationship between the three pyramids defines the sacred geography of the city. The Pyramid of the Sun, built over the cave, connects the city to the underworld. The Pyramid of the Moon, echoing the mountain, connects the city to the earth and the sky. The Temple of the Feathered Serpent, with its imagery of the plumed serpent, connects the realms above and below. Together, the three structures create a cosmological triangle -- a spatial arrangement that maps the structure of the Mesoamerican cosmos onto the landscape of the valley. The city is not just a place to live. It is a model of the universe.
