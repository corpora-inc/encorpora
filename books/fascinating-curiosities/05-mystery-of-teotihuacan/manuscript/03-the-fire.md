# Part Three: The Fire

## Chapter 7 — The Burning

Sometime around 550 CE -- give or take a few decades -- the ceremonial center of Teotihuacan was deliberately and systematically burned.

The evidence is unambiguous. The principal temples along the Avenue of the Dead show clear signs of intense fire: charred beams, melted plaster, heat-reddened stone, and thick layers of ash. The burning was thorough and selective. It targeted the temples, the public buildings, and the elite residential compounds along the avenue. The ordinary residential neighborhoods -- the apartment compounds where most of the population lived -- were largely untouched. The fire destroyed the symbols of power, not the homes of the people.

The selectivity is the key evidence. A city-wide conflagration -- a fire caused by accident, or by an invading army intent on total destruction -- would burn everything. The 550 CE fire burned almost exclusively the ceremonial core: the temples, the palaces, the public ritual spaces. The apartment compounds that housed the general population were left standing. Whoever set these fires knew what they were burning and why. The fires were targeted at the institutions, not the people.

![Charred remains of a temple along the Avenue of the Dead](../images/placeholders/08-burned-temple.png)

Three hypotheses compete to explain the burning.

The first is internal revolution. In this reading, the burning was an uprising by the city's own population against its ruling elite. The temples and palaces represented the authority of the rulers; destroying them was an act of political overthrow. The fact that residential neighborhoods were spared supports this interpretation -- the rebels lived in those neighborhoods. They were burning the system that governed them, not their own homes.

The parallel with San Lorenzo in Volume 3 is striking. Around 900 BCE, the Olmec ceremonial center at San Lorenzo was systematically dismantled: its monuments were defaced, buried, and destroyed, apparently by the site's own inhabitants. At Monte Albán, discussed in Volume 1, the ceremonial center was abandoned around 700 CE under circumstances that suggest a similar loss of political authority. At Tula, discussed in Volume 6, the ceremonial center would be burned around 1150 CE in circumstances that echo Teotihuacan's destruction almost exactly. The pattern recurs across Mesoamerican history: ceremonial centers built, used for centuries, and then destroyed by their own people. The destruction is not random. It is aimed at the symbols and structures of power. It is political.

If the burning of Teotihuacan was an internal revolution, it raises the question of what triggered it. The environmental evidence provides one possible answer: around 535-536 CE, a massive volcanic eruption (possibly Ilopango in El Salvador, or an eruption in the northern hemisphere) ejected enormous quantities of ash into the atmosphere, causing a "volcanic winter" that reduced temperatures, disrupted agriculture, and caused crop failures across the Northern Hemisphere. The event is recorded in Chinese, Roman, and other historical sources, and tree-ring data confirms a period of abnormally cold conditions in the mid-sixth century. If the eruption's effects reached the Valley of Mexico -- reducing maize yields, causing food shortages -- the resulting stress could have undermined the legitimacy of Teotihuacan's rulers. When the corn fails, the social contract fails with it. A ruling class that promises cosmic order and delivers famine does not rule for long.

The second hypothesis is foreign invasion. An outside military force -- from the Gulf Coast, from the Maya lowlands, from the northern frontier -- conquered the city and destroyed its religious infrastructure. The selectivity of the burning, in this view, reflects the invaders' strategy: destroy the symbols of the old regime's authority while leaving the general population in place to be governed. This is a standard tactic of ancient conquest -- you burn the temple of the defeated god but leave the farmers in their fields.

The problem with the invasion hypothesis is the absence of evidence for the invaders. No foreign army left its calling card. No new architectural style or ceramic tradition replaced Teotihuacan's at the burned site. No foreign artifacts appear in the destruction layers. If an invading force burned the city, it left no trace of its own identity -- an unusual omission for conquerors, who typically mark their victory.

The third hypothesis is ritual destruction -- the deliberate burning of the ceremonial center as a religious act, carried out by the city's own leaders. In Mesoamerican cosmology, the destruction and recreation of the world is a recurring theme. The Aztec creation myth describes four previous worlds, each destroyed and replaced by a new one. The ritual "killing" of temples -- through burning, burial, or dismantlement -- was practiced across Mesoamerica as a way of ending one cosmic cycle and beginning another. In this reading, the 550 CE fire was not a catastrophe but a ceremony: the old order was burned so that a new order could be born.

The ritual hypothesis has difficulties of its own. If the burning was planned and voluntary, why was the city not rebuilt on the same scale? After the fire, Teotihuacan's population declined, its monumental construction ceased, and its influence waned. A ritual destruction should lead to renewal. The 550 CE fire led instead to a long decline. If it was a ritual, it was one that went wrong -- or one whose intended renewal was hijacked by political and environmental forces that the ritualists did not anticipate.

The fire left physical traces that archaeologists can read with precision. Charcoal from burned beams provides radiocarbon dates that cluster around the mid-sixth century. Heat patterns on stone walls indicate the intensity of the fire -- temperatures high enough to turn the plaster coating the walls into powder and to redden the underlying stone to a depth of several centimeters. The accumulation of ash in some buildings is over a meter thick, indicating prolonged burning of structures that had been packed with combustible materials -- possibly deliberately, to ensure complete destruction. The Temple of the Feathered Serpent, whose carved facade was one of the wonders of the ancient city, was defaced during this period: many of the projecting serpent heads were deliberately smashed, their fragments left on the ground at the base of the pyramid. The destruction was thorough, targeted, and personal -- the destruction of art, not just architecture.

The most probable explanation combines elements of all three hypotheses. Internal tensions -- exacerbated by environmental stress, possibly triggered by the volcanic winter of the 530s -- led to a political crisis. The crisis expressed itself in the destruction of the ceremonial center, an act that was simultaneously political (overthrowing the ruling elite), religious (ending a cosmic cycle), and practical (destroying the physical infrastructure of the old power). The revolution may have been partially ritualized -- carried out according to Mesoamerican conventions of how you end an era -- while also being genuinely violent and politically transformative.

The archaeological evidence for the 535-536 CE volcanic event is growing. Ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica show sulfate deposits consistent with a major eruption. Tree rings from across the Northern Hemisphere show dramatically reduced growth in 536-540 CE. Chinese historical records describe a "dust veil" that dimmed the sun. The Byzantine historian Procopius wrote that the sun "gave forth its light without brightness, like the moon, during this whole year." If this global event reached the Valley of Mexico -- and there is no reason to think the Valley was exempt from a hemispheric climate disruption -- then Teotihuacan's agricultural system would have been stressed at precisely the moment when the city's institutions were most vulnerable.

The timing is suggestive: a global climate shock in the late 530s, followed by the burning of Teotihuacan's ceremonial center around 550. Fifteen years between the crisis and the response -- time enough for failed harvests to erode the population's faith in their leaders, for internal factions to organize, for the social contract to fray beyond repair. The fire, in this reading, was the end of a process that the volcanic winter began.

What is clear is that the fire of 550 CE was a turning point. The Teotihuacan that existed before the fire -- the cosmopolitan metropolis of 125,000 people, the center of a Mesoamerican-wide network of influence -- did not exist afterward. The fire ended an era.


## Chapter 8 — The Aftermath

Teotihuacan did not empty overnight. The fire of 550 CE destroyed the ceremonial core, but people continued to live at the site for centuries afterward. The population declined -- from 125,000 or more at its peak to perhaps 30,000 in the centuries after the fire, and eventually to a few thousand in the terminal phase. The mound construction stopped. The long-distance connections weakened. The apartment compounds continued to be inhabited, but the city's identity as a major power was over.

The decline of Teotihuacan sent shockwaves across Mesoamerica. For over five centuries, Teotihuacan had been the dominant force in the region -- the largest city, the most influential culture, the center of a web of trade and diplomacy that touched every corner of the Mesoamerican world. Its green obsidian, its talud-tablero architecture, its ceramic styles, and its religious iconography had been markers of prestige and power across thousands of kilometers. When Teotihuacan fell, the system it had anchored fell with it. Trade networks collapsed or reorganized. Political alliances that had been structured around Teotihuacan's influence dissolved. Communities that had defined themselves in relation to the great city -- as allies, as tributaries, as trading partners -- were suddenly adrift. The period from roughly 550 to 700 CE is sometimes called the "Epiclassic" -- a transitional era characterized by political fragmentation, population movement, and the rise of new regional powers that filled the vacuum Teotihuacan's collapse had created.

The term "Epiclassic" itself captures the scholars' uncertainty about how to categorize this period. "Classic" refers to the era of Teotihuacan's dominance. "Epi-" means "after" or "upon." The Epiclassic is the period that came after the Classic -- a time of transition, experimentation, and competition, when the Mesoamerican world was reorganizing itself in the absence of its former center. The great achievements of the Epiclassic -- the art of Xochicalco, the architecture of Cacaxtla, the ball courts of El Tajín -- are impressive in their own right, but they exist in the shadow of what had been lost.

In the Basin of Mexico, the city of Xochicalco (soh-chee-KAHL-koh) rose to prominence, built on a fortified hilltop in what is now the state of Morelos. Xochicalco's art combines Teotihuacan, Maya, and Zapotec elements -- a synthesis of styles that reflects the cosmopolitan character of the post-Teotihuacan world, when people and ideas from multiple traditions mixed in new and creative ways. Cholula (choh-LOO-lah), east of the Basin of Mexico in what is now the state of Puebla, continued to grow and would eventually contain the largest pyramid by volume in the world -- the Great Pyramid of Cholula, larger at its base than the Great Pyramid of Giza. Cholula may have absorbed some of Teotihuacan's population and inherited some of its prestige.

Further east, the site of Cacaxtla (kah-KAHSH-tlah) in the state of Tlaxcala produced some of the most extraordinary murals in Mesoamerican art -- battle scenes showing warriors in Maya and Teotihuacan regalia, painted with a sophistication that suggests artists trained in multiple traditions. Cacaxtla's murals are a visual record of the Epiclassic's cosmopolitan character: a world where the boundaries between cultural traditions were fluid, where artists combined elements from multiple civilizations into new and original works.

In the Maya lowlands, Teotihuacan's withdrawal was followed by a flowering of independent kingdoms. The Classic Maya period (roughly 250-900 CE) is often described as a time of city-state competition -- dozens of independent kingdoms, each with its own ruling dynasty, competing for territory and prestige. Teotihuacan's presence in the Maya world had constrained this competition; its absence liberated it. The great Maya cities of the Late Classic -- Palenque, Copán, Calakmul, and above all Tikal -- flourished in the centuries after Teotihuacan's fall, producing the extraordinary art, architecture, and inscriptions that define the Classic Maya period in the popular imagination.

The Aztecs encountered Teotihuacan's ruins centuries later and assigned them cosmic significance. According to Aztec mythology, the Fifth Sun -- the current age of the world -- was created at Teotihuacan. The gods gathered at the site and two of them, Nanahuatzin (nah-nah-WAHT-seen) and Tecciztecatl (tehk-sees-TEH-kahtl), threw themselves into a great fire to become the sun and the moon. The Pyramid of the Sun and the Pyramid of the Moon were named for this myth. The Avenue of the Dead was named because the Aztecs believed the platforms lining it were the burial places of the gods.

The Aztec names, which we still use, encode a misunderstanding that reveals something true. The Aztecs did not know who built Teotihuacan. They did not know its language, its political system, or its history. But they recognized its power. They saw the pyramids and knew that only beings of extraordinary capacity -- gods, or people who had become gods -- could have built them. The Aztec relationship to Teotihuacan was one of awe, reverence, and creative mythmaking -- they incorporated the ruins into their own cosmology, making the ancient city the birthplace of the cosmos they inhabited.

Aztec rulers made pilgrimages to Teotihuacan. The Aztec emperor Montezuma I is said to have visited the ruins and made offerings at the Pyramid of the Sun. Aztec tombs contained Teotihuacan-style objects -- either looted from the ruins or crafted in deliberate imitation. Archaeologists excavating the Templo Mayor in Mexico City -- the great temple at the heart of the Aztec capital -- have found Teotihuacan-style masks and objects carefully placed in ritual deposits, nine hundred years after Teotihuacan's fall. The Aztecs were collecting antiquities from the ruins and incorporating them into their own sacred architecture. They were, in a sense, the first archaeologists of Teotihuacan -- though their archaeology was religious, not scientific.

The Aztec temple complex of Tenochtitlan incorporated architectural elements that echo Teotihuacan's forms. The talud-tablero profile, Teotihuacan's architectural signature, appears in Aztec buildings. Aztec sculpture includes deliberate imitations of Teotihuacan masks -- smooth, stylized faces with half-closed eyes and slightly parted lips, rendered in greenstone or basalt, that the Aztecs recognized as the work of a civilization they revered but did not understand. The influence of the dead city on the living empire was profound and pervasive. Teotihuacan may have burned, but its shadow was long.

The connections between Teotihuacan and other sites discussed in this series are worth noting. At Monte Albán, discussed in Volume 1, Tomb 7 -- the famous grave looted by Alfonso Caso in 1932 -- contained objects that reflect Teotihuacan influence, evidence that the connections between the two cities outlasted Teotihuacan itself. At Cahokia, discussed in Volume 4, the Mississippian tradition's mound-and-plaza complexes developed independently but arrived at strikingly similar solutions: platform pyramids, ceremonial avenues, planned urban layouts. The Olmec, discussed in Volume 3, developed feathered serpent imagery centuries before Teotihuacan, suggesting that the theological tradition Teotihuacan monumentalized had deep roots in Mesoamerican thought. The Toltecs, discussed in Volume 6, would explicitly claim to be the heirs of Teotihuacan's legacy. The city's shadow stretches across the entire series.

The site was never truly forgotten. Unlike Cahokia, which vanished from memory so completely that even nearby peoples could not explain the mounds, Teotihuacan remained visible and revered. The pyramids were too large to be absorbed by the landscape. They stood, eroding slowly, through the Epiclassic, the Postclassic, the Aztec period, and the Spanish colonial era. When the first modern archaeologists arrived in the nineteenth century, they found the ruins much as the Aztecs had found them -- massive, mysterious, and silent.

Modern visitors to Teotihuacan walk the same Avenue of the Dead, climb the same pyramids, and look out over the same valley as the Aztec pilgrims who came before them. Over two million people visit the site each year, making it the most visited archaeological site in Mexico. On the spring equinox, tens of thousands of people gather at the Pyramid of the Sun in a festival that mixes indigenous tradition, New Age spirituality, and national pride. They come to absorb the energy of the ancient city -- an energy that, whatever its actual nature, has drawn human beings to this place for over two thousand years. The city's power to attract and inspire has outlasted the city itself.

The silence is the point. Teotihuacan burned, contracted, and faded -- but it never explained itself. It left its pyramids, its tunnels, its murals, its sacrificial victims, and its grid of apartment compounds. It did not leave its name, its language, or the story of its rulers. The fire of 550 CE destroyed the ceremonial center, and whatever records may have existed were likely consumed with it. The people who survived and continued to live at the site were inheritors of a diminished city, not the builders of the great one. They lived among the ruins of pyramids they did not build, in apartment compounds originally designed for a population ten times their number. The knowledge of what Teotihuacan had been -- the full story, the names, the narratives -- was lost in the flames.

And yet the city's physical presence was inescapable. The pyramids could not be hidden. The Avenue of the Dead could not be plowed under. The grid of the city, with its thousands of apartment compounds, persisted as a physical framework that shaped the landscape for centuries after the city's fall. Later communities built among the ruins, reusing Teotihuacan's walls as foundations for their own structures, incorporating the ancient stones into new buildings. The city died, but its body remained -- too large to be absorbed by the earth, too monumental to be forgotten, too enigmatic to be understood.
