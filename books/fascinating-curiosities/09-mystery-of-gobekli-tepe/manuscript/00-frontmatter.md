# A Note to the Reader

In 1994, a German archaeologist named Klaus Schmidt visited a hilltop in southeastern Turkey and recognized something extraordinary. Beneath the soil of a mound that local farmers had plowed around for generations lay the remains of the oldest known monumental architecture on Earth -- massive carved stone pillars arranged in circles, built roughly twelve thousand years ago.

Twelve thousand years. Seven thousand years before Stonehenge. Six thousand years before the invention of writing. Four thousand years before the first cities. The people who built Göbekli Tepe (guh-BEK-lee TEH-peh) were hunter-gatherers. They had no metal tools, no pottery, no domesticated animals, and no agriculture. They lived by hunting wild gazelles, aurochs, and boar, and by gathering wild grains, nuts, and fruits. And yet they organized the labor of hundreds of people to quarry, carve, transport, and erect stone pillars weighing up to ten tons.

This discovery inverted one of the foundational assumptions of archaeology: that monumental architecture required agriculture, surplus food, and settled communities. Göbekli Tepe suggests the opposite -- that the desire to build something monumental may have come first, and that agriculture developed, at least in part, to feed the people who gathered to build and worship at the temple on the hill.

This is the story of the site that rewrote the opening chapter of human civilization.
