# Part Three: The Vanishing

## Chapter 10 — The Decline

For about two hundred years, between roughly 300 and 500 CE, Monte Albán was at the height of its power. Archaeologists call this Period IIIA. The population was at its peak. The Grand Plaza was in its final, monumental form. The carved inscriptions were at their most sophisticated — complex texts with multiple dates, personal names, titles, and narrative sequences arranged according to strict compositional rules.

During this golden age, Monte Albán conducted diplomacy with the greatest power in all of Mesoamerica: Teotihuacan (teh-oh-tee-wah-KAHN).

Teotihuacan was a city in the Basin of Mexico, about 225 miles northwest of Monte Albán, near what is now Mexico City. At its peak, it may have housed 100,000 to 200,000 people, making it one of the largest cities on Earth at the time. Its enormous pyramids — the Pyramid of the Sun and the Pyramid of the Moon — still stand today, and they are among the most visited archaeological sites in the Americas. Teotihuacan was to Mesoamerica roughly what Rome was to the Mediterranean: the center of gravity around which everything else orbited.

The relationship between Monte Albán and Teotihuacan is one of the most intriguing diplomatic puzzles in Mesoamerican archaeology. Carved stones on the South Platform at Monte Albán depict named individuals dressed in Teotihuacan-style clothing — distinctive goggle-eyed headdresses and tasseled garments that are unmistakably associated with the northern city. These figures are shown traveling to Oaxaca, apparently for a specific event: the inauguration of a Monte Albán ruler.

These are not depictions of conquerors. The Teotihuacan figures are identified as visitors, ambassadors, witnesses. They came to attend a ceremony and then, presumably, went home. The carving records the event as a matter of prestige: our inauguration was important enough that Teotihuacan sent representatives.

This suggests that Monte Albán and Teotihuacan had a diplomatic relationship between peers — or at least, that Monte Albán wanted to present it that way. The two cities were separated by 225 miles of rugged mountain terrain, and there is no evidence that either city tried to conquer the other. Instead, they seem to have maintained a respectful distance, trading goods and exchanging diplomatic courtesies across the highlands.

There is even archaeological evidence of a Zapotec neighborhood within Teotihuacan itself. In a district that researchers call the "Oaxaca Barrio," located in the western part of the great city, archaeologists found Zapotec-style pottery, Zapotec burial practices, and a carved stone with Zapotec glyphs — evidence that a community of Oaxacans lived permanently in Teotihuacan, probably as merchants, diplomats, or resident ambassadors. This was not a one-way relationship. It was a two-way exchange between civilizations that recognized each other's importance.

At its peak, this relationship gave Monte Albán access to a vast trade network stretching across Mesoamerica. Obsidian from the central highlands. Cacao from the tropical lowlands. Greenstone and jade from Guatemala. Feathers from exotic birds. The prestige goods that flowed through this network were not just luxuries — they were the currency of political power. A ruler who could distribute rare and beautiful objects to his followers was a ruler who could command loyalty. When the network broke down, so did the loyalty.

But after about 500 CE, something changed.

The carved monuments that had reached such sophistication during Period IIIA began to decline in quality and complexity. Javier Urcid, who has studied the inscriptions more carefully than perhaps anyone alive, describes what happened to the writing system during the following period — Period IIIB, roughly 500 to 800 CE — as "devastating."

The word is not used casually. What Urcid documented was a systematic breakdown of the conventions that had governed Zapotec writing for centuries. The complex complementary date sequences — paired dates that anchored events in both the ritual calendar and the solar calendar — disappeared. The narrative structure that had organized the texts — sequences of events presented in logical order — fell apart. Personal names and calendar dates continued to be carved, but the sophisticated framework around them was gone.

It is as if a civilization that had been writing novels suddenly could only manage shopping lists. The individual pieces — the day names, the number signs — survived. But the system that organized them into complex, meaningful texts collapsed.

Why?


## Chapter 11 — The Walk Away

Between roughly 750 and 900 CE, the people of Monte Albán left.

There is no dramatic story to tell. No siege. No fire. No mass grave with arrow-pierced skeletons. No layer of ash and charred timber that would indicate destruction. The archaeological record at Monte Albán shows something arguably more mysterious than catastrophe. It shows a slow, steady emptying.

The population declined over a century or more. The monumental construction stopped. The elite residences on the main plaza were no longer maintained. The terraces on the hillsides were gradually abandoned, from the lowest and most distant first, then progressively closer to the summit. By 900 CE, the mountaintop that had been home to 25,000 or 30,000 people was essentially empty.

The people did not die. They moved. Down the mountain. Into the valley. Into the dozens of smaller communities scattered across the three arms of the Valley of Oaxaca. The population of the valley as a whole did not crash — it redistributed. Instead of one great city on a mountaintop, there were now many small and medium-sized towns on the valley floor, each with its own local rulers, its own political identity, its own ambitions.

Monte Albán did not fall. It dispersed.

This kind of urban collapse was not unique to Monte Albán. It was happening across Mesoamerica during the same period. The Classic Maya cities of the southern lowlands — Tikal, Copán, Palenque, Calakmul — were abandoned during the eighth and ninth centuries in a process that archaeologists call the Classic Maya Collapse. Teotihuacan had already fallen, around 550 CE, in an event that may have involved internal rebellion and deliberate burning of the city center. Across the highlands and lowlands of Mexico and Central America, the great cities of the Classic period were emptying out.

Something systemic was happening. But what?

Recent paleoclimate research offers one piece of the puzzle. Studies of lake sediment cores and stalagmite growth rings from across Mexico and Central America suggest that the eighth and ninth centuries saw a series of severe, prolonged droughts — perhaps the worst in several thousand years. A civilization on a mountaintop with no natural water supply would have been exceptionally vulnerable to drought. But climate alone cannot explain everything, because some cities in wetter regions also collapsed while some in drier regions survived. Drought was probably a trigger, but the gun was already loaded.

For Monte Albán specifically, researchers have proposed several explanations, and the honest assessment is that several of them probably contributed simultaneously.

**Environmental degradation.** Running a city of 25,000 people on a mountaintop with no natural water source for over a thousand years takes an enormous toll. The terraced hillsides were farmed intensively, year after year, century after century. The native forests that once covered the mountainsides were cleared for fuel, for construction timber, and to open new land for agriculture. With the trees gone, the soil eroded faster. Rainfall ran off the slopes instead of soaking in. The reservoirs that collected rainwater silted up. The canals that distributed water required constant maintenance.

Modern soil studies on the terraces of Monte Albán show evidence of severe erosion — topsoil stripped away, bedrock exposed in places that once supported productive fields. Pollen analyses from lake sediments in the valley below show a dramatic decrease in tree pollen during the centuries of Monte Albán's peak occupation, followed by partial recovery after the city was abandoned. The mountain was being worn out. After twelve hundred years, the land may simply have been exhausted.

**The collapse of trade networks.** When Teotihuacan fell around 550 CE, Monte Albán lost its most important diplomatic and economic partner. The long-distance trade in obsidian, greenstone, cacao, and other prestige goods that had connected the great cities of Mesoamerica was disrupted. Without access to these networks, the Monte Albán elite had fewer resources to distribute and fewer reasons for distant communities to maintain their allegiance.

**Political fragmentation.** As the central power weakened, the communities on the valley floor had less reason to send tribute uphill. Why carry corn and water and labor up a mountain to support a ruling class that can no longer provide security, trade goods, or spiritual authority in return? The tributary system that had sustained the city for centuries broke down as valley communities reasserted their independence.

**Internal pressure.** There is a simpler way to put it. The people on the terraces — the farmers, the laborers, the craftspeople who did the actual work of keeping Monte Albán alive — may have decided that the arrangement was no longer worth it. The elites on the main plaza depended entirely on the labor of the people below them. If that labor was withdrawn — through migration, refusal, or simply indifference — the city could not survive.

**Drought.** The paleoclimate evidence deserves a closer look. Studies of oxygen isotopes in stalagmites from caves in southern Mexico reveal that the period between roughly 750 and 900 CE was marked by a series of severe droughts — not a single continuous drought, but repeated episodes of exceptionally low rainfall, each lasting years or decades. For a city that depended on collected rainwater, even a few consecutive dry years could be catastrophic. Several consecutive dry decades could make the mountaintop uninhabitable.

The drought hypothesis is particularly compelling because it explains the timing. The population decline at Monte Albán, the Classic Maya Collapse, and the fragmentation of other Mesoamerican polities all cluster in the same period. If a series of severe droughts hit the entire region simultaneously, every civilization would have been affected — but the ones in the most environmentally fragile locations would have suffered the most. A mountaintop city with no river is about as environmentally fragile as it gets.

The most honest answer is probably: all of the above, in varying proportions at different moments, over the course of a century or more. There was no single cause. There was no single moment. There was a long, slow unwinding, and by the time it was done, the greatest city the Zapotec people ever built was empty.

There is something both sobering and instructive about this answer. We want history to give us clear causes and dramatic turning points. We want to know who was responsible, what went wrong, what could have been done differently. Monte Albán refuses to cooperate. It offers us instead a story about complexity — about how multiple pressures, none of them individually fatal, can combine over time to make an entire way of life unsustainable. That story may be less dramatic than a conquering army at the gates, but it is more useful, and more honest.


## Chapter 12 — The Afterlife of a City

Monte Albán was abandoned, but it was not forgotten.

The Zapotec communities in the valley below continued to regard the mountaintop as sacred ground. They did not rebuild the city or reoccupy its grand plaza, but they came back to bury their dead. The tombs that honeycombed the mountain — built during the centuries of active occupation — were reopened and reused for centuries after the city itself was empty.

And then another people arrived.

The Mixtecs (MEESH-teks) were a highland people from the mountainous region to the west and northwest of the Valley of Oaxaca — a region known as the Mixteca. They were skilled craftspeople, brilliant painters, and ambitious political operators. Beginning around the tenth century CE, Mixtec communities began expanding into the Valley of Oaxaca, sometimes through conquest, sometimes through marriage alliances with Zapotec ruling families, sometimes through gradual migration.

The Mixtecs did not rebuild Monte Albán. But they treated it as what it was — a place of immense historical and spiritual significance — and they used it for their own purposes.

This brings us to Tomb 7.

Tomb 7 is a burial chamber on the north slope of Monte Albán, originally constructed by the Zapotecs during the Classic period. Sometime in the fourteenth or fifteenth century — five hundred years after the city had been abandoned by its builders — Mixtec elites reopened this Zapotec tomb, cleared out or rearranged some of the original burial contents, and placed their own dead inside along with one of the most spectacular collections of grave goods ever found in the Americas.

Alfonso Caso discovered Tomb 7 on January 9, 1932. What he found inside would make headlines around the world.

The tomb contained the remains of several individuals, accompanied by over four hundred objects of extraordinary craftsmanship.

The gold alone was staggering. Necklaces, breastplates, rings, ear ornaments, and a pectoral — a chest ornament — depicting the Mixtec god of death, with a skull face, a feathered headdress, and pendant bells that would have jingled as the wearer moved. These were not crude metal objects. They were created using the lost-wax casting technique, one of the most sophisticated methods of metalworking in the ancient world. In this method, the artist first sculpts the desired object in wax, then encases the wax in clay. The clay mold is heated, the wax melts and drains out, and molten gold is poured into the void left behind. When the clay is broken away, the gold object emerges — a perfect replica of the original wax sculpture, down to the finest detail. The Mixtec goldsmiths had mastered this technique to a degree that astonished the Spanish when they first encountered Mixtec metalwork.

Beyond the gold, there were carved jaguar bones inscribed with scenes from Mixtec mythology — tiny, intricate narratives scratched into bone with obsidian tools. There were turquoise mosaics assembled from hundreds of tiny cut stones. Crystal cups carved from single pieces of rock crystal. Pearl ornaments from the Pacific coast. And a human skull entirely covered with a mosaic of turquoise tiles — each tile individually cut and fitted, the whole thing assembled with meticulous precision to create a death mask of extraordinary beauty and eeriness.

![Tomb 7 interior showing layered Zapotec and Mixtec elements](../images/placeholders/13-tomb-7.png)

Tomb 7 is extraordinary not just for the beauty and value of its contents, but for what it represents historically. Here was a Zapotec tomb — built by one civilization, abandoned by that civilization — reopened and reused by a completely different civilization five centuries later. The Mixtecs did not simply dump their dead into a convenient hole. They chose this specific tomb on this specific mountain because of its associations. Monte Albán still meant something, even to people who had not built it.

Caso spent the rest of his career studying the objects from Tomb 7 and the Mixtec codices they illuminated. The carved bones from the tomb depict scenes that can be matched to narratives in the surviving Mixtec painted manuscripts — creating a rare bridge between archaeological objects and written documents. Through this work, Caso was able to identify historical figures, trace genealogies, and reconstruct elements of Mixtec political history.

But notice the irony. Caso could read the Mixtec codices. He could identify the Mixtec figures carved on the bones in Tomb 7. But the Zapotec inscriptions on the older stones of Monte Albán — the writing of the people who had actually built the city — remained largely unreadable. The later tenants' records were more accessible than the original owners'.

When the Spanish finally arrived in the Valley of Oaxaca in the early 1520s, Monte Albán had been a ruin for six hundred years. The conquistador Francisco de Orozco led a military expedition into the valley in 1521 and encountered Zapotec and Mixtec communities living on the valley floor, in towns like Zaachila and Mitla. The mountaintop was impressive enough to be noted, but it was not inhabited. It was a monument to a past that even the local population could not fully explain.

The first serious archaeological work at Monte Albán was undertaken by Leopoldo Batres in 1902, during the rule of President Porfirio Díaz. Batres cleared vegetation and did some excavation, but his methods were crude by modern standards — he was more interested in impressive finds than in careful documentation. It was Caso, beginning in 1931, who brought scientific rigor to the site and transformed our understanding of what Monte Albán had been.

Since then, Monte Albán has been continuously studied — by Mexican, American, and international teams — for nearly a century. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987, a designation that has helped protect it from development while also making it one of the most visited archaeological sites in Mexico. Today, over 350,000 people visit the site each year, climbing the same mountain that the Zapotec founders climbed 2,500 years ago.

The name it carries today is not its name. The Zapotec names that have come down to us through oral tradition — Danipaguache, Danibaan — are themselves translations and approximations, recorded centuries after the original names were last spoken by the people who lived there. The name the founders used for their city during the twelve centuries of its existence — whatever word they carved into stone, however they referred to their sacred mountain in conversation, in prayer, in song — that name is lost.

The city remains. The stones remain. The carvings remain. But the word is gone.
