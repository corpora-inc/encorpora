# A Note to the Reader

Five thousand years ago, while the Egyptians were building pyramids and the Mesopotamians were pressing cuneiform into clay tablets, a third great civilization flourished in the floodplains of what is now Pakistan and northwestern India. The Indus Valley civilization -- named for the great river along which its cities were built -- was the largest of the three, spanning 1.5 million square kilometers. Its cities were marvels of urban planning: grid-patterned streets, standardized brick sizes, covered drains, and public baths. Its merchants traded with Mesopotamia, exchanging goods across thousands of kilometers of sea and land.

And then it fell silent. The cities were abandoned. The people dispersed. And the script they had used -- carved on thousands of small stone seals, pressed into clay, scratched onto pottery -- became unreadable. It remains unreadable today.

The Indus script is the only undeciphered writing system of a major Bronze Age civilization. Over one hundred scholars have proposed decipherments since the 1920s. None has been accepted. The inscriptions are heartbreakingly short -- an average of four to five signs, the longest only twenty-six. There is no bilingual text, no Rosetta Stone, no key to unlock the code. The Indus Valley civilization left behind some of the most sophisticated cities of the ancient world, and they did it in a language we cannot read and may never understand.

This is the story of the silence of Harappa (hah-RAH-pah).
