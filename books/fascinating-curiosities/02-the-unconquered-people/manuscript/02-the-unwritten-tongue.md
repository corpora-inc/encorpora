# Part Two: The Unwritten Tongue

## Chapter 5 — A Language Without a State

Every major language you have probably studied in school has a state behind it. English was spread by the British Empire. Spanish was carried across the Americas by conquistadors and missionaries. French, Portuguese, Arabic, Russian, Mandarin — each one rides on the back of a political entity that imposed it, standardized it, taught it in schools, and punished people who refused to speak it. Languages backed by empires grow. Languages abandoned by empires — Latin, Sanskrit, Classical Nahuatl, Ancient Egyptian — fade into scholarship and ceremony.

Mapudungun followed none of these rules.

It was never backed by a state, because the Mapuche never had a state. It was never standardized by an academy, because the Mapuche had no academies. It was never written down by its own speakers, because the Mapuche developed no writing system. It was never imposed on conquered peoples, because the Mapuche conquered no peoples. And yet it survived — through five centuries of colonization, through military occupation, through forced schooling in Spanish, through urbanization and migration and every force that has driven hundreds of other indigenous languages to extinction.

Mapudungun is a language isolate. It has no known relatives — no sister languages, no parent language, no language family to which it can be assigned. Linguistically, it stands alone, the way Basque stands alone in Europe or Ainu in Japan. Every attempt to connect it to neighboring language families — Quechua, Aymara, the languages of Patagonia — has failed to produce a convincing relationship. The linguist Lyle Campbell, one of the leading authorities on the classification of American languages, treats it firmly as an isolate. Joseph Greenberg placed it in his controversial "Amerind" macro-family, but that classification has been rejected by most historical linguists as methodologically flawed. Where Mapudungun came from, and how long it has been spoken in its present territory, remain open questions. It is one of the enduring puzzles of South American linguistics — a language that appears to have no relatives anywhere on Earth.

The first European to systematically study the language was the Jesuit missionary Luis de Valdivia (no relation to the conquistador Pedro de Valdivia). In 1606, he published *Arte y Gramática General de la Lengua que corre en todo el Reyno de Chile* — "Art and General Grammar of the Language Spoken Throughout the Entire Kingdom of Chile." The title itself is revealing. Mapudungun, in 1606, was the lingua franca of the entire region — spoken across a territory larger than many European countries, by a population estimated between 500,000 and one million people. The Spanish had to learn it to communicate, to trade, to negotiate, and to missionize. The Jesuit grammar was a practical tool for an empire that found itself forced to accommodate an indigenous language rather than replace it.

The most comprehensive modern reference is Ineke Smeets' *A Grammar of Mapuche*, published by Mouton de Gruyter in 2008 — a dense, 600-page linguistic analysis based on years of fieldwork. Smeets documented a language of remarkable structural complexity, one that packs information into single words with an efficiency that makes most European languages look wasteful.

Today, roughly 1.74 million people in Chile and 200,000 in Argentina identify as Mapuche. But identification and language fluency are very different things.

How many people speak Mapudungun today is genuinely hard to say. Estimates range from 100,000 to 400,000, depending on what "speaker" means. A person who can conduct a full conversation? A person who understands when spoken to but responds in Spanish? A person who knows ceremonial phrases and greetings? The boundaries between fluent speaker, passive speaker, and heritage speaker are blurry, and census data on indigenous languages in Chile and Argentina is unreliable. What is clear is that the number of fluent speakers has declined sharply over the past century, and that intergenerational transmission — children learning the language at home from parents and grandparents — has been severely disrupted.

The language is endangered. The question of whether it can survive is one of the genuinely open problems in this story. But before we ask whether it will survive, it is worth understanding what kind of thing it is — because Mapudungun is a genuinely unusual language, and losing it would mean losing a way of encoding thought that exists nowhere else on Earth.


## Chapter 6 — How Mapudungun Works

Imagine you want to say: "I am looking for water for you."

In English, that takes seven words. In Spanish, it takes six: *Te estoy buscando agua para ti.* In Mapudungun, it takes one:

*Kintukulelaeyu.*

One word. Seven syllables. It contains the root *kintu-* (to search), a directional suffix indicating purpose, a benefactive marker meaning "for someone," a tense marker, and embedded pronouns identifying both the speaker and the listener. The entire sentence is a single verb.

This is a polysynthetic language — a language in which words are built by stacking meaningful elements together, one after another, until a single word carries the information that would require an entire clause in a language like English. Mapudungun is among the most polysynthetic languages on Earth. A single verb can contain a root, multiple derivational suffixes, markers for direction, causation, benefaction, reflexivity, evidentiality, tense, aspect, mood, and person — all fused into one phonological unit.

For a reader encountering this for the first time, the closest analogy might be a chemical formula. Just as H₂SO₄ encodes "two hydrogen atoms, one sulfur atom, four oxygen atoms, bonded in a specific structure," a Mapudungun verb encodes an entire situation — who is acting, who is affected, in what direction, for whose benefit, when, how the speaker knows, and what the speaker's attitude is — in a single compressed package.

The inverse system is one of the features that makes Mapudungun distinctive among the world's polysynthetic languages. In English, the sentence "The dog bit the man" and "The man bit the dog" differ in meaning because of word order — subject first, then verb, then object. Mapudungun handles this differently. The language tracks which participant is more prominent in the conversation — the "proximate" participant — and marks whether the action flows from the proximate participant to the other (the "direct" form) or from the other to the proximate (the "inverse" form). The choice of direct versus inverse depends on a hierarchy of person (first person outranks second, second outranks third) and on discourse context. The effect is a system that foregrounds the relationship between participants rather than fixing a rigid grammatical order.

But the feature of Mapudungun that stops linguists in their tracks is evidentiality.

In English, you can say "It's raining" without revealing how you know. Maybe you looked out the window. Maybe someone told you. Maybe you heard rain on the roof and you are guessing. English allows all of these possibilities to hide behind the same sentence.

Mapudungun does this differently. The language has grammatical markers — suffixes that attach to verbs — that encode the speaker's source of knowledge. If you saw the rain yourself, you use one form. If someone told you it was raining, you use a different form. If you are inferring that it is raining from indirect evidence — wet streets, people carrying umbrellas — you use yet another form. These are obligatory. You must choose one. It is grammatically impossible to state a fact in Mapudungun without simultaneously telling your listener how you know it.

This is called an evidentiality system, and it exists in many of the world's languages — Turkish, Tibetan, Quechua, and several dozen others all have some form of it. What makes Mapudungun's version notable is the way it interacts with the language's other systems. Because Mapudungun is polysynthetic, the evidentiality marker is fused into the same word as the tense marker, the person marker, and everything else — creating verb forms that carry an extraordinary density of information about who knows what, how they know it, and how certain they are.

For a society that operated entirely through oral communication — no written contracts, no legal codes, no records — this system makes pragmatic sense. When everything depends on spoken testimony, a language that forces speakers to reveal the basis of their claims provides a built-in mechanism for evaluating reliability. It is epistemological discipline encoded in grammar.

Consider what this means in practice. At a koyagtun — a deliberative assembly where lonkos debated matters of war and peace — every claim a speaker made was grammatically tagged with its evidentiary basis. "The Spanish are building a fort at the river" — and the verb form tells every listener whether the speaker saw the fort himself, heard about it from a messenger, or is reasoning from the movement of troops. The assembly could weigh the claim accordingly. Hearsay from a trusted source might carry weight. Inference without direct evidence would be treated with appropriate caution. The language itself functioned as a built-in credibility system, doing automatically what a modern courtroom does through formal rules of evidence.

Mapudungun also has a system of noun incorporation that creates words of extraordinary specificity. A verb can absorb a noun to create a single compound word meaning something highly particular — where English would need a phrase or a sentence, Mapudungun creates a word. The result is a language that can say very precise things very economically. A single Mapudungun verb might specify the action, the object acted upon, the direction of action, who benefits, how the speaker knows about it, and when it happened — all in one word that takes about two seconds to say.

## Chapter 7 — What the Language Encodes

The word *Mapuche* itself opens a window into a worldview.

*Mapu* means "land." *Che* means "people." *Mapuche* = "people of the land." In most European languages, this would scan as a metaphor — a poetic way of saying "we are connected to our territory." In Mapuche thought, it is an ontological statement. People and land are parts of the same system. The land is alive, and people are an expression of it, the way a tree is an expression of the soil it grows from.

This understanding is embedded in the language at every level. Mapudungun is rich with terms for landscape that encode relationships rather than mere description. The word *mawida* (mah-WEE-dah) means "forest," but it also carries connotations of a living entity with its own character and agency. A forest is a place that acts, that gives, that demands respect. The distinction between "forest as resource" and "forest as being" — a distinction that Western languages force you to choose between — does not exist in Mapudungun. The word holds both meanings simultaneously.

The spiritual landscape is populated by *ngen* (nyen) — spirits or owners of natural features. *Ngen-ko* is the spirit of water. *Ngen-mawida* is the spirit of the forest. *Ngen-winkul* is the spirit of the mountain. These are specific beings with specific domains, and interacting with their domains requires acknowledging them. You do not simply take water from a river; you interact with the ngen-ko who holds that water. You do not simply cut trees in a forest; you negotiate with the ngen-mawida. The language encodes a world in which every natural feature has a guardian, and every human action in the natural world is a social interaction.

The supreme spiritual being, *Ngünechen* (ngoo-neh-CHEN), embodies a fundamental principle of duality. Ngünechen is simultaneously four beings: an old man (*fucha*), an old woman (*kushe*), a young man (*weche wentru*), and a young woman (*üllcha domo*). Male and female, old and young — all present in a single divine entity. This duality pervades Mapuche cosmology. The world is organized in complementary pairs: light and darkness, sky and earth, east and west, life and death. Balance between these pairs is the fundamental condition of well-being. Imbalance — in a person, a community, or the cosmos — is the root of illness and misfortune.

The *machi* (mah-CHEE) — the spiritual specialist, almost always a woman — is the person responsible for diagnosing and restoring balance. The machi's role encompasses healing, prophecy, communication with the spirit world, and the maintenance of cosmological order. Her primary instrument is the *kultrun* (kool-TROON), a ceremonial drum whose flat surface is painted with a representation of the Mapuche cosmos — the four cardinal directions, the sun, the moon, the stars, divided into the four quarters of the world. When a machi plays the kultrun during ceremony, she is simultaneously making music and activating a cosmological map.

The *rewe* (REH-weh), a carved wooden post that stands near the machi's home, functions as a vertical axis connecting the layers of the Mapuche cosmos — the world above (*Wenu Mapu*), the world of the living (*Nag Mapu*), and the world below (*Minche Mapu*). During the *nguillatun* (ngee-yah-TOON), the most important collective ceremony, the machi ascends the rewe — literally climbing its carved steps — as a physical enactment of spiritual travel between worlds.

All of this — the ngen, the duality, the machi, the layered cosmos — is carried, transmitted, and perpetuated through Mapudungun. The concepts are embedded in the language's vocabulary, its grammar, its patterns of expression. They are taught through oral instruction, through story, through ceremony, through the daily practice of speaking a language that encodes a particular understanding of the relationship between people, land, and spirit. There is no sacred text to preserve them. There is only the living practice of speaking and listening.

This is what makes the survival of Mapudungun so remarkable — and its potential loss so consequential. A written text can be preserved in a library. An oral tradition can only be preserved in living minds and active practice. When the last fluent speakers of a language die, the conceptual universe encoded in that language disappears with them, as completely as if a library had burned.


## Chapter 8 — Why It Survived

The simplest explanation for why Mapudungun survived five centuries of colonization is also the most important: the Mapuche controlled their own territory.

From the mid-sixteenth century to the 1880s — over three hundred years — the Mapuche maintained effective sovereignty over the land south of the Bío-Bío River. The Spanish Empire, and later the Chilean Republic, controlled the territory to the north. Between them lay a frontier — permeable, contested, punctuated by trade and violence — but a frontier nonetheless. South of that line, Mapudungun was the language of daily life, of trade, of ceremony, of diplomacy. Spanish had no foothold there because Spain had no foothold there.

The diplomatic system that maintained this frontier was conducted in Mapudungun. The *parlamentos* — the great diplomatic assemblies where Spanish governors and Mapuche leaders met to negotiate treaties, resolve disputes, and regulate trade — followed Mapuche protocols and were conducted primarily in the Mapuche language. The Spanish governors brought interpreters called *lenguaraces* (len-gwah-RAH-sehs) — "tongue-people" — who had learned Mapudungun through years of immersion. At the parlamentos, it was the imperial power that accommodated the indigenous language, a reversal of the pattern that prevailed everywhere else in the Americas.

This linguistic sovereignty had consequences that extended beyond communication. A language that is used for diplomacy, for trade, for legal proceedings, and for the management of territorial affairs is a language with prestige and practical value. Mapudungun was worth learning because speaking it gave access to the Mapuche world — its trade networks, its political relationships, its ceremonies. Spanish settlers on the frontier learned Mapudungun. Mixed-race communities along the Bío-Bío spoke it. Captives taken in warfare — both Spanish captives held by Mapuche and Mapuche captives held by Spanish — became bilingual, and some became cultural intermediaries.

The missionaries, paradoxically, also helped preserve the language. Luis de Valdivia's 1606 grammar was intended as a tool for converting the Mapuche to Christianity — but it also created the first systematic record of the language's structure. Subsequent Jesuit and Franciscan missionaries continued the work, producing vocabularies, sermon collections, and catechisms translated into Mapudungun. Their goal was to replace Mapuche spirituality with Catholicism. Their effect was to create a written archive of a language that might otherwise have left no textual trace.

Some scholars have argued that the absence of a writing system was itself a form of resilience. Written languages can become frozen — standardized in a literary form that diverges from the spoken language over centuries, creating a gap between the "official" language of texts and the "real" language of daily life. This happened to Latin, to Classical Arabic, to Sanskrit. Mapudungun, precisely because it was never written down, remained a living, spoken language — flexible, adaptive, embedded in social practice rather than preserved in documents. It changed as its speakers changed. It absorbed new vocabulary when new things arrived — the Spanish horse became *kawellu*, the Spanish cow became *waka* — while maintaining its structural core. The language bent without breaking.

There is a deeper point here about the relationship between language and power. In the colonial Americas, the usual pattern was stark: the colonizer's language replaced the colonized language within a few generations. Spanish replaced Nahuatl in central Mexico, Quechua in the Inca heartland, and dozens of smaller languages across the continent. The mechanism was consistent — the colonial language controlled access to power, trade, law, and education, and indigenous languages were pushed into the domestic sphere and then out of existence.

The Mapuche reversed this pattern for three hundred years. Because they controlled their own territory, their language controlled access to the Mapuche world. A Spanish trader who wanted to do business south of the Bío-Bío needed Mapudungun. A Spanish governor who wanted to negotiate a treaty needed Mapudungun. A missionary who wanted to convert souls needed Mapudungun. The language had power because the people who spoke it had power — and the people had power because they held their land.

This raises a question that linguists and anthropologists actively debate: is writing always an advantage for language survival? The conventional wisdom says yes — writing creates permanence, records, education materials, literature. But the counterexamples are striking. The Zapotec writing system described in Volume 1 of this series died centuries before the language itself was threatened. Literary Latin survived for centuries after anyone stopped speaking it as a mother tongue, but it survived as a dead language — a language of scholars and liturgy, permanently separated from living speech. Mapudungun, unwritten, remained alive.


## Chapter 9 — Mapudungun Today

The language survived the colonial period. It survived the nineteenth century. Whether it will survive the twenty-first century is genuinely uncertain.

The turning point was the Occupation of Araucanía in the 1860s through 1880s, which we will examine in Part Three. When the Chilean military conquered Mapuche territory and confined the population to small reservations called *reducciones*, it shattered the conditions that had sustained the language for centuries. The territorial sovereignty that had given Mapudungun its prestige and practical value was gone. Spanish became the language of the state, the schools, the courts, the markets, and the military. Mapudungun became the language of the reservation.

The Chilean education system actively suppressed it. Mapuche children in state schools were punished for speaking their own language — a practice that continued, in various forms, well into the twentieth century. The message was clear and consistent: Spanish was the language of modernity, opportunity, and citizenship. Mapudungun was the language of backwardness. Generations of Mapuche parents, wanting their children to succeed in Chilean society, made the agonizing decision to raise them in Spanish.

Urban migration accelerated the shift. Beginning in the mid-twentieth century, large numbers of Mapuche moved from rural communities to Santiago, Temuco, and other cities in search of work and education. In the city, Mapudungun had little practical function. Social pressure to assimilate was intense. Many urban Mapuche of the second and third generations grew up hearing Mapudungun spoken by grandparents but responding in Spanish — passive understanding without active fluency.

The result is a language in crisis. The best current estimates suggest that between 100,000 and 250,000 people speak Mapudungun with some degree of fluency, most of them over the age of fifty. The number of children acquiring the language as their first language at home has dropped sharply. UNESCO classifies Mapudungun as "definitely endangered" — a language that children are no longer learning as their mother tongue in the home environment.

But the story has a second chapter.

Beginning in the 1990s, a revitalization movement emerged. Bilingual intercultural education programs, established under Chilean law, introduced Mapudungun instruction in schools serving Mapuche communities. Mapudungun-language radio programs appeared. Digital media created new spaces — Mapudungun-language podcasts, YouTube channels, social media accounts, and apps for language learning. Young urban Mapuche who had grown up speaking only Spanish began seeking out language instruction, driven by a resurgent sense of cultural identity.

The Mapuche language movement is inseparable from the broader Mapuche political movement. The demand for linguistic recognition — the right to use Mapudungun in official settings, to have place names restored, to teach the language in schools — is part of a larger demand for cultural and territorial rights. The language has become a symbol of identity and resistance, carrying the same freight of political meaning in the twenty-first century that it carried in the seventeenth, when Spanish governors had to learn it to negotiate with people they could not conquer.

The paradox is striking. Mapudungun survived three hundred years of active warfare and colonial pressure — survived while the Mapuche held their land and governed themselves. It began to die only after the Occupation of Araucanía removed the conditions that had sustained it. The language's survival was never about linguistic resilience in the abstract. It was about power. When the Mapuche had power, their language thrived. When power was taken from them, the language began to fade.

Whether revitalization efforts can reverse the decline is an open question. The global track record for language revitalization is mixed. Hebrew was successfully revived as a daily spoken language in the twentieth century, but that required a state, a territory, and a massive institutional commitment. Welsh has stabilized with strong government support in Wales. Many other revitalization efforts have slowed decline without reversing it. Whether Mapudungun can survive without the territorial sovereignty that sustained it for centuries — whether digital tools and school programs can substitute for intergenerational transmission in a living community — is a question that only time will answer.

The most encouraging signs come from urban Mapuche youth — young people who grew up speaking only Spanish but who are now actively seeking out their ancestral language. Mapudungun classes in Santiago attract hundreds of students. Online communities share vocabulary, pronunciation guides, and conversation practice. Musicians compose and perform in Mapudungun, blending traditional instruments with contemporary genres. The language is becoming, for a new generation, a deliberate choice rather than an inherited habit — a way of asserting identity in a society that spent a century trying to erase it.

What is certain is that the loss of Mapudungun would be the loss of something that exists nowhere else. A language isolate with no relatives. A polysynthetic system of extraordinary structural elegance. A grammar that forces epistemological honesty. A vocabulary that encodes a world in which people and land are parts of the same living system. If Mapudungun disappears, the particular way of understanding reality that it carries disappears with it — as irretrievably as a species driven to extinction.
