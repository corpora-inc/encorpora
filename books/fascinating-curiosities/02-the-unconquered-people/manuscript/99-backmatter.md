# What We Still Don't Know

The honest list is long. Here are the questions that remain genuinely open — the frontier of what scholars are still working on.

**What happened at the Maule?** The Inca frontier in Chile near the Maule River is archaeologically confirmed, but whether the boundary was established by a single decisive battle (as Garcilaso de la Vega described a century later), by a series of costly skirmishes, or by a logistical calculation that the territory beyond was more trouble than it was worth remains debated. Tom Dillehay and others have argued that ecological and logistical factors mattered as much as military resistance.

**How many people lived in Wallmapu before contact?** Estimates range from 300,000 to 1.5 million, with most modern scholars clustering around 500,000 to 1 million. The uncertainty is real. We lack the kind of archaeological settlement data that would allow a confident estimate, and colonial-era Spanish counts were unreliable.

**How did the reche become the Mapuche?** Guillaume Boccara's ethnogenesis thesis — that the collective Mapuche identity was forged through colonial-era resistance — is well-established, but the details of the transformation remain sketchy. When exactly did people begin identifying as Mapuche rather than reche? How did autonomous kinship groups develop a sense of shared identity across such vast distances, without written communication or centralized institutions?

**What was Lautaro really like?** Much of what we "know" about Lautaro comes from Alonso de Ercilla's epic poem and later colonial chronicles, both of which had literary and political purposes. The basic outline — capture, service, escape, military leadership, tactical genius — is accepted by scholars, but the specific details of his character, his speeches, and his strategic thinking are partly legendary.

**How did the parlamento system actually produce consensus?** We know the structure — the gathering, the speeches, the deliberation — but the mechanism by which hundreds of autonomous leaders reached binding agreements without voting, without written records, and without centralized enforcement remains poorly understood. Dillehay and Zavala's research has advanced this question, but much of the internal dynamics of the koyagtun are still opaque.

**How many people speak Mapudungun?** Estimates range from 100,000 to 250,000 fluent speakers, but the boundary between fluent speaker, passive speaker, and heritage speaker is blurry. The rate of intergenerational decline is clear; the current state of the language is harder to pin down than it should be.

**Can Mapudungun survive without territorial sovereignty?** The language survived for centuries because the Mapuche controlled their own land and conducted their own affairs in their own tongue. Since the Occupation, those conditions have been absent. Whether digital tools, school programs, and cultural revitalization can substitute for the conditions that originally sustained the language is a question that only the coming decades will answer.

**What was the relationship between Mapuche communities east and west of the Andes before colonial disruption?** The Andes were a corridor, not a barrier, for the Mapuche. Communities on both sides maintained kinship ties, trade relationships, and seasonal migration patterns. The full extent and nature of this trans-Andean network — and what was lost when Argentina's "Conquest of the Desert" closed the eastern frontier — is still being reconstructed by archaeologists and historians.

These are open questions. Every one of them is being actively investigated. The mystery is alive.


# Key Names and Terms

**Ad mapu** (ahd MAH-poo) — Mapuche customary law, transmitted orally and governing social behavior, dispute resolution, and community obligations.

**Ayllarewe** (eye-yah-REH-weh) — A confederation of nine rewe, the largest peacetime political unit in Mapuche society. Could mobilize for collective military action and diplomacy.

**Araucaria** — The monkey puzzle tree (*Araucaria araucana*), called *pewen* in Mapudungun. Its seeds (piñones) have been a staple food for millennia.

**Arauco War** — The prolonged military conflict between the Mapuche and the Spanish Empire, lasting from the 1550s to roughly the 1820s. One of the longest wars in recorded history.

**Bengoa, José** — Chilean historian. Author of *Historia del pueblo mapuche (siglo XIX y XX)* (1985/2000) and *Historia de los antiguos mapuches del sur* (2008). The leading narrative historian of the Mapuche.

**Bío-Bío River** — The major river in south-central Chile that served as the de facto frontier between Spanish/Chilean territory and Mapuche territory from 1598 to the 1860s.

**Boccara, Guillaume** — French anthropologist at EHESS (Paris). His ethnogenesis thesis, published in the *Hispanic American Historical Review* (1999), demonstrated that the Mapuche collective identity was forged through colonial-era resistance.

**CONADI** — Corporación Nacional de Desarrollo Indígena. Chile's National Corporation for Indigenous Development, created by the 1993 Indigenous Law.

**Curalaba, Battle of** (1598) — The battle in which toqui Pelantaru destroyed a Spanish column and killed Governor Martín García Óñez de Loyola, triggering the Destruction of the Seven Cities.

**Dillehay, Tom D.** — American archaeologist at Vanderbilt University. Leading English-language scholar of Mapuche political organization and the parlamento system.

**Ercilla, Alonso de** (1533-1594) — Spanish soldier and poet who fought in the Arauco War and wrote *La Araucana* (published 1569, 1578, 1589), the epic poem that is the foundational literary work of Chilean culture.

**Evidentiality** — A grammatical system that encodes the speaker's source of knowledge (direct observation, hearsay, or inference). Obligatory in Mapudungun.

**Foye** (FOH-yeh) — The cinnamon tree (*Drimys winteri*), sacred in Mapuche culture. Branches of the foye were carried as symbols of peace in diplomatic encounters.

**Koyagtun** (koh-yag-TOON) — The indigenous Mapuche deliberative assembly, the precursor and foundation of the parlamento system.

**Kultrun** (kool-TROON) — The ceremonial drum of the machi, painted with a cosmological map showing the four cardinal directions, sun, moon, and stars.

**Lafkenche** (lahf-KEN-cheh) — "People of the sea." Mapuche communities along the Pacific coast.

**Lautaro** (lah-oo-TAH-roh) — Mapuche toqui (war chief), c. 1534-1557. Captured as a child and enslaved by the Spanish governor Pedro de Valdivia, he escaped, learned European warfare, and led the Mapuche to victory at the Battle of Tucapel (1553), killing Valdivia.

**Lof** — The basic Mapuche kinship and residential unit, an extended family group led by a lonko. Also spelled *lov* in older sources.

**Lonko** (LONG-koh) — The leader of a lof, whose authority rested on prestige, oratory, and generosity rather than hereditary right.

**Machi** (mah-CHEE) — Mapuche spiritual specialist, almost always a woman. Responsible for healing, ceremony, communication with the spirit world, and maintaining cosmological balance.

**Mapu** (MAH-poo) — Land, territory, earth. The foundational concept in Mapuche ontology.

**Mapuche** (mah-POO-cheh) — "People of the land." The indigenous people of south-central Chile and southwestern Argentina. The term emerged during the colonial period from the earlier identity of *reche*.

**Mapudungun** (mah-poo-DOON-goon) — "The speech of the land." The Mapuche language, a language isolate with no known relatives.

**Ngen** (nyen) — Spirits or guardians of natural features in Mapuche cosmology. *Ngen-ko* (water), *ngen-mawida* (forest), *ngen-winkul* (mountain).

**Ngünechen** (ngoo-neh-CHEN) — The supreme spiritual being in Mapuche cosmology, simultaneously embodying four aspects: old man, old woman, young man, young woman.

**Nguillatun** (ngee-yah-TOON) — The most important collective ceremony in Mapuche culture, involving prayer, sacrifice, and community gathering.

**Parlamento** — The diplomatic assemblies between Mapuche leaders and Spanish (later Chilean) officials, conducted primarily in Mapudungun and following Mapuche protocols. Dozens were held between the 1640s and the 1820s.

**Pelantaru** (peh-lahn-TAH-roo) — Mapuche toqui who led the uprising of 1598, destroying the Seven Cities and establishing the Bío-Bío frontier.

**Pewenche** (peh-WEN-cheh) — "People of the araucaria." Mapuche communities of the Andean highlands.

**Quilín, Treaty of** (1641) — The treaty in which Spain formally recognized Mapuche sovereignty south of the Bío-Bío River. The only such recognition of indigenous sovereignty in the history of the Spanish Empire in the Americas.

**Reche** (reh-CHEH) — "Authentic people" or "true people." The pre-colonial self-designation of the peoples who would later become known as Mapuche.

**Reducción** — The reservation system imposed after the Occupation of Araucanía (1883), confining the Mapuche to approximately 475,000 hectares from a territory of roughly 10 million hectares.

**Rewe** (REH-weh) — (1) An alliance of several lof for ceremonial and political purposes. (2) The carved wooden post near a machi's home, serving as a vertical axis connecting the layers of the Mapuche cosmos.

**Saavedra, Cornelio** — Chilean general who designed and led the Occupation of Araucanía (1861-1883), the military campaign that ended Mapuche territorial sovereignty.

**Smeets, Ineke** — Dutch linguist. Author of *A Grammar of Mapuche* (Mouton de Gruyter, 2008), the definitive modern reference grammar of Mapudungun.

**Toqui** (TOH-kee) — A war chief elected for a specific military campaign, whose authority ended when the campaign ended.

**Tucapel, Battle of** (December 25, 1553) — The battle in which Lautaro's forces destroyed a Spanish column and killed Governor Pedro de Valdivia.

**Valdivia, Luis de** (c. 1561-1642) — Jesuit missionary who published the first grammar of Mapudungun in 1606.

**Valdivia, Pedro de** (c. 1497-1553) — Spanish conquistador who founded Santiago (1541) and led the Spanish expansion into Mapuche territory. Killed at the Battle of Tucapel.

**Wallmapu** (wahl-MAH-poo) — "The surrounding land." The traditional Mapuche territory, spanning from the Itata River to the island of Chiloé and across the Andes into the Argentine Pampas.

**Wenufoye** (weh-noo-FOH-yeh) — The Mapuche flag, created in 1992 by the Consejo de Todas las Tierras. The name means "canelo of the sky."

**Witral** (wee-TRAHL) — The traditional Mapuche vertical loom, used for weaving textiles.

**Zavala, José Manuel** — Chilean historian. Collaborator with Tom Dillehay on research into the parlamento system and interethnic diplomacy.


# Further Reading

These are the best starting points for a reader who wants to go deeper. They are listed roughly in order of accessibility.

**For the general reader:**

- Crow, Joanna. *The Mapuche in Modern Chile: A Cultural History*. University Press of Florida, 2013. — The most accessible English-language account of Mapuche history from the nineteenth century to the present.

- Bengoa, José. *Historia del pueblo mapuche (siglo XIX y XX)*. LOM Ediciones, 2000 (revised edition). — The most comprehensive narrative history. In Spanish.

- Bengoa, José. *Historia de los antiguos mapuches del sur*. Catalonia, 2008. — Pre-colonial and early colonial Mapuche history. In Spanish.

**For the military and diplomatic history:**

- Dillehay, Tom D., and José Manuel Zavala, eds. *The Hispanic-Mapuche Parlamentos: Interethnic Geo-Politics and Concessionary Spaces in Colonial America*. Springer, 2020. — The definitive scholarly study of the parlamento system.

- Ercilla, Alonso de. *La Araucana*. 1569/1578/1589. — The Spanish epic poem about the Arauco War, written by a soldier who fought in it. Available in English translation, though much of the poetry's power is in the original Spanish.

**For the language:**

- Smeets, Ineke. *A Grammar of Mapuche*. Mouton de Gruyter, 2008. — The definitive modern reference grammar. Technical, detailed, and comprehensive.

- Baker, Mark C., and Carlos A. Fasola. "Araucanian: Mapudungun." In *The Oxford Handbook of Polysynthesis*, edited by Michael Fortescue, Marianne Mithun, and Nicholas Evans. Oxford University Press, 2017. — Excellent overview of Mapudungun's polysynthetic structure for the linguistically curious.

**For the ethnogenesis thesis:**

- Boccara, Guillaume. "Etnogénesis mapuche: resistencia y reestructuración entre los indígenas del centro-sur de Chile (siglos XVI-XVIII)." *Hispanic American Historical Review* 79, no. 3 (1999): 425-461. — The foundational article arguing that the Mapuche identity was forged through colonial resistance.

**For the broader context:**

- Valdivia, Luis de. *Arte y Gramática General de la Lengua que corre en todo el Reyno de Chile*. Lima, 1606. — The first grammar of Mapudungun, by the Jesuit missionary who studied the language during the war.


# About This Series

**Fascinating Curiosities of Human History** is a series of short, intense books about the most interesting things people have done, built, written, and wondered about. Each volume begins with a genuine mystery and uses it as a doorway into a world of knowledge — the people, the places, the context, and the open questions.

The series is designed for curious readers of all ages. Every sentence should be accessible to a bright ten-year-old and fascinating to an adult. We never talk down, we never pad, and we are always honest about what we know and what we do not.

Volume 1: *The Mystery of Monte Albán* — Sacred Mountain, Lost Script, Vanished City

Volume 2: *The Unconquered People* — Unconquered Nation, Unwritten Tongue, Invisible State

Future volumes are in development.
