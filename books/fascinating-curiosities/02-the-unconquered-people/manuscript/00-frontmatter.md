# A Note to the Reader

This book is about a people who did something that should have been impossible.

About five hundred years ago, the largest empire in the Americas — the Inca, with twelve million subjects, professional armies, roads spanning thousands of kilometers, and a bureaucracy that tracked every grain of corn — pushed south through the Andes and ran into a loose collection of farming communities in what is now southern Chile. These communities had no emperor. They had no capital city. They had no standing army. The Inca stopped.

Then the most powerful empire on Earth arrived. Spain — fresh from conquering the Aztecs in two years and the Inca in three — sailed south, founded cities, and declared the land claimed. The Mapuche (mah-POO-cheh) fought them for over three hundred years. They learned to ride Spanish horses. They adapted European weapons. They burned seven Spanish cities to the ground in a single campaign. In 1641, the Spanish Empire signed a treaty recognizing Mapuche sovereignty — the only time Spain did this anywhere in the Americas.

They did all of this while speaking a language that had never been written down and governing themselves through a system so decentralized that the Spanish could find no one to surrender.

We are going to look at how they did it, what their language reveals about how they understood the world, and why their story still matters. As always: when we know something, we will say so. When scholars disagree, we will show you both sides. When we genuinely do not know, we will say that too.
