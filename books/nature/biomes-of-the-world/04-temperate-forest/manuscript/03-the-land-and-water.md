# The Land and the Water

The land under a temperate forest is rich.

For thousands of years, leaves have fallen from the trees and turned slowly into dark, soft soil.

This kind of soil holds water well, and it gives the trees plenty of food.

The forest floor is covered in old leaves, broken twigs, and patches of green moss.

When you push aside the leaves, you find dark soil full of tiny living things.

Worms and insects move through it.

Mushrooms push up from underneath after a rain.

Streams wind through every temperate forest.

They run cold and clear in the spring, full of melting snow.

In the summer they run slower, with stones showing in the shallow water.

In the autumn, golden leaves float down them like little boats.

In the winter, the smaller streams freeze, and the water hides under a roof of ice.

Many of the great rivers of the temperate world begin in these forests.

The trees hold the soil in place and feed the streams a little water at a time.

Without the forest, the rivers would not be the same.
