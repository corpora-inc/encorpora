# The Land and the Water

The most important thing about the tundra lies under your feet.

Below the surface, the ground is frozen solid all year long.

This frozen ground is called permafrost.

In some places the permafrost reaches down deeper than the tallest building.

It has been frozen for thousands of years.

In summer, only the very top layer of the soil thaws.

This thin thawed layer is where all the plants must live.

Their roots cannot go deep, because just below them the ground is hard as rock.

The frozen ground also traps water on the surface.

Rain and melting snow cannot drain away through the permafrost.

So in summer the tundra becomes a land of shallow pools, bogs, and slow streams.

From above, the summer tundra shines with thousands of small ponds.

The land itself is mostly flat or gently rolling.

In many places the ground is broken into strange shapes by the freezing and thawing.

The soil freezes and lifts, then thaws and settles, year after year.

This slow movement cracks the ground into rings and stripes and low mounds.

The soil of the tundra is thin and poor and cold.

Dead plants break down very slowly in the cold, so good soil builds up slowly.

It is a hard land, frozen and wet and bare, but it is quietly full of life.
