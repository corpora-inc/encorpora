# The Land and the Water

The boreal forest grows over old, quiet land.

Long ago, great sheets of ice covered all of this country.

The ice was thicker than the tallest mountains we know today.

When the ice melted, many thousands of years ago, it left the land flat and scratched and full of holes.

The holes filled with water.

That is why the boreal forest has so many lakes.

Canada alone has more lakes than the rest of the world put together.

Many of them sit inside the boreal forest, shining like dark mirrors between the trees.

Wide slow rivers cross the forest, carrying meltwater to the great northern seas.

Between the lakes and the rivers, there are soft, wet places called bogs.

A bog is a piece of ground where water sits on top of the soil all year.

Mosses grow thick on the bog, soft and deep underfoot.

Below the surface, the bog water is cold and brown.

The soil of the boreal forest is poor and thin.

Pine needles and dead leaves break down slowly in the cold.

The good food for plants takes a long time to build up.

In the far north of the boreal forest, the ground deeper than a meter or two stays frozen all year.

This frozen ground is called permafrost.

Even on a hot summer day, the soil under the moss is cold to the touch.
