## Chapter 7 — At the Persian Table

Now we sit down.

The Persian year begins at the spring equinox, around the twenty-first of March. The festival is called Nowruz, the New Day. It is the most important holiday of the year.

Every family sets a special table called the haft-sin. Haft means seven. Sin is the letter S. The table holds seven things that begin with S in Persian. There is sib, an apple. Sekkeh, a coin. Sonbol, a hyacinth flower. Samanu, a sweet wheat pudding. Senjed, the dried fruit of the oleaster tree. Somagh, sumac. Serkeh, vinegar. Each one stands for something — health, wealth, beauty, patience, love, the rising sun, the passing of time.

The meal of the new year is sabzi polo with mahi. It is rice, deep green from chopped fresh herbs, served with white fish. Spring on a plate.

Another classic is ash reshteh, a thick soup of beans, herbs, fried onion, kashk, and noodles. Persians say you should eat it before a hard decision. The noodles, they say, untangle the threads of fate.

The teahouse is also a Persian invention. It is called the qahveh-khaneh, literally the coffee house, even though the drink is now tea. For five hundred years, men and women have gathered there to play backgammon, listen to poetry, and argue about the world. Many great Persian stories were first told over a steaming glass.

A famous Persian doctor, Avicenna, who lived a thousand years ago, taught that every food is either hot or cold. Saffron and walnuts and lamb are hot. Yogurt and cucumber and watermelon are cold. A good meal balances them. In many Persian homes, that idea is still alive at lunch.

Persian food is patient. It is generous. It is old. And it is still being cooked, every day, on millions of stoves.
