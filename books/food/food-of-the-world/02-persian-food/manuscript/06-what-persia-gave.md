## Chapter 6 — What Persia Gave, What Persia Took

Persia sat in the middle of the old world. Caravans crossed it. Empires rose and fell on it. Food crossed it too, in both directions.

Look at the words. English took dozens of food words from Persian. Sugar, candy, lemon, orange, jasmine, lilac, tulip, kiosk, sherbet, and paradise are all Persian in origin. So is the word checkmate, from shah mat, "the king is helpless." Persia put words into the mouths of half the world.

Persian rice cooking went east. The Mughal kings of India loved Persian food and brought Persian cooks to their court. Those cooks made biryani. The pilaf, that long-grained, spiced rice dish from the Mediterranean to the steppes of Central Asia, is rooted in Persian rice tradition.

Persian fruits went west. Romans grew the peach in Italy because they got it from Persia. Spinach reached Europe through Spain, brought by traders from the Persian world. Tulips, the symbol of the Netherlands, were first cultivated in Persia and Turkey before they ever reached Holland.

Persia took things in too. The Mongols, who invaded in the thirteenth century, brought a stronger taste for grilled and roasted meat. Arab agriculture brought sugarcane, citrus, and rice from the south and east. The samovar came from the north, from Russia, in the eighteenth century.

The Greek historian Herodotus, writing two thousand five hundred years ago, said that Persians celebrated their birthdays more lavishly than any other day of the year. On that day, he wrote, the table is set more abundantly than usual. Some things have not changed.
