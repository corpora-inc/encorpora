## Chapter 4 — The Kebab and the Patient Stew

The word kebab is Persian. It is kabab, meaning meat cooked over fire. Persians have been cooking lamb on long flat skewers for thousands of years.

The most popular kebab today is koobideh. It is ground lamb or beef, mixed with onion and salt, pressed onto a wide flat skewer, and grilled over charcoal. It looks ancient. It is not. The meat grinder reached Iran only in the late eighteen hundreds. Koobideh, the most Persian of kebabs, is barely more than a hundred years old.

Older kebabs are made from sliced meat. Joojeh is chicken in saffron and yogurt. Barg is a thin fillet of lamb. Both are served on a bed of saffron rice with a grilled tomato.

Now meet the patient food: khoresh, the Persian stew. A khoresh cooks for hours.

The most famous is fesenjan. It is chicken or duck, slow-cooked in a thick sauce of ground walnuts and pomegranate juice. The recipe is one of the oldest stew recipes ever found. A version of it is written on cuneiform tablets from the ruins of Persepolis. That makes it more than two thousand five hundred years old. People still cook it the same way.

Another classic is ghormeh sabzi: lamb, kidney beans, and a mountain of fresh herbs, all cooked down with dried lime. Many Iranians call it the national dish.
