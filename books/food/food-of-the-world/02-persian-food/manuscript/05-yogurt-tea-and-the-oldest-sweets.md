## Chapter 5 — Yogurt, Tea, and the Oldest Sweets

Persians eat yogurt with everything. Plain yogurt is called mast. Mast with cucumber and dried mint is called mast o khiar. A salty yogurt drink with mint is called doogh. There is also kashk, a strong dried whey, used in soups.

Then there is tea. Persians drink it morning, noon, and night, brewed strong in a tall pot on a samovar. Tea is so central that it surprises people to learn it arrived late. For most of Persian history, the drink was coffee. Tea took over only in the late eighteen hundreds. Today Iran is one of the top tea drinkers in the world.

There is a small ritual to it. You hold a sugar cube between your teeth. You sip the tea through the cube. The cube melts a little. The tea is hot, dark, and sweet on the way down.

Now the sweets. Persian sweets are old, rich, and small.

The oldest is faloodeh. It is thin rice noodles, frozen in rosewater syrup, served with lime juice and pistachio. Persian writers describe it from about four hundred years before Christ. That makes faloodeh one of the oldest frozen desserts in the world. It is more than two thousand years older than ice cream.

Other classics: baklava from Tabriz, lighter than the Turkish kind, with walnut and rosewater. Halva, a dense paste of sesame or saffron. Gaz from Esfahan, a soft nougat with pistachio. Sohan, a brittle, golden saffron toffee. Nokhodchi from Qom, a tiny chickpea cookie.

A small plate of sweets, a cup of tea, a slow conversation. That is a Persian afternoon.
