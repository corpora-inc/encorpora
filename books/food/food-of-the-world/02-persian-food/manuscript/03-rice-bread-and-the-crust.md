## Chapter 3 — Rice, Bread, and the Sacred Crust

Rice came to Persia from India about two thousand five hundred years ago. It became the heart of the table.

Persians cook rice in two ways. Plain rice is called chelo. Rice mixed with herbs, meat, or fruit is called polo. Both are long, light, and separate, every grain on its own.

But the magic is at the bottom of the pot. It is called tahdig. The cook puts a thin layer at the bottom of the pan — sometimes saffron rice, sometimes a slice of potato, sometimes a piece of bread. Then the rice goes on top. Then the lid goes on, wrapped in a tea towel, and steam does the work. After an hour, that bottom layer is golden and crisp.

Tahdig is sacred. Families fight for it. Iranian television has whole shows about who makes the best one. A perfect tahdig comes out of the pan in one beautiful round disk.

Then there is the bread. The most famous is sangak. Sangak is baked on a bed of hot pebbles, a method that is five thousand years old. The pebbles still come out in the bread. You pick them out at the table.

Other breads are barbari, lavash, and taftoon. A Persian breakfast is bread, tea, and a soft white cheese.
