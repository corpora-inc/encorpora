## Chapter 2 — The Native Pantry

Open a Persian pantry. The smells tell the story.

The first smell is saffron. Iran grows about ninety percent of the world's saffron. Most of it comes from a province called Khorasan. To make one gram, a farmer hand-picks the red threads from about two hundred crocus flowers. The work is done in cold autumn dawns. Saffron is the most expensive spice in the world. A pinch turns rice gold and water into perfume.

The second smell is pomegranate. Persians have grown pomegranates for thousands of years. The fruit is the symbol of love and of paradise. The juice is squeezed for stews. The seeds are sprinkled on rice and salads.

Then come the pistachios. Iran has pistachio groves that are three thousand years old. The pistachio you eat in the world is, often, the descendant of a Persian tree.

There are walnuts, sour cherries, quinces, melons, and dates. There is sumac, a dark red, sour spice from a wild bush. Before lemons were everywhere, sumac made food taste bright. It still does.

A Persian pantry holds half the history of fruit.
