# What We Still Don't Know

**Who were the Sea Peoples?** The Egyptian names -- Peleset, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Denyen, Weshesh, Sherden, Lukka, Ekwesh -- have been tentatively matched to known peoples and places, but most identifications remain uncertain. Only the Peleset (Philistines) can be identified with confidence. The origins, languages, and social organization of the other groups are largely unknown.

**Were the Sea Peoples the cause of the collapse or a symptom of it?** The Egyptian records portray them as destroyers of nations. But the systems collapse model suggests they may have been populations displaced by the same forces (drought, trade disruption, political instability) that were already undermining the Bronze Age world. The answer may be both: the Sea Peoples were displaced by the collapse and, through their migrations and attacks, accelerated it.

**Was the collapse primarily caused by external invasion, internal rebellion, climate change, earthquakes, or trade disruption?** Scholars have proposed all of these factors, and the current consensus favors a multi-causal explanation -- a "perfect storm" of interacting crises. But the relative importance of each factor is debated, and new evidence from climate studies, archaeological excavations, and ancient DNA analysis continues to reshape the picture.

**What happened to the populations that disappeared?** The population of Greece declined by 75 percent or more after the Mycenaean collapse. Where did the people go? Some died. Some migrated. Some simply ceased to be archaeologically visible -- reverting to a simpler lifestyle that leaves fewer material traces. The demographic history of the Dark Age remains poorly understood.

**What destroyed the Hittite Empire?** The fall of Hattusa around 1180 BCE was the end of one of the two great superpowers of the Late Bronze Age. Internal revolt, external attack, or some combination of both have been proposed. The evidence is insufficient to determine the cause with confidence.

**Was the Trojan War real?** The destruction of Troy VIIa around 1180 BCE is real. The Homeric tradition of a Greek siege of Troy preserves genuine Bronze Age details. But whether the *Iliad* describes a specific historical event, a composite of multiple conflicts, or a legendary elaboration of a minor incident remains unknown.

**What role did climate change play?** Paleoclimatic evidence suggests a prolonged drought in the Eastern Mediterranean during the late thirteenth and early twelfth centuries BCE. The severity, duration, and geographic extent of this drought are debated, as is its impact on agricultural production and population movements.

**Could it happen again?** The parallels between the Bronze Age collapse and modern global systems -- interconnected trade networks, dependence on long-distance supply chains, vulnerability to cascading failure -- have been noted by scholars and popular commentators alike. Whether the analogy is illuminating or misleading is an open question.


# Key Names and Terms

**Ahhiyawa** (ah-hee-YAH-wah) -- A western Anatolian or Aegean power mentioned in Hittite texts; often identified with the Achaean Greeks of Homer.

**Alashiya** (ah-lah-SHEE-yah) -- The ancient name for Cyprus, or a kingdom on Cyprus; mentioned in the Amarna Letters and in Sea Peoples records.

**Amarna Letters** -- A cache of roughly 380 cuneiform tablets found at Tell el-Amarna in Egypt, containing diplomatic correspondence between the Egyptian pharaoh and other Bronze Age rulers (~14th century BCE).

**Cline, Eric** -- American archaeologist; author of *1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed* (2014); the leading proponent of the systems collapse model.

**Denyen** (DEN-yen) -- One of the Sea Peoples groups named in Egyptian records; sometimes identified with the Danaans (Homer's name for the Greeks).

**Drews, Robert** -- American historian; author of *The End of the Bronze Age* (1993); proposed that a revolution in warfare tactics caused the collapse.

**Hattusa** (hah-TOO-sah) -- The capital of the Hittite Empire, located in central Anatolia (modern Turkey); destroyed around 1180 BCE.

**Hittite Empire** -- A major Bronze Age power based in Anatolia; controlled territory from the Aegean to northern Syria; destroyed during the Bronze Age collapse.

**Linear B** -- A syllabic script used by the Mycenaean civilization of Bronze Age Greece; deciphered as an early form of Greek by Michael Ventris in 1952; lost after the Mycenaean collapse.

**Medinet Habu** (meh-DEE-net HAH-boo) -- The mortuary temple of Ramesses III at Luxor (ancient Thebes), Egypt; site of the most important surviving records of the Sea Peoples.

**Merneptah** (mer-NEP-tah) -- Egyptian pharaoh (~1213-1203 BCE); recorded an earlier encounter with the Sea Peoples around 1208 BCE.

**Mycenaean** (my-suh-NEE-uhn) -- The Bronze Age civilization of mainland Greece (~1600-1100 BCE); centered on palace complexes at Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, and other sites.

**Peleset** (PEL-eh-set) -- One of the Sea Peoples groups; confidently identified with the Philistines who settled on the southern Levantine coast.

**Philistines** -- An Aegean-origin population that settled in the southern Levant (the coastal plain of modern Israel/Palestine) after the Bronze Age collapse; known from both Egyptian records and the Hebrew Bible.

**Ramesses III** (RAM-eh-seez) -- Egyptian pharaoh (~1186-1155 BCE); fought and defeated the Sea Peoples around 1177 BCE; recorded the event at Medinet Habu.

**Sandars, Nancy** -- British archaeologist; author of *The Sea Peoples: Warriors of the Ancient Mediterranean* (1978); foundational study.

**Sea Peoples** -- The conventional term for the diverse groups of maritime raiders and migrants who attacked the Eastern Mediterranean civilizations around 1200-1150 BCE.

**Shekelesh** (SHEK-eh-lesh) -- One of the Sea Peoples groups; sometimes identified with the Siculi of Sicily.

**Sherden** (SHER-den) -- One of the Sea Peoples groups; sometimes identified with Sardinia; some served as Egyptian mercenaries.

**Systems collapse** -- A model for the Bronze Age collapse arguing that multiple interacting factors (drought, earthquake, trade disruption, invasion) produced a cascading failure of the interconnected Bronze Age world.

**Tjeker** (CHEH-ker) -- One of the Sea Peoples groups; settled at Dor on the coast of modern Israel.

**Treaty of Kadesh** -- A peace treaty between Egypt (Ramesses II) and the Hittite Empire (Hattusili III) following the Battle of Kadesh (~1259 BCE); one of the earliest known international peace agreements.

**Troy** -- An ancient city in northwestern Anatolia (modern Turkey); the archaeological site of Hisarlik; Troy VIIa was destroyed around 1180 BCE.

**Ugarit** (OO-gah-rit) -- A major Bronze Age trading city on the coast of modern Syria; destroyed around 1185 BCE; its archives provide crucial evidence for the period.

**Weshesh** (WEH-shesh) -- One of the Sea Peoples groups; the least identifiable of the named groups.

**Yasur-Landau, Assaf** (AH-sahf yah-SOOR lahn-DOW) -- Israeli archaeologist; author of *The Philistines and Aegean Migration at the End of the Late Bronze Age* (2010).


# Further Reading

**Eric Cline, *1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed* (2014; revised edition 2021)** -- The essential modern synthesis, written for a general audience. Cline presents the systems collapse model with clarity and wit, drawing on the full range of archaeological, textual, and paleoclimatic evidence. The best single book on the topic.

**Robert Drews, *The End of the Bronze Age: Changes in Warfare and the Catastrophe ca. 1200 B.C.* (1993)** -- A detailed argument that the collapse was caused by a revolution in military tactics. Technical but rewarding, with thorough engagement with the archaeological evidence.

**Nancy Sandars, *The Sea Peoples: Warriors of the Ancient Mediterranean* (1978)** -- The classic study that defined the field. Dated in some respects but still valuable for its comprehensive treatment of the Egyptian sources and its clear presentation of the evidence.

**Assaf Yasur-Landau, *The Philistines and Aegean Migration at the End of the Late Bronze Age* (2010)** -- The most thorough study of Philistine origins, combining archaeological evidence with migration theory. Essential for understanding who the Sea Peoples were -- or at least who the Peleset were.

**Trevor Bryce, *The Kingdom of the Hittites* (2005)** -- A comprehensive history of the Hittite Empire, providing essential context for understanding one of the major powers that was destroyed in the collapse.

**Amanda Podany, *Brotherhood of Kings: How International Relations Shaped the Ancient Near East* (2010)** -- An accessible and engaging account of the diplomatic world of the Late Bronze Age, based on the Amarna Letters and other correspondence.

**Barry Strauss, *The Trojan War: A New History* (2006)** -- A readable account of the archaeological and literary evidence for the Trojan War, placing it in the context of Late Bronze Age warfare and diplomacy.

**Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman, *The Bible Unearthed* (2001)** -- While focused on biblical archaeology, includes important discussions of the Philistine settlement and the Iron Age transition in the Levant.

**Oliver Dickinson, *The Aegean from Bronze Age to Iron Age* (2006)** -- A detailed archaeological study of the transition from the Mycenaean period to the Greek Dark Age.


# About This Series

**Fascinating Curiosities of Human History** is a series about the mysteries that still surround the ancient world -- the questions that remain unanswered, the civilizations that left incomplete records, and the puzzles that continue to challenge scholars. Each volume focuses on a single mystery or a connected set of mysteries, tracing the evidence, examining the competing theories, and explaining what we know, what we suspect, and what remains genuinely unknown.

The series is designed for curious readers of all ages -- accessible to younger readers (ages nine and up) while remaining substantive and honest enough to engage adults. We do not simplify by removing complexity. We simplify by explaining it clearly.

**Volume 1: The Mystery of Monte Albán** -- The Zapotec capital in the mountains of Oaxaca, its undeciphered script, and the Building of the Dancers.

**Volume 2: The Mystery of the Mapuche** -- The language isolate of southern Chile, the people who defeated the Inca and fought the Spanish to a standstill for three centuries.

**Volume 3: The Mystery of the Olmec** -- The "mother culture" of Mesoamerica, the colossal stone heads, and the Cascajal Block -- sixty-two glyphs in a writing system used once and never seen again.

**Volume 4: The Mystery of Cahokia** -- America's forgotten metropolis, the largest earthen mound in the Americas, and the mass sacrifice of Mound 72.

**Volume 5: The Mystery of Teotihuacan** -- The city of 200,000 people with no known name, no known language, and no known rulers.

**Volume 6: The Mystery of the Toltecs** -- The phantom empire that the Aztecs claimed as their ancestors -- history, legend, or political invention?

**Volume 7: The Mystery Before the Inca** -- Tiwanaku, Puma Punku, and the Gate of the Sun: the engineers of the altiplano who built in stone at 3,800 meters and vanished before the Inca arrived.

**Volume 8: The Mysteries of the Arctic Peoples** -- The Sivullirmiut, the vanished Dorset, and the ivory death masks of Ipiutak: three mysteries from the top of the world.

**Volume 9: The Mystery of Göbekli Tepe** -- The twelve-thousand-year-old temple that was built before agriculture, before pottery, before cities -- and then deliberately buried.

**Volume 10: The Mystery of the Indus Valley Script** -- The only undeciphered writing system of a major Bronze Age civilization, locked behind inscriptions averaging four to five signs long.

**Volume 11: The Mystery of Great Zimbabwe** -- The stone city they said couldn't exist: medieval Africa's greatest architectural achievement and the colonial lie that denied it for a century.

**Volume 12: The Mystery of the Sea Peoples** -- The nameless invaders who ended the Bronze Age: the interconnected world of the Eastern Mediterranean, its catastrophic collapse, and the Dark Age that seeded Classical civilization.
