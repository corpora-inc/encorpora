# A Note to the Reader

In 1901, a retired Australian gold prospector signed a concession with the Shah of Persia for the exclusive right to explore for oil across most of the country. His name was William Knox D'Arcy. He had made his fortune in the Mount Morgan gold mine in Queensland, and he was looking for a new venture. The terms he secured were extraordinary: sixty years of exclusive rights to prospect, obtain, exploit, develop, render suitable for trade, carry away, and sell natural gas and petroleum across an area of 480,000 square miles -- roughly three-quarters of the entire Persian Empire -- in exchange for 20,000 pounds in cash, 20,000 pounds in shares, and sixteen percent of annual net profits. The Shah's government would receive no say in operations and no access to the company's books.

Seven years later, on May 26, 1908, D'Arcy's drillers struck oil at Masjed Soleyman in the Zagros Mountains of southwestern Persia. The gusher rose fifty feet above the derrick. That single geological fact shaped every intelligence operation, every coup, and every war in the region for the next century.

This volume tells the story of how oil turned Persia from a declining empire caught between British India and Tsarist Russia into the most contested intelligence battlefield on earth. The timeline runs from D'Arcy's concession in 1901 through the Allied invasion of 1941 to the Azerbaijan Crisis of 1946 -- the first confrontation of the Cold War. The cast includes diplomats, spies, soldiers, tribal leaders, and oil executives. The stakes were the fuel supply of the Royal Navy, the survival of the Soviet Union, and the sovereignty of a country that had the misfortune of sitting on one of the largest petroleum reserves in the world.

Where the documentary record is clear, we follow it. Where it remains classified or disputed -- and significant portions of the 1921 coup file remain sealed in British archives -- we say so. The story of foreign intelligence operations in Persia is a story of consequences: decisions made in London, Moscow, and Berlin that determined the lives of millions of Iranians who had no voice in any of them. Understanding those decisions, and their lasting effects, requires following the oil.
