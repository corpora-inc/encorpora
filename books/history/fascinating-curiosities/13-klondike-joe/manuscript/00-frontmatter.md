# A Note to the Reader

In 1917, a fifty-year-old Canadian gold baron walked into the middle of the Russian Revolution, talked his way across multiple front lines, and personally recovered the Romanian crown jewels from Bolshevik-held territory. He had no orders. He held no diplomatic credentials. He answered to no intelligence service. He did it because he decided it needed doing.

Joseph Whiteside Boyle -- "Klondike Joe" -- had already lived several extraordinary lives before that moment. Small-town Ontario boy. Sailor. Gold prospector. Klondike millionaire. Machine-gun unit commander in the Canadian Expeditionary Force. Each transition was improbable. The last one -- from military officer to freelance spy-diplomat operating across revolutionary Eastern Europe -- was unprecedented.

Queen Marie of Romania, one of the most remarkable women of the early twentieth century, credited Boyle with saving her country. He received Romania's highest military decorations. The precise nature of their relationship -- professional gratitude, deep friendship, or something more -- has occupied biographers for decades. The documentary record is thin. Boyle kept few papers. Marie's memoirs are guarded on the subject.

This is the story of a man who operated in the cracks between institutions -- between armies, between governments, between the old world and the new one being born in the chaos of 1917-1918. When we know something, we will say so. When the record is thin or contested, we will say that too. Espionage history, especially from this era, is built on incomplete archives and unreliable memoirs. The gaps are part of the story.
