# What We Still Don't Know

**What was the Conical Tower for?** The solid stone tower inside the Great Enclosure -- 9 meters tall, with no doors, windows, or internal chambers -- remains unexplained. Proposals include a symbol of royal authority or fertility, a grain silo (unlikely, given the solid construction), a ritual monument, and a territorial marker. The tower's function may have been symbolic rather than practical, but the specific symbolism is debated.

**How was political power organized?** The architecture of Great Zimbabwe implies a hierarchical society with a ruling elite, but the specific political structure -- monarchy, council, priesthood, or some combination -- is not clear from the archaeological evidence alone. Oral traditions describe kings, but oral traditions have been reshaped by centuries of retelling. The relationship between political authority, religious authority, and economic control at the site is poorly understood.

**What did the soapstone birds represent?** The eight carved soapstone birds found at the Hill Complex are unique in African art. Were they ancestral spirits, royal emblems, religious symbols, or something else? The combination of bird and human features suggests complex symbolic meaning, but the specific meaning is debated. Thomas Huffman's interpretation (representations of dead kings) is influential but not universally accepted.

**Why did the Great Enclosure exist?** The largest structure at the site, with its massive outer wall and internal divisions, has been variously interpreted as a royal wives' compound, an initiation school, a palace, and a ceremonial center. The evidence is consistent with multiple interpretations, and the true function may have combined several of these roles.

**What caused the decline?** Environmental degradation (deforestation, overgrazing, soil depletion), trade route shifts, and political fragmentation have all been proposed. The relative importance of each factor, and the mechanisms by which they interacted, are debated. The decline was gradual, spanning decades, and may have had different causes in different phases.

**How extensive was Great Zimbabwe's political authority?** Over 300 stone-walled sites are known on the Zimbabwe Plateau. Were they all subordinate to Great Zimbabwe? Were some independent? Were they organized in a hierarchical system with multiple levels of authority? The political geography of the medieval Zimbabwe Plateau is poorly understood.

**What happened to the people when Great Zimbabwe was abandoned?** The population dispersed, but the details -- where they went, how the transition was organized, and what the experience was like for ordinary people -- are largely unknown. The successor states (Mutapa, Torwa) absorbed some of the population, but the demographic history of the transition remains unclear.

**How much archaeological evidence was destroyed by colonial-era excavators?** Richard Nicklin Hall's removal of meters of stratified deposits from the Great Enclosure destroyed an unknown quantity of irreplaceable information. The full extent of the damage, and the nature of the evidence that was lost, can never be fully assessed.


# Key Names and Terms

**Aksum** (AHK-soom) -- An ancient kingdom in the Ethiopian Highlands; one of the great powers of the ancient world, with monumental stone stelae and early adoption of Christianity.

**Bantu** -- A large family of languages spoken across sub-Saharan Africa; the ancestors of the Shona people (builders of Great Zimbabwe) were Bantu speakers.

**Caton-Thompson, Gertrude** -- British archaeologist who conducted the most thorough early excavation of Great Zimbabwe (1929); confirmed African origin of the site; published *The Zimbabwe Culture* (1931).

**Chirikure, Shadreck** (shad-RECK chee-ree-KOO-reh) -- Zimbabwean archaeologist at the University of Cape Town; author of *Great Zimbabwe: Reclaiming a 'Confiscated' Past* (2020).

**Conical Tower** -- A solid dry-stone tower inside the Great Enclosure at Great Zimbabwe, approximately 9 meters tall, with no doors, windows, or internal chambers. Its purpose is unknown.

**Daga** (DAH-gah) -- Pole-and-clay construction material used for houses within the stone-walled enclosures at Great Zimbabwe and related sites.

**Dry-stone construction** -- A building technique using fitted stone blocks without mortar or cement; the defining architectural technique of the Zimbabwe tradition.

**Garlake, Peter** (1934-2011) -- British-born archaeologist; author of *Great Zimbabwe* (1973); driven from Rhodesia for publishing evidence of African construction.

**Great Enclosure** -- The largest structure at Great Zimbabwe and the largest single ancient building in sub-Saharan Africa; an oval enclosure bounded by walls up to 11 meters high.

**Hall, Richard Nicklin** -- Colonial-era curator of Great Zimbabwe who destroyed meters of archaeological deposits while seeking to remove evidence of African occupation.

**Hill Complex** -- The oldest part of Great Zimbabwe, built on a steep granite hill above the valley; probable seat of political and religious authority.

**Huffman, Thomas** -- South African archaeologist; developed the "Zimbabwe Pattern" model of political organization and interpreted the soapstone birds as representations of dead kings.

**Karanga** -- A branch of the Shona people; identified as the probable builders of Great Zimbabwe based on pottery analysis and oral traditions.

**Khami** (KAH-mee) -- A stone-walled site near modern Bulawayo; capital of the Torwa state, which succeeded Great Zimbabwe in the western Zimbabwe Plateau.

**Kilwa Kisiwani** (KEEL-wah kee-see-WAH-nee) -- A medieval island port off the coast of Tanzania; one of the wealthiest cities in the Indian Ocean trade network; handled much of the gold trade from the Zimbabwe Plateau.

**Kopje** (KOP-ee) -- A small hill or rocky outcrop characteristic of the southern African landscape; from Afrikaans.

**Mapungubwe** (mah-poon-GOOB-weh) -- A site in the Limpopo valley of South Africa; the predecessor of Great Zimbabwe and the first known state in southern Africa (flourished ~1075-1220 CE).

**Mauch, Karl** (MOWKH) -- German geologist and explorer who visited Great Zimbabwe in 1871; falsely attributed the ruins to non-African builders.

**Masvingo** (mah-SVEEN-goh) -- The modern city nearest to Great Zimbabwe, in southeastern Zimbabwe.

**Mutapa** (moo-TAH-pah) -- A kingdom in northern Zimbabwe and Mozambique that was one of the successor states to Great Zimbabwe; also called the Munhumutapa Empire.

**Ndoro, Webber** -- Zimbabwean scholar specializing in heritage management and conservation at Great Zimbabwe.

**Pikirayi, Innocent** -- Zimbabwean archaeologist; author of *The Zimbabwe Culture* (2001); specialist in the archaeology of the Zimbabwe Plateau.

**Randall-MacIver, David** -- British Egyptologist who conducted the first professional excavation of Great Zimbabwe (1905); confirmed African origin.

**Rhodes, Cecil** (1853-1902) -- British mining magnate and colonizer; founder of Rhodesia; promoted the "foreign builders" theory to justify colonial occupation.

**Rozvi** (ROH-zvee) -- A state that succeeded the Torwa kingdom on the western Zimbabwe Plateau; controlled the region until disrupted by Nguni migrations in the early nineteenth century.

**Shona** -- A Bantu-speaking people of the Zimbabwe Plateau; the descendants of the builders of Great Zimbabwe.

**Soapstone birds** -- Eight carved steatite figures found at the Hill Complex of Great Zimbabwe; stylized birds with human features; now the national emblem of Zimbabwe.

**Sofala** (soh-FAH-lah) -- A port on the coast of Mozambique; the main coastal outlet for the gold trade from the Zimbabwe Plateau.

**Swahili Coast** -- The eastern seaboard of Africa from Somalia to Mozambique; lined with medieval stone-built trading cities connected to the Indian Ocean trade network.

**Torwa** (TOR-wah) -- A state centered at Khami that succeeded Great Zimbabwe in the western Zimbabwe Plateau.

**Zimbabwe** -- From the Shona *dzimba-dza-mabwe*, meaning "houses of stone"; the name given to the ruins, the archaeological tradition, and the modern nation.

**Zimbabwe Pattern** -- Thomas Huffman's model for the spatial organization of power at Great Zimbabwe and related sites: rulers on elevated ground, commoners below, with stone walls defining the boundary between elite and common space.


# Further Reading

**Gertrude Caton-Thompson, *The Zimbabwe Culture: Ruins and Reactions* (1931)** -- The foundational archaeological study that definitively established the African origin of Great Zimbabwe. A landmark work in African archaeology, written with clarity and intellectual courage.

**Peter Garlake, *Great Zimbabwe* (1973)** -- The most comprehensive archaeological analysis of the site, by a scholar who paid a personal price for his commitment to the evidence. Essential reading.

**Peter Garlake, *Great Zimbabwe Described and Explained* (1982)** -- A more accessible version of Garlake's analysis, intended for a general audience. Excellent introduction.

**Thomas Huffman, *Snakes and Crocodiles: Power and Symbolism in Ancient Zimbabwe* (1996)** -- Huffman's interpretive framework for the symbolism and political organization of Great Zimbabwe. Controversial but influential.

**Shadreck Chirikure, *Great Zimbabwe: Reclaiming a 'Confiscated' Past* (2020)** -- A contemporary African perspective on the archaeology of Great Zimbabwe, challenging colonial-era frameworks and advocating for new approaches.

**Innocent Pikirayi, *The Zimbabwe Culture: Origins and Decline of Southern Zambezian States* (2001)** -- A comprehensive synthesis of Zimbabwe Plateau archaeology by a leading Zimbabwean scholar.

**Webber Ndoro, "Great Zimbabwe" (*Scientific American*, 1997)** -- A concise and accessible overview of the site and its significance, by a Zimbabwean heritage management specialist.

**Joost Fontein, *The Silence of Great Zimbabwe: Contested Landscapes and the Power of Heritage* (2006)** -- An anthropological study of how Great Zimbabwe's heritage is understood, contested, and used in contemporary Zimbabwe.

**Paul Sinclair, "The Archaeology of the African Iron Age" in *The Oxford Handbook of African Archaeology* (2013)** -- Places Great Zimbabwe in the broader context of Iron Age African civilization.

**Edward Matenga, *The Soapstone Birds of Great Zimbabwe: Archaeological Heritage, Religion and Politics in Postcolonial Zimbabwe* (2011)** -- A detailed study of the soapstone birds and their role in national identity.


# About This Series

**Fascinating Curiosities of Human History** is a series about the mysteries that still surround the ancient world -- the questions that remain unanswered, the civilizations that left incomplete records, and the puzzles that continue to challenge scholars. Each volume focuses on a single mystery or a connected set of mysteries, tracing the evidence, examining the competing theories, and explaining what we know, what we suspect, and what remains genuinely unknown.

The series is designed for curious readers of all ages -- accessible to younger readers (ages nine and up) while remaining substantive and honest enough to engage adults. We do not simplify by removing complexity. We simplify by explaining it clearly.

**Volume 1: The Mystery of Monte Albán** -- The Zapotec capital in the mountains of Oaxaca, its undeciphered script, and the Building of the Dancers.

**Volume 2: The Mystery of the Mapuche** -- The language isolate of southern Chile, the people who defeated the Inca and fought the Spanish to a standstill for three centuries.

**Volume 3: The Mystery of the Olmec** -- The "mother culture" of Mesoamerica, the colossal stone heads, and the Cascajal Block -- sixty-two glyphs in a writing system used once and never seen again.

**Volume 4: The Mystery of Cahokia** -- America's forgotten metropolis, the largest earthen mound in the Americas, and the mass sacrifice of Mound 72.

**Volume 5: The Mystery of Teotihuacan** -- The city of 200,000 people with no known name, no known language, and no known rulers.

**Volume 6: The Mystery of the Toltecs** -- The phantom empire that the Aztecs claimed as their ancestors -- history, legend, or political invention?

**Volume 7: The Mystery Before the Inca** -- Tiwanaku, Puma Punku, and the Gate of the Sun: the engineers of the altiplano who built in stone at 3,800 meters and vanished before the Inca arrived.

**Volume 8: The Mysteries of the Arctic Peoples** -- The Sivullirmiut, the vanished Dorset, and the ivory death masks of Ipiutak: three mysteries from the top of the world.

**Volume 9: The Mystery of Göbekli Tepe** -- The twelve-thousand-year-old temple that was built before agriculture, before pottery, before cities -- and then deliberately buried.

**Volume 10: The Mystery of the Indus Valley Script** -- The only undeciphered writing system of a major Bronze Age civilization, locked behind inscriptions averaging four to five signs long.

**Volume 11: The Mystery of Great Zimbabwe** -- The stone city they said couldn't exist: medieval Africa's greatest architectural achievement and the colonial lie that denied it for a century.
