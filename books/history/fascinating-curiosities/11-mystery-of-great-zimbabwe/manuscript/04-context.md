# Part Four: Context

## Chapter 8 — Africa's Medieval Cities

The colonial myth that Great Zimbabwe could not have been built by Africans was part of a larger myth: that sub-Saharan Africa had no urban civilizations before European contact. This myth -- promoted by colonial governments, embedded in European school curricula, and reinforced by decades of biased scholarship -- is false. Medieval Africa had thriving cities, sophisticated trade networks, powerful kingdoms, and architectural traditions that rival those of contemporary Europe and Asia.

Kilwa Kisiwani, the island port that handled much of Great Zimbabwe's gold trade, was one of the most impressive urban centers in the medieval world. The Swahili Coast -- the eastern seaboard of Africa from Somalia to Mozambique -- was lined with stone-built trading cities: Kilwa, Mombasa (mom-BAH-sah), Malindi (mah-LIN-dee), Zanzibar, Mogadishu (moh-gah-DEE-shoo), and dozens of smaller ports. The Swahili cities were multicultural commercial centers where African, Arab, Persian, and Indian merchants traded goods from across the Indian Ocean. The architecture was coral-stone, built with lime mortar and decorated with carved coral and imported ceramics. The Great Mosque of Kilwa, dating to the twelfth century, was one of the largest mosques in sub-Saharan Africa, and the palace complex of Husuni Kubwa featured over a hundred rooms, a bathing pool, and a commercial warehouse. The Swahili cities were not Arab or Persian colonies planted on African soil -- they were African cities, built by Swahili-speaking Bantu peoples who had adopted Islam and engaged in Indian Ocean trade over many centuries.

In West Africa, the medieval empires of the Sahel -- the semi-arid zone south of the Sahara -- were among the largest and wealthiest states in the world. The Ghana Empire (not to be confused with the modern nation of Ghana, which is located in a different region) flourished from roughly the eighth to the twelfth century, controlling the trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt. The Mali Empire, which succeeded Ghana in the thirteenth century, was one of the largest empires on Earth during the reign of Mansa Musa (MAHN-sah MOO-sah), who ruled from approximately 1312 to 1337. Mansa Musa's pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 -- during which he distributed so much gold in Cairo that he reportedly depressed the gold market for a decade -- is one of the most famous displays of wealth in world history. The city of Timbuktu (tim-buk-TOO), a major center of the Mali and later Songhai (SONG-high) empires, was a center of Islamic scholarship with a university (the University of Sankore) that attracted students and scholars from across the Muslim world. Timbuktu's manuscript libraries contained hundreds of thousands of texts -- works of theology, law, astronomy, medicine, and literature -- written in Arabic and in local languages adapted to the Arabic script.

The Swahili cities deserve particular attention because they were Great Zimbabwe's direct trading partners -- the coastal outlets through which Zimbabwe's gold and ivory reached the wider world. The Swahili civilization was not a single state but a network of independent city-states, each with its own ruling dynasty, its own commercial relationships, and its own architectural and cultural traditions. The cities shared a common language (Swahili, a Bantu language with significant Arabic vocabulary), a common religion (Islam, adopted gradually from the eighth century onward), and a common orientation toward maritime trade. The houses of the wealthy were built of coral-stone and lime plaster, with carved wooden doors, interior courtyards, and indoor plumbing -- a standard of domestic comfort that compared favorably with contemporary European towns. The Swahili cities minted coins, maintained written records (in Arabic script), and supported scholars, poets, and craftsmen. They were, by any measure, sophisticated urban centers -- and they were African.

Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe's predecessor in the Limpopo valley, has been recognized as one of the most important archaeological sites in southern Africa. The site's gold artifacts -- including a famous gold rhinoceros figurine, constructed from gold foil wrapped around a carved wooden core -- demonstrate sophisticated metalworking techniques. Mapungubwe was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2003 and a South African National Heritage Site. The discovery of Mapungubwe in 1932 was itself suppressed for decades by the South African government, which feared that evidence of a sophisticated pre-colonial African state would undermine the ideological foundations of apartheid.

The Hausa (HOW-sah) city-states of northern Nigeria -- Kano, Katsina, Zaria, and others -- were developing during this same period into major centers of trade, craft production, and Islamic scholarship. Kano, one of the oldest cities in West Africa, had massive earthen walls (portions of which still stand) encircling an area of over 20 square kilometers. The Hausa cities were centers of indigo-dyed textile production, leather working, and metalworking, and they were connected to the trans-Saharan trade through which they exported these products to North Africa and the Mediterranean world.

In the Great Lakes region of Central Africa, the kingdom of Buganda (boo-GAHN-dah), the Bunyoro-Kitara (boon-YOH-roh kee-TAH-rah) kingdom, and other states developed complex political systems with centralized monarchies, administrative hierarchies, and specialized craft production. In the Ethiopian Highlands, the kingdom of Aksum (AHK-soom) had been one of the great powers of the ancient world -- a state that controlled trade between the Red Sea and the African interior, minted its own coins, erected massive stone stelae (carved stone pillars, some over 20 meters tall), and adopted Christianity in the fourth century CE, making it one of the oldest Christian states in the world. The rock-hewn churches of Lalibela (lah-lee-BEH-lah), carved from solid rock in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, are among the most remarkable architectural achievements anywhere in the medieval world.

In the Congo Basin, the kingdom of Kongo (not to be confused with the modern Democratic Republic of the Congo, though it occupied part of the same territory) was emerging during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries as a powerful centralized state. When the Portuguese made contact with Kongo in 1483, they found a kingdom with a capital city (Mbanza Kongo), a system of provincial governance, a royal court with elaborate protocol, and a population of perhaps half a million. The Kongolese king, or Manikongo (MAH-nee-KONG-goh), ruled through a hierarchy of provincial governors and maintained diplomatic relations with neighboring states. The Kongo kingdom adopted Christianity in the late fifteenth century and maintained a diplomatic relationship with Portugal that included exchanges of ambassadors -- one of the earliest examples of African-European diplomatic relations conducted on something approaching equal terms.

In the Horn of Africa, the Somali city-states of the medieval period -- Mogadishu, Zeila (ZAY-lah), and Berbera (BER-beh-rah) -- were major commercial centers linked to the Indian Ocean trade. The Ajuran (ah-JOO-rahn) Sultanate, which controlled much of southern Somalia and parts of eastern Ethiopia from the thirteenth to the seventeenth century, built an extensive system of limestone wells and aqueducts to support agriculture in the arid landscape -- a feat of hydraulic engineering comparable in ambition, if not in scale, to the water management systems of the Indus Valley civilization (Volume 10).

The point is not to catalog African achievements for their own sake, but to establish the context in which Great Zimbabwe should be understood. Great Zimbabwe was not an anomaly. It was not a mysterious exception to an otherwise undeveloped continent. It was one expression of a continent-wide pattern of urbanization, state formation, long-distance trade, and architectural achievement that characterized medieval Africa. The question "How could Africans have built Great Zimbabwe?" is the wrong question -- not because the answer is obvious, but because the question assumes that African architectural achievement requires special explanation. It does not. The real question is how Great Zimbabwe fits into the broader pattern of African civilization, what made it distinctive, and what it tells us about the societies of the medieval Zimbabwe Plateau.


## Chapter 9 — What Was Happening Elsewhere

The peak of Great Zimbabwe, roughly 1200-1400 CE, was a period of extraordinary dynamism across the world. Medieval civilizations on every inhabited continent were building cities, expanding trade networks, and creating the political and economic structures that would shape the modern world.

In Europe, the period was the High Middle Ages -- an era of cathedral building, university founding, and commercial expansion. The population of Europe was growing rapidly, recovering from the post-Roman decline, and new towns were being founded across the continent. The Gothic cathedrals of France (Chartres, Notre-Dame de Paris, Reims, Amiens) were under construction during the same decades that Great Zimbabwe's Great Enclosure was being built. The technical challenges were different -- European builders worked with cut limestone and mortar, using pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and flying buttresses to create soaring interior spaces -- but the organizational challenge was similar: mobilizing large labor forces, coordinating skilled craftsmen, and sustaining construction projects over decades. The European Crusades, launched in 1095 and continuing through the thirteenth century, had opened trade routes between Europe and the Islamic world, bringing new goods, ideas, and technologies into European commerce. The rise of the Italian city-states -- Venice, Genoa, Florence, Pisa -- as commercial powers in the Mediterranean created a mercantile economy that would eventually challenge the feudal order.

In the Islamic world, the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries saw the aftermath of the Mongol invasions and the subsequent cultural flowering of the successor states. The Mamluk (MAM-look) Sultanate of Egypt and Syria (1250-1517) was one of the most powerful states in the world, controlling the trade routes between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean. The Il-Khanate (ill-KAH-nayt) of Persia, founded by Hulagu Khan after the Mongol conquest of Baghdad in 1258, became a center of Persian cultural and intellectual life. The city of Isfahan (is-fah-HAHN) in Persia was adorned with mosques, madrasas, and gardens that rank among the finest achievements of Islamic architecture.

In East Asia, China under the Yuan (yoo-AHN) Dynasty (1271-1368) -- the Mongol dynasty founded by Kublai Khan -- was the largest and most prosperous state on Earth. The Yuan capital of Dadu (modern Beijing) was described by the Venetian traveler Marco Polo as the greatest city in the world. Chinese porcelain from this period has been found at Great Zimbabwe -- physical evidence that the trade networks connecting East Africa to East Asia were active and productive. The celadon shards found at the site traveled over 10,000 kilometers from their kilns in China to the stone enclosures of the Zimbabwe Plateau -- one of the longest trade routes in the medieval world.

In South Asia, the Delhi Sultanate (1206-1526) ruled much of the Indian subcontinent, while the Chola (CHOH-lah) dynasty of southern India had built a maritime empire that controlled trade across the Indian Ocean from Indonesia to East Africa. Indian textiles, glass beads, and other trade goods reached the East African coast in large quantities, and Indian merchants were active participants in the Indian Ocean commercial system that connected to Great Zimbabwe through the Swahili ports.

In Southeast Asia, the Khmer (kuh-MARE) Empire, centered at Angkor in present-day Cambodia, was at its peak during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The temple of Angkor Wat, completed in the early twelfth century, is the largest religious monument in the world -- a massive stone complex covering over 160 hectares. The Khmer civilization, like Great Zimbabwe, invested enormous labor in stone construction, organized a large population through centralized authority, and controlled lucrative trade routes. The Khmer and the builders of Great Zimbabwe never knew of each other's existence, but the parallel between the two stone-building traditions -- on opposite sides of the Indian Ocean, at the same moment in history -- illustrates the universality of the impulse to build in permanent materials.

In the Americas, the period saw the rise of several major civilizations. The Aztec Empire had not yet been founded (it would emerge in the early fifteenth century), but the city of Tenochtitlan (teh-NOCH-teet-lahn) would eventually surpass any European city in population. Cahokia (Volume 4 of this series) had already declined by the time Great Zimbabwe reached its peak -- the great Mississippi Valley city was in decline by 1200 CE. In South America, the Kingdom of Chimor (chee-MOR) controlled the coast of Peru, and the Inca Empire was beginning its expansion from the Cuzco (KOOZ-koh) valley. In Mesoamerica, the Maya city-states of the Postclassic Period continued to flourish in the Yucatán Peninsula.

In West Africa, as discussed in the previous chapter, the Mali Empire under Mansa Musa was contemporary with Great Zimbabwe's peak. The two African civilizations -- one in the west, one in the southeast -- represent parallel developments: both were organized around the control of long-distance trade routes (trans-Saharan in Mali's case, Indian Ocean in Great Zimbabwe's), both derived their wealth from gold, and both expressed their power through monumental architecture (the mud-brick mosques and palaces of Mali, the stone enclosures of Great Zimbabwe). The two civilizations had no direct contact with each other -- they were separated by thousands of kilometers of forest, savanna, and desert -- but their parallel emergence demonstrates that the conditions for complex state formation existed across multiple regions of the continent simultaneously.

The Mongol Empire, at its peak in the late thirteenth century, was the largest contiguous land empire in world history, stretching from Korea to Hungary. The Mongol conquests -- devastating in their immediate impact -- created a period of unprecedented connectivity across Eurasia: the Pax Mongolica, during which trade, ideas, and technologies flowed across the continent along the Silk Road and other routes more freely than at any previous time. The period also facilitated the spread of the Black Death -- the bubonic plague that killed perhaps one-third of Europe's population in the 1340s and 1350s. The plague also affected parts of the Middle East and Central Asia, though its impact on sub-Saharan Africa (including the Zimbabwe Plateau) is unclear.

The Indian Ocean trade system that connected all of these civilizations was itself one of the great achievements of the medieval world. Unlike the Mediterranean, which was dominated by a single political power at various points (Rome, the Arab Caliphate, the Italian city-states), the Indian Ocean was a multi-polar commercial space in which no single state controlled the sea lanes. Merchants from Arabia, Persia, India, Southeast Asia, China, and East Africa all participated in the network, trading goods across thousands of kilometers of open ocean. The monsoon winds -- blowing northeast from November to March and southwest from April to October -- provided the natural engine for this trade, allowing sailing vessels to cross the ocean on predictable schedules. The monsoon system had been exploited by traders for at least two thousand years before Great Zimbabwe's peak, and the regularity of the winds made the Indian Ocean one of the most navigable oceans in the world.

The world of Great Zimbabwe's peak was, in short, a connected world -- not fully globalized in the modern sense, but linked by trade routes that spanned oceans and continents. Gold from the Zimbabwe Plateau reached Chinese ports. Porcelain from Chinese kilns reached the Zimbabwe Plateau. The Indian Ocean was the highway that connected them, and the Swahili Coast was the interchange. Great Zimbabwe stood at the southern end of this network -- distant from the major centers of Eurasian civilization, but connected to them through chains of trade that spanned the globe. The stone city on the Zimbabwe Plateau was not isolated. It was part of the medieval world.

The tendency to treat Great Zimbabwe as an anomaly -- a mysterious exception requiring explanation -- is itself a product of the colonial mentality discussed in Part Two. In reality, Great Zimbabwe was one expression of a global pattern. In the twelfth through fourteenth centuries, complex states and cities were emerging or flourishing on every inhabited continent. The conditions that produced Great Zimbabwe -- agricultural surplus, control of trade, social stratification, specialized craft production, and the ambition to build in permanent materials -- were the same conditions that produced Gothic cathedrals, Khmer temples, Chinese pagodas, and Inca stone walls. Great Zimbabwe was not an accident or an anomaly. It was what happens when human societies accumulate wealth, develop political hierarchies, and decide to build something that will last. The builders of Great Zimbabwe made the same decision as the builders of Chartres, of Angkor, of the Forbidden City: they chose permanence. They chose stone.
