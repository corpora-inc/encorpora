# Part Two: The Lie

## Chapter 4 — "Africans Couldn't Have Built This"

The European encounter with Great Zimbabwe began with a lie, and the lie persisted for over a century.

In 1871, Karl Mauch (MOWKH, rhymes with "ouch"), a German geologist and explorer, arrived at the ruins of Great Zimbabwe. Mauch was searching for King Solomon's legendary gold mines -- a persistent European fantasy that imagined vast mineral wealth hidden somewhere in the African interior, waiting for European discovery. When Mauch saw the massive stone walls of the Great Enclosure, he was astonished. He measured the walls, sketched the ruins, and collected artifacts. And then he announced his conclusion: the ruins could not have been built by Africans. They must be the work of an ancient civilized people -- Phoenicians, perhaps, or the builders of King Solomon's Temple, or the servants of the Queen of Sheba.

Mauch went so far as to claim that a wooden lintel he found at the site was made of cedar -- the cedar of Lebanon, the same wood used in King Solomon's Temple. He sniffed the wood and declared it to be cedar. It was, in fact, a local African hardwood. The identification was wishful thinking elevated to the status of evidence -- a pattern that would repeat itself for the next century.

Mauch's reasoning was not based on evidence. It was based on a premise: that Africans were incapable of building in stone, incapable of urban organization, and incapable of the architectural achievement that the ruins represented. This premise was not unique to Mauch. It was the default assumption of European thought in the nineteenth century -- an assumption rooted in centuries of racial ideology that classified Africans as inherently inferior, incapable of civilization, and suitable only for subjugation. The ruins of Great Zimbabwe, visible proof of African architectural and organizational genius, contradicted this ideology. Rather than revise the ideology, European observers denied the evidence.

Mauch's "discovery" attracted immediate attention in Europe. The ruins were romanticized, mythologized, and incorporated into the fantasy literature of the imperial age. H. Rider Haggard's novel *King Solomon's Mines* (1885), one of the most popular adventure novels of the nineteenth century, was inspired in part by the reports from Great Zimbabwe and the legends of ancient gold mines in the African interior. The novel's depiction of a lost civilization in Africa, built by non-African people and discovered by brave European adventurers, perfectly captured the colonial imagination -- and reinforced the assumption that African achievements must have non-African origins.

Cecil Rhodes -- the British mining magnate, politician, and empire-builder who colonized the territory that would bear his name (Rhodesia, now Zimbabwe and Zambia) -- took a personal interest in the ruins. Rhodes saw Great Zimbabwe as evidence of ancient civilized occupation of the region -- by non-Africans, of course -- which supported his claim that the land was not truly African land and that European colonization was therefore legitimate. The British South Africa Company, Rhodes's vehicle for colonial expansion, funded early excavations at the site. These excavations were conducted not by trained archaeologists but by treasure hunters and amateur enthusiasts whose primary interest was finding gold and other valuable artifacts. The damage they did to the archaeological record was enormous. Stratigraphic layers were destroyed, artifacts were removed without documentation, and the contextual information that would have allowed systematic archaeological analysis was permanently lost.

The most destructive of these early excavators was Richard Nicklin Hall, who was appointed curator of the site by the British South Africa Company in 1902. Hall removed what he estimated to be between one and four meters of archaeological deposits from the Great Enclosure and other areas of the site, discarding the material as "filth and decadence of the Kaffir occupation" -- using a racial slur to dismiss the very evidence that demonstrated African construction and occupation. Hall's explicit goal was to remove the African layers and expose the "original" (non-African) construction beneath. In doing so, he destroyed irreplaceable archaeological evidence -- pottery sequences, floor levels, organic remains, and the stratigraphic relationships that would have allowed precise dating and interpretation.

The first professional archaeological excavation of Great Zimbabwe was conducted by David Randall-MacIver in 1905, sponsored by the British Association for the Advancement of Science. Randall-MacIver was a trained Egyptologist who applied systematic archaeological methods to the site. His conclusion was unambiguous: the ruins were "unquestionably African in every detail," built by the ancestors of the people who still lived in the region. The pottery, the building techniques, the artifacts, and the layout of the site were all consistent with known African traditions and inconsistent with any non-African origin. Randall-MacIver's findings were published in 1906 in his book *Medieval Rhodesia*.

The reaction was hostile. The white settler community of Rhodesia rejected Randall-MacIver's conclusions outright. The "foreign builders" theory was not merely a scholarly hypothesis -- it was a political necessity. If Africans had built Great Zimbabwe, then Africans were capable of civilization, and the colonial argument that Africans needed European guidance to develop was undermined. The settlers attacked Randall-MacIver's credibility, questioned his methods, and continued to promote the Phoenician/Sabean/Solomonic origin theories.

In 1929, Gertrude Caton-Thompson, a distinguished British archaeologist, conducted the most thorough excavation of Great Zimbabwe to that date. Caton-Thompson was methodical, rigorous, and fearless. She excavated several areas of the site, documented the stratigraphy in detail, analyzed the pottery and other artifacts, and reached the same conclusion as Randall-MacIver: the ruins were built by Africans, during the medieval period, with no input from any non-African civilization. She presented her findings to the British Association for the Advancement of Science in 1931 and published them as *The Zimbabwe Culture* -- still one of the fundamental works on the site.

Caton-Thompson's excavation was meticulous. She focused on areas that had not been disturbed by Hall's destructive digging, working in a section of the Great Enclosure where the stratigraphy was still intact. She documented each layer of occupation, recording the position of every artifact, the composition of every floor surface, and the relationship between the construction phases of the walls and the occupation deposits within them. Her methods were state-of-the-art for the period, and her documentation was thorough enough that her excavation records remain valuable to modern researchers.

Her conclusions were devastating to the "foreign builders" theory. The pottery was African. The building technique was African. The imported goods (glass beads, porcelain fragments) dated to the medieval period, consistent with the Indian Ocean trade that was well documented for the East African coast during the eleventh through fifteenth centuries. There was no trace -- not a single artifact, not a single architectural feature, not a single pottery sherd -- that could be attributed to Phoenician, Sabean, or any other non-African civilization. The evidence was unambiguous.

Caton-Thompson's presentation to the British Association for the Advancement of Science in 1931 was met with outrage from the Rhodesian delegates at the conference. She was jeered, interrupted, and personally attacked. Her scholarship was dismissed as politically motivated. The colonial establishment in Rhodesia continued to maintain the "foreign builders" theory, and the government of Rhodesia actively promoted it through museum displays, school textbooks, and official publications. For the next fifty years, the white-minority government of Rhodesia would treat the African origin of Great Zimbabwe as a dangerous political claim rather than an established archaeological fact.

The suppression was systematic. Museum displays at the site were required to present the "foreign builders" theory as the primary interpretation. Guidebooks sold to tourists promoted the Phoenician or Sabean origin hypothesis. Government censors reviewed archaeological publications and suppressed those that attributed the ruins to African builders. Archaeologists working in Rhodesia understood that publishing the correct conclusion about Great Zimbabwe's builders could end their careers, cost them their research permits, or lead to deportation. The effect was a chilling of scholarly inquiry: several generations of archaeologists either avoided Great Zimbabwe entirely or produced carefully worded publications that avoided directly contradicting the official line.


## Chapter 5 — What the Evidence Actually Shows

The evidence for the African origin of Great Zimbabwe is overwhelming. It comes from multiple independent lines of investigation -- radiocarbon dating, pottery analysis, architectural analysis, metallurgical evidence, and oral tradition -- all of which converge on the same conclusion: the site was built by the ancestors of the Shona people, a Bantu-speaking population that has occupied the Zimbabwe Plateau for over a thousand years.

Radiocarbon dating has established the chronology of the site. The earliest occupation dates to the eleventh century CE (roughly 1000-1100 CE), with the most intensive construction occurring during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. The latest occupation dates to the fifteenth century. This chronology places Great Zimbabwe firmly within the Iron Age of southeastern Africa, consistent with the known history of Bantu-speaking peoples in the region. It is inconsistent with any proposed connection to ancient Mediterranean civilizations (Phoenician, Sabean, or Israelite), all of which flourished a thousand or more years earlier than Great Zimbabwe.

The pottery found at Great Zimbabwe belongs to a well-established regional tradition. The ceramic styles -- forms, decorative techniques, and fabric types -- are consistent with the pottery traditions of Iron Age Bantu-speaking communities across southeastern Africa. They show no resemblance to the pottery of any non-African civilization. The pottery sequence at Great Zimbabwe can be traced through a series of gradual changes over the site's occupation, demonstrating continuous local development rather than the introduction of a foreign tradition.

The building technique itself is the strongest evidence for local origin. Dry-stone construction using local granite is a regional tradition with deep roots on the Zimbabwe Plateau. The technique was developed locally, using the natural properties of the region's granite (which splits into regular blocks along fracture planes), and it was practiced at hundreds of sites across the plateau. The construction methods at Great Zimbabwe are consistent with this regional tradition and are inconsistent with the building techniques of any non-African civilization. Phoenician, Sabean, and ancient Israelite architecture used mortar, ashlar masonry (precisely cut rectangular blocks), and architectural forms (columns, arches, dressed-stone façades) that are entirely absent at Great Zimbabwe.

The metallurgical evidence confirms the African context. Iron smelting and forging were practiced at Great Zimbabwe, using techniques consistent with the well-documented iron-working traditions of sub-Saharan Africa. The smelting technology -- the furnace types, the bellows mechanisms, the slag compositions -- is African, developed within the Bantu iron-working tradition that had been practiced across sub-Saharan Africa for over a thousand years before Great Zimbabwe was built.

Gold working was also practiced at the site. Gold was obtained from alluvial deposits and shallow reef mines on the Zimbabwe Plateau and was worked into wire, sheet, and small objects using techniques consistent with local African traditions. The gold trade was the economic foundation of Great Zimbabwe's wealth (as we will discuss in Chapter 6), but the gold-working techniques were indigenous, not imported.

The textile evidence, though less well preserved than stone and metal, provides additional confirmation. Spindle whorls -- small stone or ceramic discs used as weights in the spinning of thread -- have been found at Great Zimbabwe and at related sites across the plateau, indicating local textile production. Cotton was grown in the region and spun into thread for weaving cloth. The textile tradition was African, part of the broader pattern of cotton cultivation and weaving that characterized the Swahili Coast and its hinterland. No Mediterranean or Middle Eastern textile technology has been identified at the site.

The evidence extends to the animal bones. The faunal assemblage at Great Zimbabwe is dominated by domestic cattle, supplemented by sheep, goats, and wild game (including various antelope species, zebra, and warthog). The cattle breeds, the herding practices, and the relative proportions of domestic and wild animals are consistent with the Iron Age pastoral traditions of southeastern Africa. The diet of Great Zimbabwe's inhabitants was African -- based on sorghum, millet, beef, and wild plants and animals of the local environment.

The soapstone birds, the most famous artifacts from the site, have no parallel in any non-African artistic tradition. Their stylistic features -- the combination of bird and human elements, the abstract treatment of form, the emphasis on symbolic rather than naturalistic representation -- are consistent with African artistic traditions and are inconsistent with the art of any proposed non-African builder culture. The birds are unique, but their uniqueness is the uniqueness of a local tradition, not evidence of foreign influence.

Peter Garlake (1934-2011), a British-born archaeologist who worked in Rhodesia in the 1960s and 1970s, was the most important twentieth-century scholar of Great Zimbabwe. Garlake's book *Great Zimbabwe* (1973) was a comprehensive archaeological and architectural analysis that demolished the remaining vestiges of the "foreign builders" theory. Garlake demonstrated, through meticulous analysis of the construction techniques, the spatial organization of the site, and the artifact assemblages, that Great Zimbabwe was the product of a local African building tradition -- a tradition that could be traced through its development from simple stone enclosures to the massive constructions of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.

Garlake paid a personal price for his scholarship. The Rhodesian government, which maintained the "foreign builders" theory as official policy, pressured Garlake to modify his conclusions. When he refused, he was harassed and eventually driven from the country. He continued his work from exile, publishing some of the most important studies of Great Zimbabwe and its related sites while living outside Rhodesia. His experience illustrates the degree to which the archaeology of Great Zimbabwe was -- and remains -- a political matter. The denial of African achievement at Great Zimbabwe was not a scholarly disagreement. It was a tool of colonial oppression, used to justify the seizure of land and the subjugation of people.

The Shona oral traditions themselves provide important corroborating evidence. The Shona people -- who number roughly thirteen million today and constitute the majority of Zimbabwe's population -- have oral traditions that describe the construction of Great Zimbabwe, the rulers who lived there, and the ceremonies that took place within its walls. The traditions identify the builders as the Karanga (kah-RAHN-gah), a branch of the Shona people who controlled the Zimbabwe Plateau during the medieval period. The traditions describe a dynasty of rulers, a court with officials and advisors, and a system of tribute and governance that extended across a wide territory. While oral traditions cannot provide the precise dates and individual details that written records preserve, they provide a cultural context for the archaeological evidence -- a living memory of the civilization that built the stone city.

The Bantu expansion -- the gradual migration and dispersal of Bantu-speaking peoples across sub-Saharan Africa over the past three to four thousand years -- provides the broader historical context. Bantu-speaking peoples originated in the region of present-day Nigeria and Cameroon and spread gradually southward and eastward, bringing with them iron-working technology, agricultural practices, and the Bantu languages that now dominate most of sub-Saharan Africa. The ancestors of the Shona arrived on the Zimbabwe Plateau during the first millennium CE, bringing iron-working skills, cattle herding, and crop cultivation. The development of the stone-building tradition, the emergence of complex political organization, and the growth of long-distance trade were all products of this Bantu-speaking population -- not importations from outside Africa.

The last white-minority government of Rhodesia fell in 1980, following a liberation war that lasted over a decade. The new nation took the name Zimbabwe -- the name of the ruins that the colonial government had said its people could not have built. The soapstone bird became the emblem of the new nation, appearing on the national flag, the coat of arms, and the currency. The renaming was an act of reclamation: the past that had been denied was now claimed as the foundation of national identity.

The legacy of the colonial denial continues to shape the archaeology of Great Zimbabwe. Shadreck Chirikure (shad-RECK chee-ree-KOO-reh), a Zimbabwean archaeologist at the University of Cape Town, has argued in his 2020 book *Great Zimbabwe: Reclaiming a 'Confiscated' Past* that the study of Great Zimbabwe remains distorted by colonial-era frameworks. The emphasis on the "mystery" of Great Zimbabwe -- who built it? why? -- perpetuates the assumption that African architectural achievement is inherently surprising and requires special explanation. Chirikure argues that the question should not be "How could Africans have built this?" but "What does this tell us about the societies that built it?" The shift in framing -- from surprise to understanding -- is the shift from a colonial to a post-colonial archaeology.

The mystery of Great Zimbabwe is not who built it. That question has been answered, definitively and repeatedly, by over a century of archaeological research. The builders were the ancestors of the Shona people, members of the Bantu-speaking tradition that has shaped sub-Saharan Africa for millennia. The real mysteries are different: how was political power organized at the site? What was the relationship between the ruling elite and the commoners? What did the Conical Tower represent? What role did the soapstone birds play in the city's ritual life? How did the gold trade function, and how was wealth distributed? These are the questions that matter -- the questions that treat Great Zimbabwe as a civilization to be understood, not an anomaly to be explained away.
