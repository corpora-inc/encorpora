# A Note to the Reader

In the rolling granite hills of southern Africa, between the Zambezi and the Limpopo rivers, stand the ruins of a medieval city built entirely of stone. The walls rise eleven meters high. They are five meters thick. They were built without mortar, without cement, without any binding material at all -- stone fitted against stone with such precision that the walls have stood for nearly a thousand years. The largest of these structures, the Great Enclosure, is the biggest single ancient building in sub-Saharan Africa. Inside it stands a solid stone tower, nine meters tall, with no doors, no windows, and no internal chambers. No one knows what it was for.

The city is called Great Zimbabwe -- from the Shona word *dzimba-dza-mabwe*, meaning "houses of stone." It was the capital of a medieval African kingdom that controlled the gold trade between the interior of southern Africa and the ports of the Indian Ocean coast. Chinese porcelain, Persian glass, and Arabian coins have been found in its ruins. At its peak, around 1300 CE, it was home to perhaps 20,000 people.

When European colonizers encountered these ruins in the nineteenth century, they refused to believe that Africans had built them. They attributed the city to Phoenicians, to the Queen of Sheba, to King Solomon, to anyone but the people who actually lived there. This denial -- maintained for over a century, enforced by colonial law, and used to justify the seizure of African land -- is one of the most sustained acts of intellectual dishonesty in the history of archaeology.

This is the story of the stone city they said couldn't exist -- and the people who built it.
