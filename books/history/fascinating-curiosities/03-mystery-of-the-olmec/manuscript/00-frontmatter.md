# A Note to the Reader

Somewhere around 1200 BCE, in the tropical lowlands along the Gulf Coast of what is now Mexico, a civilization emerged that would shape every culture that came after it in the Americas. They carved enormous portraits from single boulders of volcanic rock and dragged them through swamps for a hundred kilometers. They invented a ball game that would be played for three thousand years. They may have created the first writing in the Western Hemisphere.

We call them the Olmec (OHL-mek) — a name they never used for themselves. It is an Aztec word meaning "rubber people," applied two thousand years after the Olmec disappeared. We do not know what they called themselves. We do not know what language they spoke. We have one stone tablet covered in 62 glyphs that could be the oldest writing in the New World — and no second example has ever been found.

This book is about what we know, what we can reasonably guess, and the extraordinary amount we still cannot explain. The Olmec left behind colossal sculptures, ruined cities, and fragments of a writing system that may never be deciphered. They also left behind a mystery: how a civilization this influential arose, flourished, and vanished in the swamps of Veracruz, leaving behind more questions than answers.

As always: when we know something, we will say so. When scholars disagree, we will show you both sides. When we genuinely do not know, we will say that too.
