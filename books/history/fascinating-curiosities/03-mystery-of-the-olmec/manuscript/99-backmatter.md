# What We Still Don't Know

The honest list is substantial. Here are the questions that remain genuinely open — the frontier of what scholars are still working on.

**What language did the Olmec speak?** The most widely cited hypothesis, proposed by Lyle Campbell and Terrence Kaufman in 1976, is that the Olmec spoke a Mixe-Zoque language. The evidence is indirect: Mixe-Zoque loanwords for culturally significant items (cacao, ritual objects, agricultural terms) appear in other Mesoamerican languages, suggesting that a Mixe-Zoque-speaking people was the source of early cultural innovations. This is consistent with the Olmec but does not prove the identification. Without a readable Olmec text, the question remains open.

**What does the Cascajal Block say?** If the 62 glyphs on the Cascajal Block represent writing, their content is unknown and may be permanently unknowable without additional examples. A single inscription in an unknown script encoding an unknown language cannot be deciphered by any known method. Future discoveries of additional inscriptions could change this — but after more than two decades since the block's discovery, no second example has surfaced.

**Why were the San Lorenzo monuments destroyed?** Internal revolution, external conquest, and ritual decommissioning have all been proposed. The evidence — selective destruction of monuments, preservation of residential areas, organized burial of fragments — is consistent with more than one interpretation. The absence of clear evidence for warfare (no weapons, no battle casualties) and the care taken in arranging the destroyed monuments favor internal or ritual explanations, but certainty remains elusive.

**Why are there no elite burials at San Lorenzo?** Despite decades of excavation, no formal burial of a ruler or elite individual has been found at San Lorenzo. Whether this reflects differential preservation, burial practices that leave no archaeological trace, burial at a separate location, or destruction during the monument demolition event is unknown.

**What was the were-jaguar?** The half-human, half-feline figure that pervades Olmec art has been interpreted as a rain deity, a shamanistic transformation, a maize symbol, a royal origin myth, and a cosmological concept. The figure was clearly important — it appears on everything from tiny jade celts to monumental thrones — but its specific meaning remains debated.

**What caused the decline of La Venta?** La Venta was abandoned around 400 BCE, and its monuments were buried in a pattern reminiscent of San Lorenzo five centuries earlier. Environmental changes (river shifts, coastal erosion), political fragmentation, and competition from rising centers elsewhere in Mesoamerica have all been proposed. The specific cause — or combination of causes — is unknown.

**Is there a connection between the Cascajal Block script and later Mesoamerican writing?** The Cascajal Block's glyphs do not match any known later script (Zapotec, Maya, Epi-Olmec). Whether the Cascajal script represents an ancestor of later systems, a parallel development, or an isolated invention that led nowhere is unknown. The gap between the Cascajal Block (~900 BCE) and the earliest confirmed Zapotec inscriptions (~600-500 BCE) is three to four centuries — long enough for significant change, short enough for continuity.

**Did the Olmec invent the calendar?** The Long Count date at Tres Zapotes (31 BCE) predates any known Maya Long Count inscription, but whether this reflects Olmec invention of the system or a late Epi-Olmec development that drew on widespread Mesoamerican traditions is debated. Earlier calendar elements may exist in Olmec art, but they have not been conclusively identified.

These are open questions. Every one of them is being actively investigated. The mystery is alive.


# Key Names and Terms

**Basalt** — Dense volcanic rock, the material used for Olmec colossal heads and many other monumental sculptures. No basalt occurs naturally in the Olmec lowlands; all monumental basalt was transported from the Tuxtla Mountains.

**Cacao** — The plant (*Theobroma cacao*) whose seeds are the source of chocolate. Chemical evidence of cacao has been found in Olmec pottery dating to as early as 1800 BCE. The word "cacao" likely derives from a Mixe-Zoque language.

**Cascajal Block** — A serpentine tablet bearing 62 carved glyphs, found near San Lorenzo in 1999. Possibly the oldest writing in the Western Hemisphere (pre-900 BCE). The script is unique — no other examples have been found.

**Castilla elastica** — The rubber tree native to the Gulf Coast lowlands, whose latex the Olmec processed into rubber balls and other objects.

**Chalcatzingo** (chahl-kaht-SEEN-goh) — An archaeological site in the highlands of Morelos showing strong Olmec artistic influence, including monumental rock carvings.

**Coatzacoalcos River** (koh-aht-sah-KOHL-kohs) — A major river in Veracruz near which San Lorenzo is located.

**Coe, Michael** — Yale archaeologist (1929-2019). Author of *America's First Civilization: Discovering the Olmec* (1968). Major proponent of the "mother culture" hypothesis.

**Colossal head** — A monumental basalt sculpture depicting a human face, believed to be a portrait of a ruler. Seventeen are known, distributed across three Olmec sites.

**Covarrubias, Miguel** — Mexican artist and scholar (1904-1957) who first proposed the Olmec as the "mother culture" of Mesoamerica.

**Cyphers, Ann** — American archaeologist, director of excavations at San Lorenzo since the 1990s.

**El Manatí** (ehl mah-nah-TEE) — A spring-fed bog near San Lorenzo where rubber balls dating to approximately 1600 BCE were discovered, along with wooden busts, jade offerings, and infant remains.

**Epi-Olmec** — A culture and writing tradition that emerged in the Olmec heartland after the decline of Olmec civilization proper, roughly 400 BCE to 500 CE.

**Jáltipan** (HAHL-tee-pahn) — Municipality in Veracruz near where the Cascajal Block was found.

**Justeson, John, and Terrence Kaufman** — Linguists who proposed a decipherment of the Epi-Olmec script in 1993, arguing it encoded a Zoquean language. Their proposal remains debated.

**La Mojarra stela** — A large stone monument found in Veracruz in 1986, bearing over 500 Epi-Olmec glyphs and two Long Count dates (143 CE and 156 CE).

**La Venta** (lah VEN-tah) — The second major Olmec center, located in Tabasco. Dominant from approximately 900 to 400 BCE. Features the Great Pyramid, buried serpentine mosaics, and four colossal heads.

**Long Count** — A Mesoamerican calendar system that tracks time in cycles of days. The earliest known Long Count date in the Olmec heartland (Stela C at Tres Zapotes) corresponds to 31 BCE.

**Mixe-Zoque** (MEE-heh SOH-keh) — A language family spoken in parts of Veracruz and Oaxaca, widely believed to be the linguistic family of the Olmec.

**Offering 4** — A buried cache at La Venta containing sixteen figurines (fifteen of jade/serpentine, one of sandstone) arranged in a deliberate semicircle, discovered in 1955.

**Olmec** (OHL-mek) — A Nahuatl (Aztec) word meaning "rubber people." Used to designate the civilization that flourished in the Gulf Coast lowlands of Mexico from approximately 1500 to 400 BCE. The Olmec's own name for themselves is unknown.

**Pool, Christopher** — American archaeologist, author of *Olmec Archaeology and Early Mesoamerica* (Cambridge, 2007).

**Rodríguez Martínez, María del Carmen** — Mexican archaeologist who led the study and publication of the Cascajal Block.

**San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán** (sahn loh-REN-soh teh-noch-tee-TLAHN) — The first major Olmec center, located on a modified natural plateau in Veracruz. Dominant from approximately 1200 to 900 BCE.

**Serpentine** — A greenish metamorphic rock used by the Olmec for carved figurines, mosaic pavements, and the Cascajal Block.

**Stirling, Matthew** — American archaeologist (1896-1975) who led the first major excavations of Olmec sites for the National Geographic Society and the Smithsonian Institution in the late 1930s and 1940s.

**Stela C** — A carved stone at Tres Zapotes bearing one of the earliest known Long Count calendar dates, corresponding to 31 BCE.

**Tres Zapotes** (trehs sah-POH-tehs) — The third major Olmec site, located at the western edge of the Tuxtla Mountains. The latest of the three centers, active into the early centuries CE.

**Tuxtla Mountains** (TOOKS-tlah) — A volcanic range in Veracruz, the source of the basalt used for Olmec colossal heads and other monumental sculptures.

**Tuxtla Statuette** — A small jade figurine found in 1902, bearing a column of glyphs and a Long Count date of 162 CE.

**Ulama** (oo-LAH-mah) — A traditional ball game still played in parts of northwestern Mexico, descended from the ancient Mesoamerican ball game.

**Ullamaliztli** (oo-yah-mah-LEES-tlee) — The Nahuatl name for the Mesoamerican ball game.

**Were-jaguar** — A recurrent motif in Olmec art depicting a figure that is part human, part jaguar, characterized by a snarling downturned mouth and often a cleft forehead. Its meaning is debated.


# Further Reading

These are the best starting points for a reader who wants to go deeper. They are listed roughly in order of accessibility.

**For the general reader:**

- Coe, Michael D. *America's First Civilization: Discovering the Olmec*. American Heritage, 1968. — The classic popular account, beautifully written. Some conclusions have been revised by later scholarship, but it remains the best introduction.

- Diehl, Richard A. *The Olmecs: America's First Civilization*. Thames & Hudson, 2004. — A concise, well-illustrated overview incorporating recent discoveries.

**For the archaeology:**

- Pool, Christopher A. *Olmec Archaeology and Early Mesoamerica*. Cambridge University Press, 2007. — The most balanced and comprehensive scholarly synthesis. Treats the "mother culture" debate fairly and incorporates evidence from across Mesoamerica.

- Cyphers, Ann. *Escultura olmeca de San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán*. UNAM, 2004. — The definitive study of San Lorenzo's monumental sculpture. In Spanish.

**For the Cascajal Block and writing:**

- Rodríguez Martínez, María del Carmen, et al. "Oldest Writing in the New World." *Science* 313, no. 5793 (2006): 1610-1614. — The original publication of the Cascajal Block.

- Houston, Stephen D. "The Archaeology of Communication Technologies." *Annual Review of Anthropology* 33 (2004): 223-250. — Broader context for understanding early writing systems in the Americas.

**For the ball game:**

- Whittington, E. Michael, ed. *The Sport of Life and Death: The Mesoamerican Ballgame*. Thames & Hudson, 2001. — A comprehensive, well-illustrated survey of the ball game's three-thousand-year history.

**For Olmec art and iconography:**

- Miller, Mary, and Karl Taube. *An Illustrated Dictionary of the Gods and Symbols of Ancient Mexico and the Mayas*. Thames & Hudson, 1993. — An indispensable reference for understanding Mesoamerican religious imagery, including Olmec motifs.

**For the "mother culture" debate:**

- Flannery, Kent V., and Joyce Marcus. "Formative Mexican Chiefdoms and the Myth of the 'Mother Culture.'" *Journal of Anthropological Archaeology* 19 (2000): 1-37. — The most influential critique of the mother culture hypothesis.
