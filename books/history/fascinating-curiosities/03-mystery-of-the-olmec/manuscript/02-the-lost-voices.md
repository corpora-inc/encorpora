# Part Two: The Lost Voices

## Chapter 4 — The Cascajal Block

In 1999, road construction workers near the village of Lomas de Tacamichapa, in the municipality of Jáltipan (HAHL-tee-pahn), Veracruz, uncovered a pile of debris at a site that had been partially destroyed by earlier construction. Among the rubble was a block of serpentine — a dark greenish stone, roughly 36 centimeters long, 21 centimeters wide, and 13 centimeters thick, weighing about twelve kilograms. Its surface was covered with carved symbols.

The block made its way to María del Carmen Rodríguez Martínez, an archaeologist at the National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH) in Mexico. She recognized its significance immediately. After years of study, she and a team of collaborators published their findings in the journal *Science* in September 2006, under the title "Oldest Writing in the New World."

![The Cascajal Block showing its carved glyphs](../images/placeholders/05-cascajal-block.png)

The Cascajal Block bears 62 glyphs arranged in what appear to be horizontal rows, reading roughly from left to right and top to bottom. Twenty-eight distinct sign types have been identified among the 62 glyphs — meaning some signs repeat. The signs include recognizable images: an insect (possibly a bee or fly), a fish, a maize cob, a dart or projectile point, a seated figure, a throne or platform, and several abstract shapes that resist easy identification. Some signs are combined or paired in ways that suggest they may form compound expressions.

The block was found in a context associated with San Lorenzo — the debris pile included ceramics and other artifacts dating to the San Lorenzo phase, roughly 1200 to 900 BCE. If the block dates to this period, it predates any other known writing system in the Americas by at least two centuries. The earliest confirmed Zapotec inscriptions from Oaxaca date to approximately 600 to 500 BCE. The earliest Maya texts are later still.

The Cascajal Block has attracted fierce scholarly debate. The central questions are straightforward, but the answers are elusive.

Is it actually writing? The definition of writing, in the strict linguistic sense, is a system of visible marks that represents the sounds or meanings of a specific spoken language. By this standard, the Cascajal Block's status is uncertain. The signs are clearly organized — they follow a consistent direction, they repeat with apparent regularity, and some appear to form sequences. But with only 62 glyphs on a single object, statistical analysis cannot determine whether the signs encode language or represent something else: a list of ritual items, a calendar notation, a series of personal names, or a pictographic record that communicates meaning without encoding specific words.

The comparison is instructive. When Jean-François Champollion deciphered Egyptian hieroglyphs in the 1820s, he had the Rosetta Stone — the same text written in three scripts, one of which (Greek) was already known — plus thousands of inscriptions from temples, tombs, and papyri. When Yuri Knorosov and later scholars cracked the Maya script in the mid-twentieth century, they had hundreds of inscriptions, a known language family, and the surviving Maya codices. Each successful decipherment rested on a large body of text and some connection to a known language.

The Cascajal Block offers none of these advantages. It is one object. The signs do not appear in any other known inscription. The language of the Olmec is unknown — no Olmec text has ever been connected to any surviving language with confidence. There is no bilingual text, no key, no decoder ring.

You cannot crack a code with one message.

This is the central tragedy of the Cascajal Block. If it is writing — if those 62 glyphs encode a specific spoken language — it represents one of the most important archaeological discoveries in the Americas. The oldest known writing in the Western Hemisphere, predating the Zapotec and the Maya, produced by the civilization that may have launched all of Mesoamerican literate culture. But we may never be able to read it.

Some scholars remain skeptical that the block represents writing at all. Steve Houston, a leading expert on Maya epigraphy, has expressed caution about the "writing" designation, noting that the block's signs lack the structural features — such as consistent sign order within repeated sequences, or clear evidence of syntactic organization — that characterize known writing systems. Other scholars, including David Stuart, have been more open to the possibility that the block represents a genuine script, even if it cannot currently be read.

The Cascajal Block also raises a question about preservation. Serpentine is a relatively durable material, but it is also unusual as a writing surface. Later Mesoamerican writing appears primarily on stone monuments, painted ceramics, plaster walls, and bark-paper codices. If the Olmec wrote on organic materials — bark paper, wooden tablets, woven cloth — those materials would have rotted long ago in the humid lowland climate. The Cascajal Block might be the sole survivor of a writing tradition that was once far more extensive, the one stone document from a society that wrote primarily on perishable surfaces.

Or it might be an isolated experiment — a one-time attempt to create a symbolic system that was abandoned or never widely adopted. Both possibilities are consistent with the evidence. The absence of other examples could mean we have lost the rest, or it could mean there was nothing else to find.

This uncertainty is itself part of the story. The Cascajal Block sits at the boundary between what we know and what we cannot know, and every interpretation of it is an act of inference built on a very small foundation.

There is also the question of what the glyphs depict. The identifiable signs — the insect, the fish, the maize cob, the dart point — are consistent with the natural and cultural world of the Gulf Coast lowlands. Several signs resemble objects known from Olmec material culture: what appears to be a throne or platform, a figure in a seated position, and shapes that recall ceramic vessels. If the signs are logographic (each sign representing a word or concept), they might record a list — an inventory, a ritual sequence, a set of offerings. If they are syllabic (each sign representing a sound), they might spell words in the unknown Olmec language.

The distinction matters enormously. A logographic system can be partially understood through context and iconography — you can guess that a picture of a fish means "fish," even if you do not know the Olmec word for it. A syllabic system is opaque without knowledge of the language — the signs represent sounds, and sounds are meaningless unless you know the language they belong to.

The Cascajal Block has one additional peculiarity: the surface shows evidence of having been smoothed and recarved. Some of the glyphs appear to overlie traces of earlier signs that were ground away. This suggests the block was reused — the surface erased and rewritten. If so, it was a working document of some kind, not a permanent monument. Documents get rewritten when their content is temporary — when the message changes. A permanent inscription (a dedication, a tomb label, a law) would not need to be erased and redone. A record of accounts, a schedule of rituals, or a diplomatic message might.


## Chapter 5 — Other Olmec Writing

The Cascajal Block is the most famous candidate for Olmec writing, but it is not the only one. Scattered across the Olmec world and its margins, fragments of what may be later Olmec or Olmec-influenced writing have turned up on a variety of objects — though never in quantities sufficient for decipherment.

The Tuxtla Statuette is a small jade figurine, about sixteen centimeters tall, carved in the shape of a duck-billed or beak-masked figure wearing an elaborate costume. It was found in 1902 in the Tuxtla Mountains of Veracruz. On its front and sides, the statuette bears a column of glyphs — a short inscription of about seventy signs, accompanied by a Long Count calendar date that scholars have read as 162 CE. The style of the glyphs falls somewhere between what we recognize as Olmec and what we recognize as early Maya — a transitional script, perhaps, linking the Olmec writing tradition to the later Epi-Olmec script.

![The Tuxtla Statuette with visible glyphs](../images/placeholders/14-tuxtla-statuette.png)

The Epi-Olmec script — "epi" meaning "after" or "upon" — refers to a writing system that appeared in the Olmec heartland region after the Olmec civilization proper had declined, during the period from roughly 400 BCE to 500 CE. The most important Epi-Olmec text is the La Mojarra stela, a large stone monument found in 1986 in a river near the town of La Mojarra in Veracruz. The stela bears over five hundred glyphs arranged in long columns, accompanied by the carved figure of an elaborately dressed ruler and two Long Count dates corresponding to 143 CE and 156 CE.

Five hundred glyphs is a substantial text — far more than the 62 of the Cascajal Block. The La Mojarra ruler depicted on the stela wears an elaborate headdress with a long quetzal-feather train, and his costume includes symbols that link him to supernatural power. The text appears to describe his deeds and his ritual authority — a royal proclamation, similar in function to the carved stelae of later Maya kings.

In 1993, the linguists John Justeson and Terrence Kaufman proposed a decipherment of the La Mojarra stela, arguing that the Epi-Olmec script encoded a Zoquean language (SOH-keh-ahn) — a member of the Mixe-Zoque (MEE-heh SOH-keh) language family, which is still spoken in parts of Veracruz and Oaxaca today. Their proposed reading generated considerable excitement and equally considerable criticism. Other scholars, including Stephen Houston and Michael Coe, found the decipherment unconvincing, arguing that the proposed readings relied too heavily on assumptions about the language and that the script's internal structure remained poorly understood.

The core problem with the Justeson-Kaufman proposal is verification. When Michael Ventris deciphered Linear B in 1952, he could test his readings against new tablets as they were discovered — and the readings consistently worked. When Maya glyphs were deciphered, the readings could be checked against the known grammar of living Maya languages. The Epi-Olmec decipherment has no such test. The La Mojarra stela and the Tuxtla Statuette are the only substantial Epi-Olmec texts. With a corpus this small, there is not enough material to confirm or falsify any proposed reading. The decipherment remains one proposal among several, and the Epi-Olmec script remains largely unread.

The connection between the Epi-Olmec script and the earlier Cascajal Block is also uncertain — they may represent stages of a single scribal tradition spanning a thousand years, or they may be independent developments. The Cascajal Block glyphs look different from the Epi-Olmec signs, but a thousand years of evolution could easily account for that difference. The Latin alphabet looks nothing like its ancestor, the Phoenician alphabet, and they are separated by a similar span of time.

Beyond these major examples, Olmec and Olmec-influenced symbols appear on a variety of objects: roller stamps and cylinder seals found at La Venta and other sites, carved designs on ceramics, and scattered glyphs on small portable objects. At Chalcatzingo (chahl-kaht-SEEN-goh), a site in the highlands of Morelos that shows strong Olmec artistic influence, rock carvings include scenes with small glyphic elements that may represent early experiments in notation.

The La Venta roller stamps and cylinder seals are particularly intriguing. These are small ceramic objects carved with repeating patterns that could be rolled across a flat surface — skin, cloth, bark paper, or wet clay — to produce a continuous design. The patterns include geometric motifs, stylized faces, and what may be symbolic notation. Roller stamps are common at many Mesoamerican sites, and their function is debated: body decoration, textile printing, or the production of graphic marks on documents are all possibilities. If the Olmec used roller stamps to apply symbols to perishable surfaces — bark paper, for instance — then a substantial body of Olmec "writing" could have existed on materials that rotted away in the humid lowland climate.

This is the preservation problem, and it haunts every attempt to understand Olmec writing. The Gulf Coast lowlands are among the worst environments in the world for preserving organic materials. Wood, cloth, paper, leather — anything organic — disintegrates rapidly in the heat and humidity. If the Olmec wrote primarily on organic surfaces (as the Maya wrote on bark-paper codices), then the vast majority of their written output would have been lost. The Cascajal Block, carved on durable stone, would be the one freak survivor — not the norm but the exception.

Consider the parallel with the Maya. We know the Maya had an elaborate literary culture because thousands of stone inscriptions survive, along with pottery texts, painted murals, and four surviving codices (bark-paper books). But the Maya themselves described libraries of codices — rooms full of books. Spanish missionaries burned virtually all of them in the sixteenth century. If those missionaries had been more thorough, and if the stone inscriptions had somehow been destroyed as well, we would know as little about Maya writing as we know about Olmec writing. The Maya literary tradition would look, from an archaeological perspective, much like the Olmec one does: fragmentary, enigmatic, and possibly unreadable.

These fragments contribute to a picture of a culture that was moving toward — or had already achieved — some form of graphic communication. The question is whether the Olmec had a fully developed writing system that has largely been lost, or whether they had a set of symbolic conventions that fell short of encoding language in the way that later Mesoamerican scripts would.

The connection to later writing traditions is tantalizing. The Zapotec script, which emerged around 600 to 500 BCE in the Valley of Oaxaca, shares certain structural principles with Olmec glyphic conventions — the use of calendar dates in conjunction with personal names and place names, the combination of pictographic and phonetic elements. The Maya script, which reached its full elaboration during the Classic period (250-900 CE), employs a logosyllabic system that some scholars believe has deep roots in the earlier Olmec and Epi-Olmec traditions.

If these connections are real — if the Maya script descends, however indirectly, from an Olmec original — then the Olmec may have launched the entire tradition of writing in the Americas. The Cascajal Block, sitting alone in its serpentine silence, might be the oldest surviving evidence of a revolution in human communication that would eventually produce the most elaborate writing system in the pre-Columbian world.

Or the connections might be coincidental, and the Maya script might have developed independently. The evidence is suggestive, not conclusive. This is one of those questions where scholars can reasonably disagree, and where future discoveries — a new inscription, a bilingual text, another Cascajal-type block — could change everything overnight.


## Chapter 6 — The Ball Game Begins

In 1989, archaeologists excavating at El Manatí (ehl mah-nah-TEE), a spring-fed bog near San Lorenzo, made a discovery that pushed the history of the Mesoamerican ball game back to its earliest known origins. Buried in the waterlogged sediments, preserved by the anaerobic conditions of the bog, were a dozen rubber balls — solid spheres of processed natural latex, each about ten to fifteen centimeters in diameter. The oldest of these balls dated to approximately 1600 BCE.

Sixteen hundred BCE. That is more than three thousand six hundred years ago. And these were clearly manufactured objects — not raw lumps of tree sap, but processed rubber, formed into spheres. The processing of natural latex into usable rubber is a multi-step chemical procedure. The sap of the Castilla elastica tree is naturally sticky and brittle. To create a ball that bounces, the Olmec mixed the latex with juice from the morning glory vine (Ipomoea alba), which contains sulfur compounds that cross-link the rubber molecules — a process that chemists would later call vulcanization. Charles Goodyear patented a version of this process in 1844. The Olmec were doing it three thousand years earlier.

![A rubber ball alongside a depiction of a ball court](../images/placeholders/09-rubber-ball-and-court.png)

The El Manatí balls are the oldest known rubber objects in the world. The site also yielded wooden busts carved with Olmec-style features, jade axes, and the bones of infants — likely sacrificial offerings. The spring was sacred. The balls were deposited there as ritual objects, placed in the water alongside other precious items.

The Mesoamerican ball game — known by various names in different cultures, including *ullamaliztli* (oo-yah-mah-LEES-tlee) in Nahuatl and *pitz* in Maya — was played for at least three thousand years across an area stretching from the American Southwest to Honduras. Hundreds of ball courts have been found at archaeological sites throughout Mesoamerica, from modest village courts to the enormous Great Ball Court at Chichén Itzá, which measures 168 meters long and 70 meters wide.

The basic principle was consistent across time and space: two teams or individuals attempted to keep a heavy rubber ball in play, striking it with their hips, forearms, or other parts of the body (the rules varied by period and region). The ball was dense and heavy — larger balls used in later periods weighed three to four kilograms and could cause serious injury. Players wore protective equipment: thick leather belts or stone yokes around the waist, knee pads, and sometimes gloves or arm guards. Stone yokes — U-shaped carved stones, often elaborately decorated with animal imagery — have been found at many sites. Whether these were actual game equipment or ceremonial replicas of leather originals is debated. A stone yoke weighing twenty kilograms would be impractical for active play; a leather yoke of the same shape would be functional. The stone versions may be ritual objects — offerings placed in tombs or temples to accompany their owners into the afterlife.

The ball courts themselves evolved over three millennia. Early courts were simple open playing areas defined by low walls or markers. By the Classic period (250-900 CE), the standard Maya ball court was a long, narrow alley flanked by sloped or vertical walls, sometimes with stone rings mounted high on the walls through which the ball could be directed. The Great Ball Court at Chichén Itzá — the largest in Mesoamerica — is an architectural marvel: its walls are so precisely aligned that a whisper at one end can be heard at the other, 150 meters away.

The ball game was simultaneously an athletic contest, a religious ritual, and an act of cosmic drama. In Maya mythology, as recorded in the *Popol Vuh* (poh-POHL VOO), the sacred book of the K'iche' (kee-CHEH) Maya, the Hero Twins played a ball game against the Lords of the Underworld — and won, defeating death itself. The ball game reenacted this cosmic struggle. The movement of the ball across the court mirrored the movement of celestial bodies — the sun, the moon, Venus — across the sky. Some scholars have argued that the ball game was, at its deepest level, a model of the universe in motion.

Human sacrifice was associated with the ball game at certain times and places, though the details varied enormously. At some Maya sites, sculpted panels show decapitation following a game — the losing captain kneeling, the winning captain holding a knife. At the Aztec capital of Tenochtitlan, the game was connected to the larger ritual complex of human offering. At other sites and periods, the game appears to have been a purely athletic and social event, played for enjoyment, gambling, or the resolution of disputes.

The variability is important. The ball game was not one thing. Over three thousand years, across hundreds of cultures, it took on different meanings and served different purposes. At its most elaborate, it was a performance of cosmic order — the ball as the sun, the court as the boundary between the worlds of the living and the dead. At its most ordinary, it was a sport — players competing, spectators watching, bets being placed. These dimensions coexisted. A modern soccer match can be simultaneously an athletic contest, a community ritual, and an expression of national identity. The Mesoamerican ball game contained the same kind of layered meaning, sustained across millennia rather than decades.

The connection between the ball game and the Olmec goes beyond the rubber balls at El Manatí. At San Lorenzo, stone monuments depict scenes that some scholars interpret as ball game-related — figures in athletic postures, wearing equipment that resembles the protective gear of ball players. At La Venta, carved images of athletes or ritual performers appear on several monuments. The precise identification of these scenes as ball game depictions is debated — without written captions, any scene of a figure in motion could be interpreted in multiple ways — but the circumstantial evidence is strong.

The word "Olmec" itself points to the ball game's significance. *Olmecatl* in Nahuatl means "rubber person" or "person from the land of rubber." The Aztecs, writing two thousand years after the Olmec flourished, named them for the material that defined the region: latex, processed into the bouncing balls of the game. The Olmec were, in Aztec memory, the people of rubber — the people who gave Mesoamerica its most enduring ritual sport.

The ball game outlasted every civilization that played it. The Olmec invented it (or at least played the earliest version we can document). The Maya elaborated it into cosmic theater. The Aztec made it a spectacle of empire. Spanish missionaries suppressed it after the conquest, recognizing its deep connection to indigenous religion. But variations of the game survived in isolated communities. In the Sierra Madre of Sinaloa and Durango, in northwestern Mexico, a version called *ulama* (oo-LAH-mah) is still played today — a living descendant of a tradition that began in an Olmec spring three and a half millennia ago.

The rubber balls at El Manatí connect us to the very beginning of that tradition. They are small, dark, and unprepossessing — nothing like the colossal heads that dominate popular images of the Olmec. But they represent something equally extraordinary: the invention of a cultural practice that would outlast not just the Olmec but every subsequent Mesoamerican civilization, surviving conquest, colonization, and suppression to persist into the twenty-first century.

Three thousand six hundred years is a long game.
