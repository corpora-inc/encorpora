# A Note to the Reader

Eight kilometers east of present-day St. Louis, Missouri, across the Mississippi River in the flat bottomlands of southwestern Illinois, there is a mound of earth one hundred feet tall. Its base covers more than five hectares — an area larger than the base of the Great Pyramid of Giza. It was built entirely by hand, one basket of earth at a time, by a people who had no draft animals, no wheels, and no metal tools.

The mound is called Monks Mound, after a community of Trappist monks who briefly gardened on its upper terraces in the early 1800s. It is the largest earthen structure in the Americas. It was the centerpiece of a city that, at its peak around 1100 CE, held between ten thousand and forty thousand people — larger than London at the same date, larger than any other settlement on the continent north of Mexico.

The city is called Cahokia (kah-HOH-kee-ah) — another borrowed name, taken from a subtribe of the Illini people who lived in the area centuries after the city was abandoned. We do not know what the builders called it. We do not know what language they spoke. By the time Europeans arrived in the Mississippi Valley, Cahokia had been empty for more than a century. The mounds were there, but the people who built them were gone, and nobody in the region could explain them.

This is the story of America's forgotten metropolis — how it rose, what it contained, why it fell, and why a nation built on its soil spent two centuries refusing to believe that Indigenous people could have built it.
