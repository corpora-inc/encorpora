# What We Still Don't Know

**What triggered the Big Bang?** The rapid transformation of Cahokia around 1050 CE -- from farming village to planned city -- remains the central mystery. Agricultural surplus (new corn varieties), a religious revolution, a charismatic leader, large-scale migration, and even the supernova of 1054 CE have all been proposed. The most likely answer involves multiple factors converging, but the specific catalyst -- the spark that lit the transformation -- is unknown.

**What was Cahokia's political structure?** Whether Cahokia was a chiefdom, a state, or something without a clear European parallel is actively debated. The evidence for concentrated power (mass sacrifice, monumental construction) is strong, but the mechanisms of governance (how leaders were chosen, how authority was enforced, how decisions were made) are invisible in the archaeological record.

**Who were the Mound 72 sacrifices?** The identity and origin of the approximately 270 individuals buried in Mound 72 -- including the rows of apparently sacrificed young women -- remain partially unclear. Isotope analysis has shown that some individuals grew up outside the Cahokia region, but their specific communities of origin, and the criteria by which they were selected for sacrifice, are unknown. The recent reidentification of the central burial as a male-female pair rather than a single male raises further questions about the cosmological significance of the interment.

**Why was Cahokia abandoned?** Environmental degradation, climate change (the transition from the Medieval Warm Period to the Little Ice Age), internal conflict, external warfare, and a catastrophic flood around 1200 CE have all been proposed. The palisade wall suggests conflict. The deforestation evidence suggests environmental stress. The climate record suggests deteriorating growing conditions. The relative contribution of each factor -- and the sequence of events that turned decline into total abandonment -- is the subject of ongoing research.

**What was Cahokia's relationship to other Mississippian sites?** Whether Cahokia was the capital of a political network, the spiritual center of a religious movement, or simply the largest and most influential site in a decentralized cultural tradition is debated. The shared iconography of the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex could reflect political control, cultural influence, or independent development from common roots.

**What happened to Cahokia's population after abandonment?** The people who left Cahokia did not vanish -- they dispersed into the surrounding region and joined or formed other communities. Tracing the specific destinations of Cahokia's diaspora, and identifying the descendant communities of the city's population, is an active area of research using archaeological, linguistic, and genetic evidence. The Dhegihan Siouan-speaking peoples (Osage, Quapaw, Kaw, Omaha, Ponca) are among the proposed candidates for descendant communities.

**What was Cahokia's name?** We do not know what the builders called their city. "Cahokia" is a name borrowed from a subtribe of the Illini who lived in the area centuries after the city was abandoned. The original name was lost with the city itself.

These are open questions. The mounds still hold secrets.


# Key Names and Terms

**American Bottom** -- The broad floodplain of the Mississippi River in southwestern Illinois, where Cahokia is located. One of the most fertile agricultural areas in North America.

**Atwater, Caleb** -- Early American surveyor of Ohio mounds who attributed them to a Hindu civilization. His work contributed to the Mound Builder myth.

**Big Bang** -- Timothy Pauketat's term for the rapid transformation of Cahokia around 1050 CE from a modest village to a planned urban center.

**Birger Figurine** -- A flint clay sculpture from the Cahokia area depicting a woman hoeing a serpent whose body forms a garden, connecting agriculture and the supernatural.

**Braden style** -- An art-historical term for a regional style within the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, centered on Cahokia, with distinctive conventions for depicting falcon warriors.

**Busycon** (BEW-sih-kon) -- A genus of large marine snails (whelks) from the Gulf of Mexico, whose shells were used to make beads and engraved cups throughout the Mississippian world.

**Cahokia** (kah-HOH-kee-ah) -- The largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico, located in the American Bottom of southwestern Illinois. Flourished ~1050-1350 CE.

**Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site** -- The modern protected site encompassing the remains of the ancient city, designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1982.

**Chunkey** (CHUN-kee) -- A Mississippian sport played with a smooth stone disc and spears. A prestige game associated with elite status, gambling, and public performance.

**Craig style** -- An art-historical term for a regional style within the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, associated with the Spiro site, distinct from the Braden style.

**Dhegihan Siouan** (day-GEE-han SOO-an) -- A language family including Osage, Quapaw, Kaw, Omaha, and Ponca, whose speakers are among the proposed descendant communities of Cahokia.

**Etowah** (EH-toh-wah) -- A major Mississippian site in northwestern Georgia, known for its carved marble figurines and repousse copper plates.

**Flint clay** -- A rare geological material found near Cahokia that can be sculpted and fired like ceramic but retains fine detail, used to create the Birger, Keller, and Willoughby figurines.

**Fowler, Melvin** -- American archaeologist (1924-2008) who excavated Mound 72 and discovered the paramount burial and mass sacrifice evidence.

**Grand Plaza** -- The approximately nineteen-hectare open space at the base of Monks Mound, artificially leveled and maintained for public gatherings and ceremonies.

**Grave Creek Tablet** -- A small inscribed stone allegedly found in a West Virginia mound in 1838, widely cited as proof of Old World contact but now recognized as a probable forgery.

**Indian Removal Act** (1830) -- US federal law authorizing the forced relocation of Indigenous peoples from east of the Mississippi. The Mound Builder myth was used to justify this policy.

**Little Ice Age** -- A period of cooler, more variable climate beginning around 1250-1300 CE, which may have reduced maize yields and contributed to Cahokia's decline.

**Lohmann phase** -- The archaeological phase preceding Cahokia's Big Bang, characterized by dispersed settlement and diverse pottery styles.

**Medieval Warm Period** -- A period of relatively warm climate (roughly 900-1250 CE) that provided favorable growing conditions during Cahokia's rise.

**Mill Creek chert** -- A type of stone quarried in southern Illinois, used to produce heavy hoes for maize cultivation that were traded across the Mississippian world.

**Mississippian** -- The broad cultural tradition of mound-building, maize-farming societies in eastern North America, roughly 800-1600 CE.

**Monks Mound** -- The largest earthen structure in the Americas, approximately 100 feet tall with a base larger than the Great Pyramid of Giza. Built in stages over approximately three centuries.

**Moorehead phase** -- The archaeological phase of Cahokia's decline (roughly 1200-1350 CE), characterized by reduced population, palisade construction, and declining long-distance trade.

**Mound 72** -- A ridgetop mound at Cahokia containing the burial of two paramount figures on a bird-shaped platform of 20,000 shell beads, surrounded by over 270 additional interments including evidence of mass sacrifice.

**Moundville** -- The second-largest Mississippian site, located on the Black Warrior River in Alabama. Featured 29 mounds and a population of several thousand.

**Northern Flint corn** -- An eight-row maize variety adapted to shorter growing seasons, whose spread through the Eastern Woodlands around 1000 CE may have contributed to Cahokia's rise.

**Pauketat, Timothy** (POW-keh-tat) -- American archaeologist, leading modern scholar of Cahokia. Author of *Cahokia: Ancient America's Great City on the Mississippi* (2009).

**Rogan Plates** -- Two repousse copper plates from Etowah depicting falcon-costumed warriors, among the most famous artifacts of Mississippian culture.

**Southeastern Ceremonial Complex** -- The shared symbolic system of the Mississippian world, including falcon imagery, the cross-in-circle motif, the hand-and-eye motif, and depictions of supernatural beings. Formerly called the "Southern Cult."

**Spiro** -- A major Mississippian site in eastern Oklahoma, containing one of the richest concentrations of ritual art in the Mississippian world. Severely damaged by looting in the 1930s.

**Stirling phase** -- The archaeological phase of Cahokia's peak (roughly 1050-1200 CE), characterized by rapid growth, monumental construction, and the spread of standardized ceramic styles.

**Thomas, Cyrus** -- American entomologist and archaeologist (1825-1910). His 1894 report for the Smithsonian's Bureau of Ethnology proved that the mounds were built by the ancestors of living Native Americans.

**Wall-trench construction** -- A building method that appeared at Cahokia during the Big Bang, in which posts are set into a continuous trench rather than individual holes, producing faster and more standardized construction.

**Wittry, Warren** -- American archaeologist who identified the Woodhenge timber circles at Cahokia in the 1960s.

**Woodhenge** -- Circles of wooden posts at Cahokia aligned to solstice and equinox sunrise positions, used as astronomical observatories and calendrical instruments. Rebuilt at least five times.


# Further Reading

**For the general reader:**

- Pauketat, Timothy R. *Cahokia: Ancient America's Great City on the Mississippi*. Penguin, 2009. -- The best modern introduction, by the leading Cahokian scholar. Accessible, well-argued, and vivid.

- Young, Biloine Whiting, and Melvin L. Fowler. *Cahokia: The Great Native American Metropolis*. University of Illinois Press, 2000. -- A comprehensive overview with excellent illustrations.

- Mann, Charles C. *1491: New Revelations of the Americas Before Columbus*. Vintage, 2006. -- An outstanding survey of pre-Columbian civilizations, with a strong chapter on Cahokia and the Mississippian world.

**For the archaeology:**

- Pauketat, Timothy R., and Thomas E. Emerson, eds. *Cahokia: Domination and Ideology in the Mississippian World*. University of Nebraska Press, 1997. -- Scholarly essays on power, ritual, and politics at Cahokia.

- Chappell, Sally A. Kitt. *Cahokia: Mirror of the Cosmos*. University of Chicago Press, 2002. -- Explores Cahokia's cosmological symbolism and astronomical alignments.

- Emerson, Thomas E. *Cahokia and the Archaeology of Power*. University of Alabama Press, 1997. -- A detailed study of Cahokian material culture and its political implications.

**For the Mound Builder myth:**

- Silverberg, Robert. *Mound Builders of Ancient America: The Archaeology of a Myth*. New York Graphic Society, 1968. -- The definitive history of the lost-race theory and its debunking.

- Thomas, Cyrus. *Report on the Mound Explorations of the Bureau of Ethnology*. Smithsonian Institution, 1894. -- The 730-page report that settled the question. Available digitally through the Smithsonian.

**For the broader Mississippian world:**

- Milner, George R. *The Moundbuilders: Ancient Peoples of Eastern North America*. Thames & Hudson, 2004. -- An excellent overview of mound-building cultures from the Archaic period through the Mississippian.

- King, Adam, ed. *Southeastern Ceremonial Complex: Chronology, Content, Context*. University of Alabama Press, 2007. -- The most current scholarly treatment of the shared Mississippian symbolic system.
