# Part Three: The Myth of the Mound Builders

## Chapter 7 — The Lie

When European settlers moved into the Ohio and Mississippi valleys in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, they found thousands of earthen mounds scattered across the landscape. Some were burial mounds -- conical heaps of earth covering the dead. Some were platform mounds -- flat-topped pyramids that had once supported buildings. Some were shaped like animals -- the Great Serpent Mound in Ohio is a quarter-mile-long earthen effigy of a snake swallowing an egg. The mounds were clearly old, clearly the work of organized societies, and clearly the product of enormous labor.

![The Great Serpent Mound in Ohio, seen from above](../images/placeholders/12-great-serpent-mound.png)

The settlers refused to believe that the ancestors of the Indigenous peoples they were displacing had built them.

Instead, a myth took hold -- one of the most consequential lies in American intellectual history. The mounds, it was claimed, had been built by a "lost race" -- a vanished civilization of advanced people who had occupied the continent before the arrival of the Indians. Candidates for this lost race included the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel, Welsh princes following the legendary explorer Madoc, the Vikings, the Phoenicians, the Hindus, and survivors of Atlantis. The specific identity varied, but the core claim was consistent: the mounds were too sophisticated, too large, and too well-engineered to have been built by the ancestors of the peoples the settlers were encountering.

This was a lie built on racism, and it served a specific political purpose.

If a "lost white race" had built the mounds and been subsequently destroyed or displaced by the Indians, then the Indians were not the original inhabitants of the land -- they were invaders who had stolen it from earlier, more civilized occupants. And if the Indians were invaders rather than original inhabitants, then displacing them was not a crime but an act of restoration. The American government was not taking Indian land; it was reclaiming land that had been stolen from a lost civilization. The logic was circular, self-serving, and extraordinarily effective.

This reasoning was not subtle, and it was not confined to the uneducated. Thomas Jefferson explored mounds in Virginia -- he excavated one near Monticello in 1784, making him one of the earliest archaeological excavators in America -- and while Jefferson was more careful than most, noting the resemblance between mound artifacts and known Indian crafts, his excavation did not settle the question in the public mind. William Henry Harrison, the ninth president of the United States, wrote confidently about the mound builders as a lost race. Caleb Atwater, the first person to systematically survey the mounds of Ohio, attributed them to a Hindu civilization that had migrated from India. Josiah Priest, in his enormously popular 1833 book *American Antiquities and Discoveries in the West*, catalogued dozens of mound sites and argued that they proved the existence of an ancient white civilization that had been exterminated by savage Indians. The book went through multiple editions and sold widely. The fantasy of the lost race was mainstream American thought.

The myth even found its way into scripture -- or what some treated as scripture. Joseph Smith, founder of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, published the Book of Mormon in 1830, telling the story of an ancient Israelite people who migrated to the Americas, built a civilization, and were eventually destroyed in a great war. The mounds of Ohio and the Mississippi Valley were widely cited by early Latter-day Saints as physical evidence of the Book of Mormon's narrative. The mound builders were the Nephites; the Indians were the descendants of the Lamanites who had destroyed them. The relationship between the Book of Mormon narrative and the mound builder myth is complex -- Smith drew on the same cultural milieu that produced the myth, and the myth in turn drew support from the scriptural narrative. The two reinforced each other.

The scientific establishment was slow to challenge the myth because the scientific establishment, in early nineteenth-century America, barely existed. Archaeology as a professional discipline was in its infancy. The Smithsonian Institution was not founded until 1846. Universities with research programs in archaeology emerged even later. In the vacuum of professional expertise, amateur collectors, travel writers, and politicians filled the interpretive space, and their interpretations served their interests.

Some voices of dissent existed. The ethnologist Henry Rowe Schoolcraft, who lived among the Ojibwe people of the Great Lakes and married an Ojibwe woman, pointed out as early as the 1830s that living Indigenous peoples maintained traditions of mound building and that the mounds were consistent with known Indigenous cultural practices. Albert Gallatin, the former Secretary of the Treasury who became one of America's first serious ethnologists, argued in the 1840s that the mound builders were the ancestors of living Native Americans. These arguments were largely ignored. They contradicted what the public wanted to believe.

The Mound Builder myth was directly invoked in support of the Indian Removal Act of 1830, the law that authorized the forced relocation of tens of thousands of Indigenous people from their homelands east of the Mississippi to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma). If the mounds proved that Indians were not the original occupants, then removing them was not dispossession -- it was justice. The lie about the mounds became a justification for one of the most devastating policies in American history. Roughly 60,000 people were forcibly removed from their homes during the 1830s. Thousands died on the journey -- the Trail of Tears. The connection between a false story about ancient mounds and the deaths of thousands of living people is direct and documented.

The myth persisted for decades, resisting evidence that should have demolished it. Explorers and early archaeologists found Indian artifacts -- pottery, stone tools, ornaments in recognizable Indigenous styles -- inside and among the mounds, demonstrating continuity between the mound-building cultures and living Native American peoples. Burial practices in the mounds matched burial practices documented among Southeastern peoples. The architectural techniques -- earth basketloading, platform construction, mound-and-plaza layouts -- had clear parallels in communities that were still building mounds when Europeans first arrived. The evidence was dismissed, reinterpreted, or ignored. The myth was too useful to abandon.

The Grave Creek Mound affair illustrates the atmosphere. In 1838, a small inscribed stone was allegedly found during the excavation of the Grave Creek Mound, a large Adena-culture burial mound in present-day West Virginia. The stone bore characters that enthusiasts identified as Phoenician, Hebrew, Runic, Libyan, or Celtic, depending on the interpreter. The "Grave Creek Tablet" was cited for decades as proof that literate Old World peoples had built the mounds. Modern analysis has identified the inscription as a probable forgery -- the characters are an inconsistent jumble of symbols borrowed from multiple scripts, exactly what an early-nineteenth-century forger with access to a comparative alphabet chart might produce. The tablet was a fraud. It was also widely believed.

The Mound Builder myth was finally killed -- or at least dealt a mortal wound -- by a single government report. In 1882, the Smithsonian Institution's Bureau of Ethnology assigned a team led by Cyrus Thomas to settle the question once and for all. Thomas had initially believed in the lost-race theory. Over the next twelve years, his team excavated hundreds of mounds across the eastern United States, cataloged their contents, and analyzed the evidence systematically. The scope was unprecedented: Thomas's team investigated mounds in twenty-four states.

Thomas's report, published in 1894 as the *Report on the Mound Explorations of the Bureau of Ethnology*, ran to 730 pages and was devastating to the lost-race theory. The mounds were built by the ancestors of living Native American peoples. The artifacts inside them were consistent with known Indigenous cultural traditions. The construction techniques were consistent with methods still practiced by Indigenous communities. The geographic distribution of mound types corresponded to the distribution of Indigenous cultural groups. There was no mystery. There was no lost race. The mound builders were the ancestors of the Muscogee (Creek), the Cherokee, the Osage, the Quapaw, the Chickasaw, and dozens of other peoples that the United States had already removed from their homelands.

Thomas's conversion -- from believer in the lost-race theory to its destroyer -- gives the report its particular force. He began the project expecting to find evidence for the lost race. He found the opposite. The method was straightforward: excavate mounds across a wide geographic area, catalog what you find, and compare the artifacts to known Indigenous cultural traditions. The results were overwhelming. Mounds in Ohio contained pottery identical to pottery made by the Shawnee and the Fort Ancient peoples. Mounds in the Mississippi Valley contained artifacts consistent with the traditions of the Quapaw, the Chickasaw, and the Muscogee. Mounds in the Southeast contained shell gorgets and copper plates in styles that ethnographers had documented among living Southeastern peoples. The continuity was clear, site after site, state after state, mound after mound.

The intellectual honesty required to publish a 730-page report demolishing your own starting hypothesis is considerable. Thomas did it anyway, and the scientific question was settled. The mounds were built by Native Americans. The lost race never existed.

There is a bitter irony in the timing. Thomas's report was published in 1894 -- sixty-four years after the Indian Removal Act, thirty years after the forced removal was complete, and two decades after the last major military campaigns against Plains Indians. The proof that Indigenous peoples were the mound builders arrived too late to prevent the policies that the lie had justified. The mounds had served their purpose as a weapon. By the time the truth was established, the damage was done.

The report settled the question in the scientific community. It did not, unfortunately, enter the popular imagination with equal force. Variants of the lost-race theory persist to this day -- in amateur archaeology, in certain television programs that present "ancient mysteries" as unsolved when scholarship has long since solved them, and in the general failure of American education to teach the sophistication and achievements of pre-Columbian North American civilizations. The pattern is remarkably persistent: when Indigenous achievements are too impressive to ignore, a certain kind of commentator will reach for an explanation that attributes them to someone else -- aliens, lost civilizations, wandering Europeans. The refusal to credit Indigenous peoples with their own achievements is the Mound Builder myth in modern dress.

Most Americans have heard of the Maya and the Aztec. Most have never heard of Cahokia. The ignorance is not accidental. It is the residue of a lie that served its purpose for a century and never fully died.


## Chapter 8 — What We Still Don't Know

Even after decades of professional archaeology, fundamental questions about Cahokia remain unanswered. The site has been studied by some of the most capable scholars in American archaeology, and they have made extraordinary progress -- but the questions that remain are as fascinating as the questions that have been answered.

The political structure of Cahokia is the most basic of these. Was Cahokia a chiefdom -- a society led by a paramount chief whose authority rested on religious prestige and the redistribution of surplus? Or was it a state -- a society with formalized institutions, a bureaucracy, a system of laws, and the coercive apparatus (police, military) to enforce them? The distinction matters because it shapes how we understand the mechanisms of Cahokian power.

The evidence is ambiguous. The Mound 72 burial -- with its mass sacrifices, its twenty-thousand-bead platform, and its clear hierarchy of interments -- suggests concentrated power of an extreme kind. The labor mobilization required for Monks Mound and the palisade implies an authority capable of directing thousands of workers. These features are consistent with a state-level society, or at least a very powerful chiefdom approaching the characteristics of a state.

On the other hand, Cahokia left no written records, no legal codes, no administrative archives. The bureaucratic apparatus of a state -- the records, the registers, the formal offices -- is absent from the archaeological record. This may mean that Cahokia governed through informal mechanisms (charisma, kinship networks, religious authority) rather than formal institutions. Or it may mean that the administrative records existed on perishable media (bark, leather, textiles) that have not survived. The Hawaiian chiefdoms of the pre-contact period provide a useful parallel -- complex, hierarchical societies with impressive monuments and organized labor, capable of coordinating the actions of thousands of people, yet governed through kinship and religious authority rather than bureaucratic institutions. Cahokia may have been something similar: a political system that achieved state-level outcomes through chiefdom-level mechanisms.

The cause of Cahokia's abandonment remains genuinely uncertain. The environmental, climatic, social, and political factors described in Chapter 6 are all plausible, and most scholars favor a multi-causal explanation. But the relative weight of each factor -- and the sequence of events that turned decline into total abandonment -- is unknown. Did the environment fail first, pulling the political system down with it? Or did the political system fail first, leaving a weakened society unable to manage the environmental challenges? The order matters, and the evidence does not resolve it.

The complete absence of Cahokia from the historical record is itself a mystery. No European ever saw Cahokia as a functioning city. The first Europeans to visit the site -- French missionaries and explorers in the late 1600s -- found only mounds and no clear oral traditions among local peoples explaining them. This is a gap of perhaps 150 to 250 years between Cahokia's abandonment and the first European contact in the region. During those centuries, the memory of who built the city and why was apparently lost.

How does a city of fifteen to twenty thousand people -- the largest on the continent -- vanish from collective memory in two or three generations? The question is sharpened by comparison. In the American Southwest, the Pueblo peoples maintain oral traditions about migrations, abandoned settlements, and ancestral communities that took place centuries ago. The Hopi trace their clan migrations across vast distances, preserving the memory of specific places and events that archaeology has confirmed. Why did no comparable oral tradition preserve the memory of Cahokia?

The answer may lie in the nature of the abandonment itself. If the population dispersed gradually, scattering into small communities across a wide area, the concentrated knowledge of the city might have fragmented as well. A story about a great city loses coherence when the people who remember it are scattered among dozens of small villages, each developing its own local traditions. Or the knowledge might have been deliberately suppressed -- if Cahokia's end was associated with social upheaval, warfare, or the failure of a religious system that had demanded human sacrifice, the people who left might have had reasons to forget.

The question of descendant communities is related and actively debated. Where did the people of Cahokia go? Linguistic and archaeological evidence has led some scholars to propose connections between Cahokia and the Dhegihan Siouan-speaking peoples -- the Osage, the Quapaw (KWAW-paw), the Kaw, the Omaha, and the Ponca -- whose oral traditions describe migrations from a large settlement in the Mississippi Valley. The Osage creation narratives describe a journey from a great center, and their historical territory included the Ozark Plateau west of the American Bottom. The connection is suggestive but unproven. Other scholars have proposed connections to the Illini, the Dhegihan, or other groups. The genetic and linguistic evidence is being developed but has not yet produced definitive answers.

The relationship between Cahokia and other Mississippian centers is another open question. Was Cahokia the capital of an empire -- the political hub that directed events at Moundville, Etowah, Spiro, and other major sites? Or was it a peer among equals -- the largest and most influential Mississippian site, but not the ruler of the others? The evidence supports the latter interpretation: Mississippian sites share cultural features (the mound-and-plaza complex, the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, the falcon-warrior imagery) but show no clear evidence of political subordination to Cahokia. The shared features may reflect a common cultural tradition -- a shared religion, a shared aesthetic, a shared understanding of how the cosmos works -- rather than political control exercised from a single center. Cahokia was the largest city in this world, and probably the most influential. It was probably not the capital.

And then there is the simplest question, and perhaps the most poignant: what was Cahokia's name? We do not know what the builders called their city. "Cahokia" is a name borrowed from a subtribe of the Illini who lived in the area centuries after the city was abandoned. The original name -- the word that the builders spoke when they referred to their home, the name that tens of thousands of people used in daily conversation for three centuries -- was lost with the city itself. Somewhere in the dispersal, in the forgetting, in the gap between the last inhabitants and the first European visitors, the name slipped away. The greatest city in North America is known by a name that is not its own.

These are open questions. The mounds still hold secrets. Cahokia has been the subject of professional archaeology for over a century, and yet only a small fraction of the site has been excavated. The central precinct alone contains dozens of unexcavated mounds. Residential neighborhoods that were destroyed by modern development were never studied. The surrounding American Bottom, with its smaller Mississippian communities and its agricultural landscape, is only partially mapped. Every excavation season produces new data, new surprises, and new questions.

What is clear is that Cahokia was extraordinary -- a planned city of unprecedented size, built in an unprecedented time frame, sustained by an unprecedented network of continental connections, and abandoned so completely that even its memory was lost. It is the most remarkable archaeological site in North America, and one of the least known. The mounds still stand. The story deserves to be told.
