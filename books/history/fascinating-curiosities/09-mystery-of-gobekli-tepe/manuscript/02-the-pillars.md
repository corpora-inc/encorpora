# Part Two: The Pillars

## Chapter 3 — The T-Shaped Giants

The pillars of Göbekli Tepe are the site's defining feature -- the objects that make it unique in the archaeological record and that have captured the public imagination since the excavations began. Each pillar is carved from a single block of limestone, quarried from the bedrock of the hill and its immediate surroundings. The pillars range in height from roughly 1.5 meters for the smaller examples to 5.5 meters for the largest. The heaviest weigh approximately ten tons.

The T-shape is the pillars' most distinctive feature. Each pillar consists of a tall, rectangular shaft topped by a broad, flat horizontal piece -- the crossbar of the T. The overall form is unmistakably anthropomorphic: the horizontal top represents a stylized head, and the vertical shaft represents the body. This interpretation is confirmed by the carvings on some pillars: low-relief arms extend from the sides of the shaft, bending at the elbows, with hands and fingers depicted at the front of the pillar, meeting at the belly. The arms and hands are the arms and hands of a being -- a human figure, a deity, an ancestor, or something else entirely -- rendered in the most abstract possible form. The head has no face. The body has no legs. The being is present, but its identity is concealed.

![Close-up of a T-shaped pillar at Göbekli Tepe showing carved arms and hands meeting at the belly](../images/placeholders/03-t-pillar-closeup.png)

The pillars are arranged in circles -- or, more precisely, in roughly oval enclosures. The excavated enclosures (designated by letters: Enclosure A, B, C, D, and so on) each contain a ring of pillars set into a low stone bench or wall, surrounding two larger central pillars. The central pillars are the tallest and most elaborately carved in each enclosure. They face each other across the interior space, their flat T-shaped tops suggesting two beings in silent dialogue, or two presences guarding whatever rituals took place between them.

The number of pillars in each enclosure varies. Enclosure D, one of the most completely excavated, contains twelve pillars in the ring plus two central pillars. Enclosure C has a similar arrangement. The enclosures are not identical in size or layout, suggesting that they were built at different times, perhaps by different groups, or for different purposes.

At least twenty enclosures have been identified through geophysical survey (ground-penetrating radar and magnetic imaging), but only a fraction of the site has been excavated. Current estimates suggest that perhaps five to ten percent of the total site has been uncovered. The remaining enclosures -- their pillars still buried under thousands of years of deliberate fill -- wait beneath the hilltop. The full scale of Göbekli Tepe is unknown.

The quarrying process can be partially reconstructed from an unfinished pillar found in the bedrock quarry near the site's summit. This pillar, still attached to the bedrock on its underside, is approximately seven meters long and would weigh an estimated fifty tons if completed -- far larger than any pillar found in the excavated enclosures. The quarry workers carved around the pillar's outline, cutting trenches on both sides and at both ends, then worked to free the bottom surface from the bedrock. The process required hammering away at the limestone with stone tools -- hard flint hammers and picks -- a slow, labor-intensive process that would have produced enormous quantities of limestone chips and dust. The quarry floor is littered with the debris of this work.

The unfinished pillar was never erected. Whether it cracked during quarrying, was abandoned because of a flaw in the stone, or was simply left behind when the site's construction phase ended is unknown. Its existence demonstrates that the builders' ambitions exceeded what they achieved -- they attempted pillars of a scale beyond what they ultimately installed. The seven-meter unfinished pillar, had it been completed and erected, would have been one of the largest freestanding carved stones in the ancient world, comparable in scale to some of the stones at Stonehenge or the moai of Easter Island, and thousands of years earlier than either.

The transport of the finished pillars from the quarry to their installation sites -- a distance of, at most, a few hundred meters across the hilltop -- was accomplished without wheels, draft animals, or metal tools. Experimental archaeology and ethnographic analogy suggest that the pillars were moved on wooden rollers or sledges, hauled by teams of people pulling ropes. A ten-ton pillar would require a team of at least fifty people pulling in coordination, guided by experienced overseers who could manage the direction and speed of the movement. The entire operation required planning, organization, and leadership -- social capacities that archaeologists had previously associated with agricultural societies, not with hunter-gatherers.

The installation of each pillar in its socket required digging a foundation pit in the bedrock or prepared floor, lowering the pillar into the pit (probably by tipping it over the edge and controlling its descent with ropes), and packing the gap between the pillar and the pit walls with rubble and fill to hold it upright. The precision of the installation is evident: the pillars stand vertical, aligned with their neighbors, their T-shaped tops level. This is skilled construction work, requiring knowledge of materials, leverage, and alignment -- knowledge that was passed from one generation of builders to the next across the centuries of the site's active use.

The floors of the enclosures are surfaced with a hard, smooth plaster -- a polished terrazzo-like surface made from crushed limestone mixed with water and allowed to set. The floors slope slightly toward the center, suggesting drainage or deliberate channeling of liquids. Whether the liquids were rainwater, ritually poured substances, or something else is unknown. The plaster floors indicate a concern with cleanliness, order, and the visual appearance of the interior space -- the enclosures were not rough, utilitarian structures but carefully finished ritual environments.

Stone benches run between the pillars along the interior walls of the enclosures. These benches are low -- suitable for sitting -- and may have accommodated the participants in whatever rituals took place inside. If thirty people could sit on the benches of an enclosure (a rough estimate based on the available bench length), the enclosures were designed for gatherings of modest size -- not mass events but intimate ceremonies conducted by a relatively small group.

The construction sequence of the enclosures -- which was built first, and how the site grew over time -- is still being worked out. Radiocarbon dating provides a broad framework: the earliest enclosures date to roughly 9500-9000 BCE, and the latest to roughly 8000 BCE. Within this fifteen-hundred-year span, enclosures were built, used, modified, and eventually buried. The stratigraphy (the layering of construction and fill deposits) shows that newer enclosures were sometimes built adjacent to or partially overlapping older ones, creating a palimpsest of construction phases.

One consistent observation is that the earlier enclosures tend to be larger and more elaborate than the later ones. Enclosure D, one of the earliest, has the tallest pillars and the most detailed carvings. Later enclosures are generally smaller, with shorter pillars and simpler decoration. This pattern of decreasing scale is unusual in the history of monumental architecture -- most traditions start small and grow larger over time as the society gains wealth, population, and organizational capacity. At Göbekli Tepe, the trajectory is reversed, suggesting either a decline in the social capacity to mobilize labor or a change in the function of the enclosures that required less monumental construction.

The pillars themselves show evidence of modification over time. Some pillars have been recarved -- earlier carvings removed or covered by later additions. Others show signs of repair: fractures patched with stone or plaster, broken edges smoothed. The enclosures were not static monuments but living spaces that were maintained, modified, and updated over the centuries of their use. The temple was not built once and left alone; it was tended, altered, and renewed -- a continuous act of creation that spanned generations.

The question of who made the specific decisions about each enclosure -- its location, size, orientation, and the choice of animal carvings for each pillar -- is unanswerable with current evidence, but it implies a design process. Someone (or some group) determined which species would be carved on which pillar, how the enclosure would be oriented relative to the landscape, and where the central pillars would be placed. The consistency within each enclosure and the variation between enclosures suggest that each enclosure was conceived as a unified composition -- a deliberate arrangement of symbols, not a haphazard accumulation. Whether the designer was a religious leader, a council of elders, a specialist artist-builder, or the collective decision of the building community is unknown. The existence of a design implies a designer, and the quality of the design implies skill and experience.

![Overview of Enclosure D at Göbekli Tepe with its ring of pillars and central pair](../images/placeholders/10-enclosure-d-overview.png)

The pillar carvings include, alongside the animals, a number of enigmatic symbols: H-shapes, circles, crescents, and what may be stylized depictions of objects (nets? bags? buildings?). These non-figurative symbols have attracted attention from scholars interested in the possibility that the builders of Göbekli Tepe possessed a system of symbolic communication -- proto-writing or a set of conventional signs with shared meanings. The symbols appear on multiple pillars and at multiple sites in the Taş Tepeler network, suggesting that they were not random or individual but part of a shared repertoire. Whether these symbols encoded specific information (numbers, names, calendar dates) or served a more general symbolic function (clan marks, ritual indicators, protective signs) is debated. The tantalizing possibility that the builders of Göbekli Tepe had a system of notation -- not writing in the full sense, but a set of conventional marks with agreed-upon meanings -- remains just that: a possibility. The evidence is suggestive, not conclusive.

The sheer volume of stone worked at Göbekli Tepe is staggering. The excavated enclosures alone contain over sixty T-shaped pillars, each quarried from the bedrock, shaped, carved, transported, and erected. The unexcavated enclosures may contain hundreds more. The total weight of stone worked at the site is in the hundreds of tons -- all of it shaped by hand, using stone tools, by people who had no metal, no wheels, and no draft animals. The scale of the undertaking is comparable to other great stone-working traditions of the ancient world -- Stonehenge, Easter Island, Tiwanaku -- and predates all of them by thousands of years.


## Chapter 4 — The Animal Kingdom

The pillars of Göbekli Tepe are covered with carved animals. The carvings are executed in both low relief (raised from the surface of the pillar) and high relief (projecting significantly from the surface), and in some cases as three-dimensional sculptures attached to the pillar's face. The variety of animals represented is striking, and the choice of species is revealing.

The most commonly depicted animals are foxes, snakes, wild boar, aurochs (wild cattle), cranes, vultures, scorpions, spiders, lions, leopards, and wild sheep. Gazelles appear less frequently. Domestic animals are entirely absent -- there are no cattle, no sheep in domesticated form, no goats, no dogs. The absence of domestic animals is consistent with the pre-agricultural dating of the site: the animals carved on the pillars are the animals of the wild world, the world before farming.

![Carved animals on a pillar at Göbekli Tepe: fox, snake, and crane in relief](../images/placeholders/04-pillar-animal-carvings.png)

The choice of animals is not a random sample of the local fauna. The species depicted are overwhelmingly wild, dangerous, or symbolically potent. Foxes, snakes, and scorpions are dangerous -- their bites and stings can harm or kill. Lions and leopards (now extinct in the region) are apex predators. Aurochs were massive and dangerous -- a wild bull weighing up to a ton, with wide horns, capable of killing a hunter. Vultures feed on the dead. Cranes are migratory birds that arrive and depart with the seasons, connecting the earth to the sky through their vertical flights.

The animals are not depicted in scenes of daily life -- there are no hunting scenes, no images of people pursuing or killing animals. The animals are presented as autonomous beings, occupying the surfaces of the pillars as if the pillars were their territory. They crawl up the shafts, stretch across the faces, and cluster in groups. In some cases, the animals appear to be moving -- walking, crawling, slithering -- across the pillar's surface. The overall impression is of a world teeming with wild life, a world in which animals are the dominant presence and human beings (represented only by the abstract T-shaped pillars themselves) are secondary.

One pillar in Enclosure D -- known informally as "the vulture stone" or "Pillar 43" -- has received particular attention. The pillar's face is carved with a complex scene: a large vulture with outstretched wings, a headless human figure, a scorpion, and several other animals. The headless figure has been interpreted as a representation of death -- a body from which the head (the seat of identity) has been removed, attended by a vulture (a bird associated with death and the consumption of the dead). The scorpion may represent a constellation or a season. The scene as a whole may depict a funerary practice, an astronomical event, or a mythological narrative -- or all three simultaneously.

The practice of excarnation -- exposing the dead to vultures and other scavengers, allowing the flesh to be consumed before the bones are collected and buried -- is documented in several early Neolithic communities in the region, including the famous site of Çatalhöyük (chah-TAHL-huh-yook) in central Turkey, where painted murals depict vultures feeding on headless human bodies. If the vulture stone at Göbekli Tepe depicts excarnation, it may represent one of the earliest known depictions of a funerary practice -- a visual record of how the builders of the temple dealt with their dead.

The interpretation of the animal carvings is further complicated by the fact that different enclosures appear to emphasize different species. Enclosure A features snakes prominently. Enclosure B features foxes. Enclosure C features wild boar. Enclosure D features a more diverse menagerie. This variation has led some scholars to propose that the enclosures were associated with different social groups -- clans or lineages identified by animal totems. In this interpretation, each enclosure was built by and for a specific group within the larger hunter-gatherer population, and the dominant animal depicted in each enclosure was the group's identifying symbol. The enclosures were not a single unified temple but a collection of clan houses or lineage shrines, each marked by its totem animal.

The fox carvings at Göbekli Tepe deserve particular attention. Foxes are among the most commonly depicted animals at the site, appearing on multiple pillars in multiple enclosures. In Enclosure B, foxes are prominently displayed, and in several cases, the fox carvings show the animals in active, dynamic postures -- leaping, running, or interacting with each other. At Nevalı Çori, the related site excavated by Hauptmann before it was flooded, fox bones were found deposited in significant quantities -- suggesting that foxes had symbolic or ritual importance in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic cultures of the region. The emphasis on foxes is puzzling because foxes are not a significant food source; they are small, their meat is not highly valued, and they are difficult to hunt. Their prominence in the art suggests a symbolic role: the fox as trickster, as liminal creature (active at dawn and dusk, between day and night), as a being that moves between the human world and the wild world.

The snakes at Göbekli Tepe are rendered with particular care. Some snake carvings extend the full height of a pillar, crawling upward from the base to the capital in sinuous curves. The snakes are depicted naturalistically, with visible scales and body proportions that identify them as specific species -- probably the Near Eastern viper or the Montpellier snake, both of which are native to the region and both of which are venomous. The vertical orientation of the snake carvings -- climbing the pillar from bottom to top -- may encode a cosmological concept: the snake moving between the underworld (below) and the sky (above), connecting the realms of the dead and the living.

The totem hypothesis is plausible but unproven. It draws on ethnographic analogies from modern hunter-gatherer societies in which social groups are identified by animal names and symbols (the "Bear Clan," the "Eagle Clan," and so on). Whether such social organization existed twelve thousand years ago, among the specific population that built Göbekli Tepe, is unknown. The hypothesis fits the evidence, but other interpretations are possible: the different animals may represent different seasons, different rituals, different phases of construction, or different mythological narratives.

One further observation about the animals: there are almost no depictions of plants. The carvings are overwhelmingly zoomorphic -- animals dominate, with geometric patterns and abstract symbols as secondary elements. This is consistent with a hunter-gatherer worldview in which the most important relationships are between humans and animals, not between humans and plants. The spiritual world of the Göbekli Tepe builders, as expressed in their art, was an animal world -- a world of predators and prey, of dangerous encounters and respectful observation. The absence of plant imagery does not prove that the builders ignored plants (they clearly ate wild grains), but it suggests that plants occupied a less prominent place in their symbolic universe than animals did. This would change dramatically in the millennia that followed: in agricultural societies, plants -- particularly grain -- became central to religion, mythology, and art. The "fertility goddess" figures of later Neolithic cultures, often associated with grain and agricultural abundance, represent a worldview fundamentally different from the animal-centered vision at Göbekli Tepe.

The carved animals at Göbekli Tepe are also, in many cases, larger than life. Snakes that stretch the full height of a pillar are vastly larger than any real snake. Foxes and boar are depicted at scales that emphasize their presence and power. The animals are not illustrations from a naturalist's field guide; they are monumental presences, amplified to match the scale of the architecture. The enlargement of the animals relative to the abstract human forms of the T-shaped pillars creates a hierarchy of scale: the animals are large and prominent; the humans are reduced to geometric abstractions. In this world, the animals are the dominant beings.

What is beyond dispute is that the builders of Göbekli Tepe lived in a world dominated by wild animals, and that they chose to make those animals the central subject of their monumental art. The pillars are a bestiary -- a catalog of the wild world as the builders knew it. And they built this catalog at the exact moment in history when that wild world was about to be transformed forever. Within a few centuries of Göbekli Tepe's construction, the people of the region would begin domesticating the wild grains that grew on the nearby hills and the wild sheep and goats that grazed on the grasslands. The wild world depicted on the pillars would give way to a domesticated landscape of fields, pastures, and villages. The temple on the hill was, in a sense, a farewell -- a monument to a world that was about to end.


## Chapter 5 — The Deliberate Burial

Around 8000 BCE -- roughly fifteen hundred years after the earliest enclosures were built -- Göbekli Tepe was deliberately buried.

The burial was not destruction. The pillars were not toppled, broken, or defaced (though some show signs of earlier modification or recarving). Instead, the enclosures were carefully filled with soil, rubble, and limestone fragments -- thousands of cubic meters of fill material brought to the site and deposited in and around the enclosures, burying the pillars and their carvings beneath a thick layer of earth. The process was systematic: the fill contains sorted layers of different materials, indicating that the burial was organized, not random. It was not the result of natural erosion or collapse. It was an intentional act, carried out over an extended period, by people who chose to bury the temple they had built.

The burial was thorough. In some enclosures, the fill reached to the top of the tallest pillars -- several meters of accumulated material, deposited in layers, packing every space between and around the pillars. The sheer volume of fill material is significant: moving thousands of cubic meters of soil and rubble to the hilltop, without wheelbarrows or carts, required substantial organized labor. The burial was itself a major construction project, comparable in effort to the original construction of the enclosures. The builders invested nearly as much work in hiding the temple as they had in building it.

The burial preserved the site remarkably well. The fill material protected the pillars and their carvings from weathering, erosion, and (until modern times) from looting. When Schmidt began excavating in 1995, the pillars were found in excellent condition -- their carvings sharp and clear, their surfaces intact, their positions undisturbed. Ten thousand years of burial had conserved them as effectively as any museum.

Why would the builders bury their temple?

The question has generated numerous hypotheses, none definitive. The most commonly discussed explanations include:

Ritual termination. In many cultures, sacred objects and spaces are "killed" or decommissioned through deliberate destruction or burial when they are no longer in use. The practice is documented in Mesoamerica (as discussed in earlier volumes of this series -- the mutilation and burial of monuments at San Lorenzo, the burning of temples at Teotihuacan and Tula) and in other traditions worldwide. If the builders of Göbekli Tepe followed a similar logic, the burial may represent the ritual end of the site's active life -- a way of closing the temple, returning it to the earth, and sealing its power within the hill.

![Workers filling in an enclosure at Göbekli Tepe -- artistic reconstruction of the deliberate burial](../images/placeholders/05-burial-reconstruction.png)

Replacement by new forms. The period of Göbekli Tepe's burial (~8000 BCE) coincides with a broader transition in the region: the shift from pre-agricultural to agricultural life, from hunter-gatherer communities to settled farming villages. The monumental architecture of Göbekli Tepe, built by and for hunter-gatherers, may have become obsolete as the society that created it transformed into something different. The temple of the wild world was buried as the tame world -- the world of fields and flocks -- replaced it. New forms of ritual and social organization, suited to the new agricultural way of life, may have made the old temple unnecessary.

Concealment. The burial may have been intended to hide the site -- to prevent its reuse by others, to protect its sacred contents from desecration, or to remove it from the landscape as a visible landmark. If the enclosures were associated with specific social groups (as the totem hypothesis suggests), the burial of an enclosure may have marked the dissolution or departure of that group.

Preservation. The burial may have been intended to protect the site for the future -- to preserve the temple in the earth, sealed and intact, for a time when it might be reopened or remembered. This interpretation attributes to the builders a sense of their own place in time, a recognition that the world was changing and that the things they had built deserved to be kept safe.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. The burial may have been simultaneously a ritual act, a practical response to social change, and an act of preservation. The full answer, if it exists, is buried with the site's unexcavated enclosures -- and with the minds of the people who made the decision to fill in the temple they had spent centuries building.

The stratigraphy of the site -- the sequence of construction and burial layers -- reveals that the enclosures were not all built or buried at the same time. The earliest enclosures (the deepest layers, dated to roughly 9500-9000 BCE) are the most elaborate: the largest pillars, the most detailed carvings, the most carefully finished floors. Later enclosures, built above and adjacent to the earlier ones, tend to be smaller, with smaller pillars and less elaborate carving. The trend is toward simplification -- a gradual reduction in scale and complexity over the site's roughly fifteen-hundred-year construction period.

This pattern of declining complexity is itself puzzling. In most archaeological sequences, monumental architecture starts small and gets bigger over time as the society that builds it grows in population, wealth, and organizational capacity. At Göbekli Tepe, the pattern is reversed: the earliest constructions are the grandest, and the later ones are more modest. Schmidt interpreted this as evidence that the social organization that built the earliest enclosures was not sustained -- that the initial burst of communal effort, perhaps driven by religious fervor or social competition, was not matched by subsequent generations. The first builders reached a peak of ambition and skill that their successors could not maintain.

An alternative interpretation is that the later, smaller enclosures reflect a change in function rather than a decline in capacity. The large, elaborate enclosures may have served a regional function -- gathering dispersed groups from across a wide area -- while the later, smaller enclosures served a more local function, built by and for smaller communities. As the regional population transitioned from mobile hunting and gathering to settled agriculture, the need for large-scale gathering places may have diminished, and the monumental architecture scaled down accordingly.

There is a further dimension to the burial. The fill material itself has been studied, and it contains an enormous quantity of animal bones, stone tools, and other cultural debris. Oliver Dietrich and his colleagues have argued that the fill was not random rubble but included the remains of feasting events -- bones of wild animals butchered and consumed in quantities consistent with large communal meals. If correct, the burial itself may have been accompanied by feasting -- a final series of communal meals marking the end of the enclosure's active life, the refuse of the farewell feast becoming part of the burial fill. The act of burial, in this interpretation, was not a simple engineering operation but a ritual event, a ceremony of closure that mirrored the ceremonies of construction.

The mystery of the burial, like the mystery of the construction, points to the same fundamental question: what did Göbekli Tepe mean to the people who built it? The construction tells us that the site mattered enormously -- enough to justify years of organized labor by people with no surplus economy. The burial tells us that the site's time came to an end -- but that the ending was managed with the same care and intention as the construction. The builders of Göbekli Tepe built something extraordinary, used it for fifteen hundred years, and then put it to rest. The hill they created above it -- the "Potbelly Hill" that local farmers plowed around for centuries -- is itself a monument: a burial mound for a temple.
