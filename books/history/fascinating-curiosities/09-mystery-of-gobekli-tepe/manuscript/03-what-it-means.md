# Part Three: What It Means

## Chapter 6 — The Labor Problem

The central practical question about Göbekli Tepe is simple: how did hunter-gatherers organize the labor required to build it?

The problem is not one of technology. The tools used to quarry and carve the pillars -- stone hammers, flint picks, and abrasive sand -- are simple and well understood. The problem is one of organization. Quarrying a ten-ton pillar, carving it, transporting it several hundred meters, and erecting it in a prepared socket requires dozens of workers, working together for days or weeks. Feeding those workers -- in a pre-agricultural economy -- requires a reliable food supply. Coordinating the work requires leadership. And doing all of this repeatedly, over a period of centuries, to build at least twenty enclosures, each containing a dozen or more pillars, requires a social system capable of sustained collective action.

Hunter-gatherer societies, as traditionally understood by anthropologists, are small-scale, egalitarian, and mobile. Groups of twenty to fifty people follow the seasonal movements of game and the ripening of wild plants, camping for days or weeks at each location before moving on. Leadership is informal -- based on experience, skill, and persuasion rather than inherited authority. Accumulation of wealth is limited by the need to carry everything you own from camp to camp. Large-scale construction projects are, in this model, impossible: there are too few people, too little food surplus, and no mechanism for organizing collective labor.

Göbekli Tepe demonstrates that this model, while useful as a generalization, is too rigid. The people who built the temple were hunter-gatherers -- the food remains confirm this -- but they were hunter-gatherers with social complexity that the standard model does not accommodate. They had the ability to organize large labor forces, to plan and execute multi-year construction projects, and to create monumental architecture of lasting quality. They did this without agriculture, without cities, and without writing. The lesson of Göbekli Tepe is that complexity can arise from many sources, and that the capacity for collective action is not a product of farming -- it is a product of human social intelligence, which existed long before the first seed was planted.

The feasting hypothesis offers one mechanism for understanding how the labor was organized. Joris Peters (YOR-iss PAY-ters), an archaeozoologist at Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich, has analyzed the animal bone assemblages from Göbekli Tepe and identified evidence of large-scale feasting. The bones are dominated by wild game -- primarily gazelle and aurochs -- and many bones show evidence of butchering, roasting, and consumption in quantities far exceeding the needs of a small residential group. The assemblages include the bones of many individuals of the same species processed at the same time, consistent with mass kill events followed by communal feasting.

![Artistic reconstruction of a feast gathering at Göbekli Tepe with roasting pits and stone enclosures](../images/placeholders/06-gobekli-tepe-feast.png)

In many traditional societies, feasting serves as a mechanism for mobilizing labor. A leader or organizer hosts a feast, providing food and drink for the participants, who in return contribute labor for a construction project, a hunt, or another collective activity. The feast creates an obligation: the participants owe a debt to the host, payable in work. Repeated cycles of feasting and labor can generate sustained construction over months or years, as long as the organizer can continue to provide the feasts.

Stone vessels found at Göbekli Tepe -- large troughs carved from limestone, with capacities of up to 160 liters -- may have been used for preparing and serving beverages at feasts. Chemical analysis of residues in the troughs has detected traces of oxalate, a compound produced during the brewing of grain-based beverages. If the builders of Göbekli Tepe were brewing beer from wild grains -- a possibility that several researchers have explored -- the feasting hypothesis gains an additional dimension: the gatherings were fueled not just by food but by alcohol, a substance that has served as social lubricant, ritual sacrament, and labor incentive across cultures and millennia.

The brewing hypothesis is speculative but intriguing. Beer production from wild grains is technically feasible: wild barley and wild wheat can be malted (allowed to germinate, which converts starches to sugars), mashed (soaked in water to extract the sugars), and fermented (exposed to wild yeasts, which convert the sugars to alcohol). The process does not require domesticated grains -- wild grains work perfectly well. If beer was produced at Göbekli Tepe, it may represent one of the earliest known instances of brewing -- and the motivation to produce a reliable supply of brewing grain may have contributed to the domestication of cereals. Build a temple, brew beer to feed the builders, cultivate grain to brew the beer: a chain of causation from religion to intoxication to agriculture.

The interpretation is appealing but should be treated with caution. The chemical evidence for brewing is suggestive, not conclusive. The oxalate residues could have other explanations. The stone troughs could have served other purposes -- water storage, food preparation, ritual ablution. The hypothesis remains a possibility, not a fact.

The labor estimates themselves are worth considering in detail. Archaeological estimates for the construction of a single enclosure at Göbekli Tepe range from several hundred to several thousand person-days of work. The lower estimates assume a focused team of experienced workers; the higher estimates include the quarrying, transport, and finishing of all the enclosure's components. For comparison, estimates for the construction of the bluestone circle at Stonehenge range from 4,000 to 10,000 person-days. The scales are roughly comparable, but Göbekli Tepe's builders achieved their construction thousands of years earlier, with simpler tools and (as far as we know) without the benefit of agricultural surplus.

The question of how hunter-gatherers fed the workforce has a possible answer in the site's environment. The early Holocene landscape of southeastern Turkey was, as noted earlier, exceptionally productive. Wild grains grew in natural stands dense enough to harvest with simple tools. Gazelle herds numbered in the thousands. Pistachio and almond trees provided calorie-rich nuts. A well-organized hunting and gathering population, exploiting a productive landscape, could plausibly have supported a construction workforce of a hundred or more people for weeks at a time, particularly if the construction was scheduled during seasons of peak resource availability -- the autumn grain harvest, the spring gazelle migrations.

This interpretation reframes the labor problem. The question is not "how could hunter-gatherers possibly build a temple?" but rather "what kind of hunter-gatherers could build a temple?" The answer: affluent ones. Hunter-gatherers in a productive environment, with extensive knowledge of their resources and the social organization to coordinate large groups, were capable of feats that the simplistic model of hunter-gatherer life could not accommodate. Göbekli Tepe is evidence that the range of what hunter-gatherers could achieve was far wider than previously assumed.

The concept of "affluent foragers" -- hunter-gatherer populations living in environments productive enough to support large, semi-sedentary communities with social complexity approaching that of agricultural societies -- was proposed by the anthropologist Marshall Sahlins in the 1960s, based on ethnographic studies of modern hunter-gatherers in resource-rich environments (the Pacific Northwest coast of North America, for example, where salmon-based economies supported large villages, hereditary chiefs, and elaborate art traditions). Göbekli Tepe is the archaeological equivalent of Sahlins's affluent foragers: a population whose environment was productive enough to support monumental construction without the economic transformation of agriculture. The site is not an exception to the rules of human social development; it is evidence that the rules are more flexible than previously assumed.

The social dynamics at Göbekli Tepe may have included an element of competition. In many traditional societies, communal construction projects serve a competitive function: rival groups or leaders demonstrate their prestige, wealth, and organizational ability by sponsoring larger, more elaborate constructions. The decreasing scale of enclosures over time at Göbekli Tepe -- from the grand, elaborately carved Enclosure D to the smaller, simpler later structures -- may reflect a competitive cycle in which an initial burst of rivalry between groups drove a period of ambitious construction, followed by a stabilization or decline as the competitive pressure eased or as social structures changed. The "big man" model of leadership, familiar from Melanesian ethnography, provides a possible parallel: leaders who gained status by organizing feasts and construction projects, whose authority lasted only as long as their generosity and organizational skill could maintain it. At Göbekli Tepe, the enclosures may be the material traces of competitive leadership -- each one the product of a leader's ambition, each one eventually superseded by the next.


## Chapter 7 — Not Alone

For years after Schmidt's initial discovery, Göbekli Tepe was treated as a unique site -- an anomaly, a one-of-a-kind phenomenon with no parallels. The assumption was understandable: no other site of comparable age and scale had been identified in the region or anywhere else. Göbekli Tepe appeared to be a singular achievement, the product of a unique set of circumstances that were not replicated elsewhere.

This assumption has been overturned. Göbekli Tepe is not alone.

Karahan Tepe (kah-RAH-hahn TEH-peh), located roughly thirty-five kilometers southeast of Göbekli Tepe, is the most important of the newly discovered sister sites. Excavations beginning in 2019, led by Necmi Karul (NEJ-mee kah-ROOL) of Istanbul University, have revealed a complex of carved stone pillars, enclosed spaces, and sculptural art that is contemporary with Göbekli Tepe and, in some respects, even more remarkable.

Karahan Tepe's most striking feature is a carved chamber -- a subterranean room hewn from the bedrock, containing a series of pillars arranged around a central sculptural group. The central sculpture depicts a seated human figure -- a naturalistic representation of a man, carved from the bedrock itself, sitting on a bench or seat with legs crossed or extended. Surrounding the figure are T-shaped pillars similar to those at Göbekli Tepe, along with other sculptural elements: carved animal heads, phallic forms, and geometric designs.

![T-shaped pillars and carved chamber at Karahan Tepe](../images/placeholders/07-karahan-tepe-pillars.png)

The seated human figure at Karahan Tepe is extraordinary because it is naturalistic -- a recognizable human form, not the abstract T-shape of the Göbekli Tepe pillars. The figure has a face (though weathered), a torso, and limbs. It appears to be presiding over the surrounding space, seated in a position of authority or contemplation. If the T-shaped pillars at Göbekli Tepe represent abstracted human beings, the seated figure at Karahan Tepe represents the same concept in a more literal form: a human presence at the center of a ritual space.

The Turkish government has launched the Taş Tepeler (TAHSH teh-peh-LEHR) project -- literally "Stone Hills" -- to investigate the network of pre-Neolithic sites in the Şanlıurfa region. At least twelve sites with T-shaped pillars, carved stone architecture, or other monumental features contemporary with Göbekli Tepe have been identified. The sites vary in size and complexity, from single enclosures with a few pillars to multi-enclosure complexes comparable to Göbekli Tepe itself. Together, they form a regional landscape of monumental pre-agricultural architecture -- a constellation of sacred places built by hunter-gatherers across a region of perhaps fifty by fifty kilometers.

The implications of the Taş Tepeler network are significant. Göbekli Tepe was not a unique anomaly but the largest known example of a widespread regional tradition. The builder communities were not a single isolated group but a population scattered across the landscape, connected by shared ritual practices, shared artistic traditions, and shared architectural forms. The T-shaped pillar is the visual signature of this network -- a standardized form that appears at site after site, indicating a shared symbolic vocabulary and, probably, shared religious beliefs.

The Taş Tepeler sites are not carbon copies of Göbekli Tepe. Each site has distinctive features -- different enclosure layouts, different sculptural styles, different emphases in the animal carvings. Karahan Tepe's carved chambers, hewn directly from the bedrock, have no parallel at Göbekli Tepe, where the enclosures are built from freestanding pillars set into prepared floors. The diversity across the network suggests that the T-shaped pillar tradition was a shared vocabulary -- a set of architectural and symbolic conventions that different communities adopted and adapted to their own needs, preferences, and local conditions. The relationship between the sites may have been similar to the relationship between Gothic cathedrals in medieval Europe: each built according to shared principles, each unique in its specific design and decoration, each expressing the identity and ambitions of its particular community.

The chronological relationship between the sites is still being established. Some may be contemporary with Göbekli Tepe; others may be earlier or later. If some sites predate Göbekli Tepe, the tradition may have originated elsewhere and reached Göbekli Tepe from another center. If Göbekli Tepe is the earliest, it may have been the founding site from which the tradition spread. The question of origins -- where the T-shaped pillar tradition began and how it spread across the region -- is one of the central problems that the Taş Tepeler project is designed to address.

The existence of a network of sites also illuminates the social organization of the builders. If multiple communities, dispersed across a region, were building monumental architecture simultaneously, they were connected by social ties that transcended individual settlement groups. These ties may have included kinship networks, marriage alliances, shared ritual obligations, or competitive display (each group trying to build a more impressive temple than its neighbors). The construction of monumental architecture may have been a form of social competition -- groups competing for prestige by building the largest, most elaborate, most richly carved enclosures. Competition of this kind is well documented in ethnographic studies of feasting societies, where rival groups attempt to outdo each other in the lavishness of their feasts and the scale of their constructions.

The seated figure at Karahan Tepe raises questions about the development of artistic representation. At Göbekli Tepe, the human form is abstract -- reduced to the geometric T-shape, with only arms and hands carved in relief. At Karahan Tepe, the human form is naturalistic -- a recognizable body, a face, a posture. Whether this difference reflects a chronological development (naturalism evolving from abstraction, or vice versa), a functional difference (different types of beings requiring different modes of representation), or a cultural difference (different communities with different artistic traditions) is unclear. The variety of representational styles across the Taş Tepeler network suggests a rich and varied artistic tradition, not a single standardized approach.

Other sites in the network add further dimensions. Harbetsuvan Tepesi (har-bet-SOO-vahn teh-peh-SEE) has produced remarkable stone sculptures, including a life-sized human head carved from limestone. Sayburç (say-BURCH), discovered in 2021, features carved wall reliefs depicting scenes with human and animal figures -- including what appears to be a narrative scene showing a human figure interacting with leopards and a bull. If the Sayburç reliefs are narrative -- depicting a sequence of events rather than a static composition -- they would represent some of the oldest known narrative art in the world.

The discovery of the Taş Tepeler network has transformed the scholarly understanding of the transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture in the Fertile Crescent. The transition was not a sudden event but a gradual process, playing out across a landscape of ritual sites, gathering places, and emerging settlements over a period of centuries. Göbekli Tepe and its sister sites were part of this process -- monuments built during the transition, embodying the values and beliefs of communities that were simultaneously maintaining their hunter-gatherer way of life and beginning to experiment with the cultivation of wild plants and the management of wild animals. The temples on the hills were not built by a static, unchanging society; they were built by a society in the process of transforming itself.

One final note on the Taş Tepeler network: the sites are under threat. Modern agriculture, urban expansion, and infrastructure development in the Şanlıurfa region are encroaching on archaeological sites. Some sites have been damaged by road construction or reservoir projects (Nevalı Çori, the site that first alerted Schmidt to the T-shaped pillar tradition, was permanently flooded by the Atatürk Dam in 1992). The Turkish government's Taş Tepeler project is, in part, an effort to identify, document, and protect the remaining sites before they are lost. The race between archaeological discovery and modern development is a recurring theme in world archaeology -- and in southeastern Turkey, where some of humanity's oldest monuments lie beneath active farmland, the urgency is acute.
