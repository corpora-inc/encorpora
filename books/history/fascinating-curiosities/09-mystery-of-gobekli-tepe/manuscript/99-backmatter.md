# What We Still Don't Know

**What was the purpose of Göbekli Tepe?** The site has no residential structures, no granaries, no workshops. It appears to have been purely ceremonial. The enclosures with their carved pillars are designed for gathering, for ritual, for some form of communal activity -- but the specific nature of that activity is unknown. Religious worship, initiation ceremonies, funerary rites, seasonal festivals, diplomatic negotiations between groups -- all have been proposed.

**Who or what do the T-shaped pillars represent?** The pillars are anthropomorphic -- they have arms and hands -- but they have no faces. Are they gods, ancestors, spirits, or abstract concepts? The abstraction of the form is deliberate: the builders chose not to give the pillars individual features, suggesting that the beings represented were generic or categorical rather than specific individuals. Who were these faceless presences?

**Why was the site deliberately buried?** The burial around 8000 BCE was careful and intentional. Ritual termination, replacement by new forms of worship, concealment, preservation -- all are possible explanations. The deliberate burial of a monumental construction that took centuries to build is one of the most puzzling acts in the archaeological record.

**Did the construction of Göbekli Tepe drive the invention of agriculture?** The hypothesis is elegant: the need to feed construction workers and feast participants created the incentive to cultivate wild grains. The proximity to the wild ancestor of domesticated wheat is suggestive. But the evidence is circumstantial, and the relationship between temple-building and plant cultivation may be more complex than a simple cause-and-effect chain.

**What do the animals on the pillars mean?** Totems? Mythological beings? Seasonal markers? A bestiary of the wild world? The animals are clearly significant -- they dominate the carvings, and different enclosures emphasize different species. The symbolic system they encode is lost.

**How large is Göbekli Tepe?** Only an estimated 5-10 percent of the site has been excavated. Geophysical surveys indicate at least twenty enclosures, most unexcavated. The full extent, complexity, and variation of the site are unknown.

**What is the relationship between Göbekli Tepe and its sister sites?** The Taş Tepeler network includes at least twelve sites with T-shaped pillars or comparable monumental architecture. The relationships between these sites -- contemporaneous or sequential, allied or competitive, equivalent or hierarchical -- are still being worked out.

**Was there writing or a recording system?** Some researchers have noted that certain symbols appear repeatedly on the pillars and on small objects found at the site -- H-shapes, circles, crescents. Whether these are decorative motifs, clan marks, or elements of a proto-writing system is debated.

The site is vast, mostly unexcavated, and full of surprises. Each season of excavation reveals something new. Göbekli Tepe has already rewritten the opening chapter of human civilization. The remaining chapters are still underground.


# Key Names and Terms

**Aurochs** (OW-roks) -- The wild ancestor of domestic cattle; a massive bovine species that could weigh up to one ton. Extinct since 1627 CE.

**Bølling-Allerød** (BUHL-ing AL-er-uhd) -- A warm interstadial period (~14,700-12,900 years ago) between the last glacial maximum and the Younger Dryas.

**Çatalhöyük** (chah-TAHL-huh-yook) -- A large Neolithic settlement in central Turkey (~7500-5700 BCE); famous for its densely packed houses, wall paintings, and evidence of excarnation.

**Clare, Lee** -- German archaeologist; current director of excavations at Göbekli Tepe for the German Archaeological Institute.

**Dietrich, Oliver** -- German archaeologist; member of the Göbekli Tepe research team.

**Einkorn** (AYN-korn) -- One of the earliest domesticated wheat species; wild ancestor grows on the Karacadağ mountains near Göbekli Tepe.

**Enclosure** -- The term used for the circular or oval pillar-ringed structures at Göbekli Tepe; designated by letters (A, B, C, D, etc.).

**Excarnation** -- The practice of exposing the dead to scavengers (particularly vultures) to remove flesh before collecting and burying the bones.

**Fertile Crescent** -- The arc of relatively well-watered land in the Middle East where some of the earliest agriculture and urbanization occurred.

**Göbekli Tepe** (guh-BEK-lee TEH-peh) -- "Potbelly Hill" in Turkish; a pre-Neolithic monumental site in southeastern Turkey dating to ~9500-8000 BCE.

**Holocene** (HOL-oh-seen) -- The current geological epoch, beginning ~9700 BCE; the warm, stable climate period in which all of recorded human civilization has occurred.

**Jericho** (JEHR-ih-koh) -- An ancient settlement in the Jordan Valley; one of the oldest continuously inhabited places on Earth, with monumental architecture dating to ~9000 BCE.

**Karacadağ** (kah-RAH-jah-dahg) -- A mountain range in southeastern Turkey; the region where wild einkorn wheat (the ancestor of domesticated wheat) grows naturally.

**Karahan Tepe** (kah-RAH-hahn TEH-peh) -- A sister site to Göbekli Tepe, ~35 km southeast; features carved chambers and naturalistic human sculpture.

**Karul, Necmi** (NEJ-mee kah-ROOL) -- Turkish archaeologist; excavator of Karahan Tepe and director of the Taş Tepeler project.

**Natufian** (nah-TOO-fee-ahn) -- A Late Epipaleolithic culture of the Levant (~12,500-9500 BCE); precursors to the first farming communities.

**Neolithic Revolution** -- The transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture; occurred independently in several world regions beginning ~10,000 years ago.

**Notroff, Jens** -- German archaeologist; member of the Göbekli Tepe research team.

**Peters, Joris** (YOR-iss PAY-ters) -- German archaeozoologist; analyzed animal remains and feasting evidence at Göbekli Tepe.

**Pillar 43** -- Also called "the vulture stone"; a pillar in Enclosure D with a complex carved scene including a vulture, a headless human figure, and a scorpion.

**Pleistocene** (PLY-stoh-seen) -- The geological epoch of the Ice Ages (~2.6 million to ~11,700 years ago).

**Schmidt, Klaus** (KLOWS SHMIT) -- German archaeologist (1953-2014); discoverer and primary excavator of Göbekli Tepe.

**Şanlıurfa** (shahn-luh-OOR-fah) -- A city in southeastern Turkey; the nearest major city to Göbekli Tepe.

**Taş Tepeler** (TAHSH teh-peh-LEHR) -- "Stone Hills"; a Turkish government project to investigate the network of pre-Neolithic monumental sites in the Şanlıurfa region.

**T-shaped pillar** -- The characteristic architectural form of Göbekli Tepe and its sister sites; a limestone pillar with a broad horizontal top (stylized head) and narrow vertical shaft (body).

**Younger Dryas** (DRY-us) -- A sudden return to near-glacial conditions (~12,900-9700 BCE); the last cold period before the current warm epoch.


# Further Reading

**For the general reader:**

- Schmidt, Klaus. "Göbekli Tepe: A Stone Age Sanctuary in South-Eastern Anatolia." *ex oriente*, 2012. -- Schmidt's own accessible account of the discovery and its significance.

- Mann, Charles C. "The Birth of Religion." *National Geographic*, June 2011. -- An excellent popular introduction to the site and its implications.

- Curry, Andrew. "Göbekli Tepe: The World's First Temple?" *Smithsonian Magazine*, November 2008. -- Clear, well-illustrated overview.

**For the archaeology:**

- Dietrich, Oliver, et al. "The Role of Cult and Feasting in the Emergence of Neolithic Communities." *Antiquity* 86 (2012): 674-695. -- Key paper on the feasting hypothesis and the social organization of construction.

- Clare, Lee, et al. "Göbekli Tepe and the Cultural Developments in the 10th and 9th Millennia cal BC in Upper Mesopotamia and Beyond." *Neo-Lithics* 2/19 (2019). -- Update from the current excavation director.

- Peters, Joris, and Klaus Schmidt. "Animals in the Symbolic World of Pre-Pottery Neolithic Göbekli Tepe." *Anthropozoologica* 39 (2004): 179-218. -- The authoritative analysis of the animal carvings and faunal remains.

**For broader context:**

- Bellwood, Peter. *First Farmers: The Origins of Agricultural Societies*. Blackwell, 2005. -- Comprehensive global survey of the transition to agriculture.

- Hodder, Ian. *The Leopard's Tale: Revealing the Mysteries of Çatalhöyük*. Thames & Hudson, 2006. -- Accessible account of the other great Neolithic site in Turkey.
