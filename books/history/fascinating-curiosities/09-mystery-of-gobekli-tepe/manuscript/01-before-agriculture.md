# Part One: Before Agriculture

## Chapter 1 — The Hill That Shouldn't Be There

Southeastern Turkey, near the city of Şanlıurfa (shahn-luh-OOR-fah), is a landscape of rolling limestone hills, dry grasslands, and ancient river valleys. The region sits at the northern edge of the Fertile Crescent -- the arc of relatively well-watered land stretching from the Persian Gulf through Mesopotamia and down the eastern Mediterranean coast, where some of the earliest experiments in agriculture took place. The modern landscape is agricultural: fields of wheat and barley, pistachio orchards, and grazing land for sheep and goats. It is productive, if not lush -- a landscape shaped by millennia of farming.

Eight kilometers northeast of Şanlıurfa, a hill rises from the plain. The locals call it Göbekli Tepe -- "Potbelly Hill," a reference to its rounded shape. The hill is roughly fifteen meters high and three hundred meters in diameter, an unremarkable mound in a landscape of gentle hills. For centuries, local farmers plowed around it, occasionally turning up fragments of carved stone that they pushed aside. The hill was known. It was not considered important.

In 1963, a joint survey by the University of Istanbul and the University of Chicago noted the hill and identified it as a possible archaeological site. Stone tools and fragments of worked limestone were visible on the surface. The surveyors classified it as a medieval cemetery and moved on. The limestone fragments were assumed to be broken gravestones. The site was set aside.

Thirty years passed.

In 1994, Klaus Schmidt (KLOWS SHMIT), a German archaeologist working at the German Archaeological Institute's research station in Heidelberg, was looking for a new excavation site in southeastern Turkey. Schmidt was interested in the transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture -- the Neolithic Revolution, the most fundamental transformation in human history. Southeastern Turkey was one of the regions where that transition had occurred, and Schmidt was searching for sites that might illuminate the process.

Schmidt had studied under Harald Hauptmann (HAH-rahlt HOWPT-mahn), a distinguished German archaeologist who had excavated the important early Neolithic site of Nevalı Çori (neh-vah-LUH choh-REE), located roughly sixty kilometers northeast of Göbekli Tepe. Nevalı Çori had produced T-shaped pillar fragments and carved stone sculptures -- materials that had no parallel at other Neolithic sites. When Nevalı Çori was flooded by the Atatürk Dam reservoir in 1992, Schmidt began searching for related sites in the region. He consulted the 1963 survey records, noted the unvisited mound at Göbekli Tepe, and made the trip.

He visited Göbekli Tepe and immediately recognized that the 1963 survey had been wrong. The stone fragments on the surface were not gravestones. They were pieces of carved T-shaped pillars -- large, carefully shaped blocks of limestone that had been deliberately worked and erected. Schmidt, who had years of experience excavating Neolithic sites in the region, recognized the T-shape as an architectural form, not a funerary one. He began excavating in 1995.

What Schmidt found beneath the surface of the hill was unlike anything previously known in archaeology. Massive carved stone pillars, arranged in circles, each pillar weighing between five and ten tons. The pillars were T-shaped: a broad, flat top (representing, Schmidt believed, a stylized human head) set on a tall, narrow shaft (the body). Some pillars had arms and hands carved in low relief -- arms extending from the sides of the shaft, hands meeting at the front, fingers touching at the belly. The pillars were not structural supports for a roof; they were freestanding, arranged in circles of ten to twelve around two larger central pillars.

The radiocarbon dates were staggering. The earliest layers of construction dated to approximately 9500 BCE -- the tenth millennium before the Common Era. The site was roughly twelve thousand years old.

To put this in context: Stonehenge was built around 3000-2000 BCE. The Egyptian pyramids were built around 2500 BCE. The earliest cities in Mesopotamia -- Ur, Uruk, Eridu -- date to roughly 4000-3000 BCE. The invention of writing occurred around 3200 BCE. The first pottery in the Near East appeared around 7000-6000 BCE. Agriculture in the Fertile Crescent began around 9500-8500 BCE -- the same period as Göbekli Tepe's earliest construction.

Göbekli Tepe was built at the very beginning of the agricultural revolution -- or possibly just before it. The people who quarried, carved, and erected the pillars were, by every available measure, hunter-gatherers. There is no evidence of domesticated plants or animals at the site. The food remains found at Göbekli Tepe are wild: wild wheat, wild barley, wild almonds, and the bones of wild animals -- primarily gazelle, aurochs (the wild ancestor of domestic cattle), wild boar, and wild sheep. The builders of Göbekli Tepe fed themselves by hunting and gathering, the same subsistence strategy that human beings had used for hundreds of thousands of years.

The site sits on a limestone ridge overlooking the Harran (hah-RAHN) Plain -- a broad, flat expanse that stretches south toward the Syrian border. The ridge provides a commanding view: from the hilltop, you can see for dozens of kilometers in every direction. The location was chosen deliberately. Göbekli Tepe is not in a river valley, not near a spring or a natural shelter, and not in a location with obvious subsistence advantages. It is on a high point, visible from afar, a landmark in the landscape. Whatever the builders intended, they intended it to be seen.

The landscape around Göbekli Tepe was, twelve thousand years ago, richer than it is today. The early Holocene climate of southeastern Turkey was wetter and more productive than the modern semi-arid environment. Oak and pistachio woodlands covered the hills. Grasslands supported herds of gazelle and wild cattle. Wild cereals -- wheat, barley, rye -- grew in natural stands dense enough to harvest. The region was not a marginal environment in which people struggled to survive; it was a productive landscape that supported a substantial hunter-gatherer population. The people who built Göbekli Tepe were well fed.

And yet they built a monumental stone complex that required organized labor on a scale previously associated only with agricultural societies. The quarrying of a single pillar -- cutting a multi-ton block of limestone from the bedrock, shaping it into a T-form, and transporting it several hundred meters from the quarry to the construction site -- would have required dozens of workers over a period of days or weeks. Erecting the pillar in its socket, aligning it with the other pillars in the circle, and stabilizing it with fill and rubble would have required still more labor. The construction of an entire enclosure -- a circle of ten to twelve pillars plus two central pillars, all set in a prepared floor with stone benches between them -- represents hundreds of person-days of skilled labor.

Organizing that labor required feeding the workers. Feeding dozens of people for weeks or months at a construction site, without the benefit of stored agricultural surplus, required either an exceptionally productive local environment or the coordination of food supplies from a wide area -- or both. The implication was revolutionary: the desire to build this monumental structure may have created the social and economic conditions that led to the development of agriculture. The temple came first. The farms came after.

The scale of the site became clearer with each season of excavation. Schmidt and his team -- working with the Şanlıurfa Museum and the German Archaeological Institute -- gradually exposed four major enclosures (A, B, C, and D), along with portions of several smaller structures. They found thousands of animal bones, stone tools, and carved stone objects. They found the quarry where the pillars had been cut from the bedrock. They found evidence of deliberate burial -- the careful filling of completed enclosures with soil and rubble. And they found, through geophysical survey, that the exposed enclosures were only a fraction of the total: ground-penetrating radar revealed the outlines of at least sixteen additional enclosures, still buried, waiting beneath the hilltop.

The site was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018 -- a recognition of its extraordinary importance to the understanding of human history. A permanent shelter now protects the excavated enclosures from weathering, and the site has become one of Turkey's most visited archaeological attractions. The modern visitor walks along raised platforms above the enclosures, looking down at the T-shaped pillars and their carved animals across a gap of twelve thousand years -- one of the longest temporal distances between a human creation and its modern audience anywhere in the world.


## Chapter 2 — The Old Model vs. The New Model

For over a century, archaeologists had operated with a relatively clear model of how human civilization developed. The model, refined through decades of research, went roughly as follows:

First came agriculture. Around 10,000 years ago, in several independent centers around the world, human beings began to domesticate plants and animals -- to cultivate wild grains, to herd wild sheep and goats, to settle in permanent villages near their fields and flocks. Agriculture produced food surplus: more food than the farmers themselves could eat. Surplus food freed some members of the community from the daily task of finding food, allowing them to specialize in other activities -- craft production, trade, administration, warfare, religion. Specialization led to social complexity: leaders, priests, warriors, artisans, merchants. Social complexity led to settlements, then towns, then cities. Cities produced monuments -- temples, palaces, fortifications -- built by the surplus labor that agriculture had made possible.

The sequence was: agriculture, then surplus, then settlement, then specialization, then social complexity, then monuments.

The model was deeply embedded in archaeological thinking. V. Gordon Childe (1892-1957), the Australian-British archaeologist who coined the term "Neolithic Revolution," built his entire framework around the primacy of agriculture. In Childe's view, the domestication of plants and animals was the transformative event -- the trigger for all subsequent development. Surplus food allowed population growth. Population growth created settlements. Settlements created specialization. Specialization created hierarchy. Hierarchy created monuments. The chain was logical, sequential, and persuasive.

This model was not wrong, exactly. It described a real process that occurred in many parts of the world, from Mesopotamia to China to Mesoamerica. But it assumed that monuments -- large-scale construction projects requiring organized labor -- could only occur at the end of the sequence, after agriculture had provided the economic foundation for everything else.

Göbekli Tepe broke the model. Here was a monumental construction project -- massive stone pillars, carefully carved and arranged in elaborate circles -- that appeared to precede agriculture. The food remains were wild. The tools were pre-agricultural. The settlement evidence was minimal: Göbekli Tepe appears to have been a ceremonial center, not a village. People came to build and to worship, but they did not live there permanently.

Schmidt proposed a radical alternative. Instead of agriculture leading to temples, perhaps temples led to agriculture. The argument ran as follows: Göbekli Tepe was a place of ritual gathering -- a site where dispersed hunter-gatherer groups came together periodically for ceremonies, feasting, and the construction of the monumental enclosures. These gatherings required food -- large quantities of it, to feed the workers and the participants in the rituals. The need to supply these gatherings with food may have created the incentive to begin cultivating wild grains in the surrounding area, transforming the landscape from a place of wild food collection to a place of deliberate food production.

The evidence for this hypothesis was circumstantial but suggestive. Göbekli Tepe sits roughly fifteen kilometers from the Karacadağ (kah-RAH-jah-dahg) mountains -- the region where genetic analysis has identified the wild ancestor of domesticated einkorn wheat. Einkorn (AYN-korn) is one of the earliest domesticated grains, a staple of the earliest farming communities in the Fertile Crescent. The proximity of the world's oldest known temple complex to the ancestral home of one of the world's first domesticated crops is, at minimum, a striking coincidence. Schmidt believed it was more than coincidence: that the labor demands of building and maintaining Göbekli Tepe created the pressure that drove the local population to begin cultivating the wild wheat that grew on the nearby mountains.

The hypothesis -- "religion first, then agriculture" -- was controversial. Many archaeologists argued that the evidence was insufficient to support such a sweeping revision of the standard model. The absence of agricultural remains at Göbekli Tepe could reflect the site's function (a ceremonial center, not a farming village) rather than the absence of agriculture in the region. The radiocarbon dates, while early, overlapped with the dates for the earliest experiments in plant cultivation elsewhere in the Fertile Crescent. Göbekli Tepe might be contemporary with the beginning of agriculture rather than preceding it.

The debate continues. What is beyond dispute is that Göbekli Tepe demonstrated that hunter-gatherers were capable of monumental construction -- that the organizational capacity, the artistic vision, and the communal commitment required to build a stone temple complex existed before the economic transformations of agriculture. The builders of Göbekli Tepe were not simple foragers eking out a marginal existence. They were members of a complex society with specialists, rituals, and the ability to mobilize large labor forces for projects that served no obvious subsistence purpose. The temple had no granary. The pillars stored no food. The purpose was something else entirely -- something that mattered enough to justify the enormous effort of construction.

The debate has also been enriched by critiques from within the archaeological community. Some scholars have argued that Schmidt's framing -- "first the temple, then the city" -- creates a false binary. The transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture was not an overnight event but a gradual process spanning centuries, with many intermediate stages. Communities may have been cultivating wild plants on a small scale long before what we would call "agriculture" became the dominant subsistence strategy. The builders of Göbekli Tepe may have been practicing an early form of plant management that doesn't show up clearly in the archaeological record -- tending wild stands, clearing competing vegetation, possibly even scattering seeds -- without yet qualifying as full-scale agriculture. In this view, the distinction between "hunter-gatherer" and "farmer" is not a sharp line but a continuum, and Göbekli Tepe's builders may have been somewhere in the middle.

Recent excavations have also complicated the picture. Work by Lee Clare and the current Göbekli Tepe team has identified evidence of more domestic activity at the site than Schmidt initially recognized. Small-scale habitation, food processing areas, and everyday tools have been found alongside the monumental enclosures, suggesting that the site was not exclusively ceremonial but may have housed at least a small permanent or semi-permanent population. The strict division between "temple" and "village" may be less clear than Schmidt proposed. Göbekli Tepe may have been both.

Schmidt himself was careful about the implications. He did not claim to have proven that religion caused agriculture. He claimed to have shown that the old model -- in which agriculture was a necessary precondition for everything complex in human society -- was too simple. Complexity, he argued, could arise from many sources: religious fervor, social competition, artistic ambition, the desire to create something permanent in a world of impermanence. Agriculture was one path to complexity. Göbekli Tepe suggested that there were others.

The site's relationship to Nevalı Çori -- the flooded site that had first alerted Schmidt to the T-shaped pillar tradition -- provides an important context. Nevalı Çori, located on the Euphrates River, had been excavated by Hauptmann from 1983 to 1991. The site produced several T-shaped pillar fragments, a life-sized carved limestone head, and a large carved stone figure of a bird -- a vulture or eagle -- perched on two human heads. These finds had no parallel at any other excavated site and suggested a monumental tradition that Nevalı Çori represented only in fragmentary form. When the site was flooded in 1992, the original context was permanently lost. Schmidt's discovery of Göbekli Tepe provided the larger picture that Nevalı Çori had hinted at: a tradition of monumental stone architecture built by pre-agricultural communities across southeastern Turkey.

The relationship between Göbekli Tepe and the surrounding region's early villages is also significant. Sites like Çayönü (chay-UH-noo), located roughly 150 kilometers to the northeast, represent some of the earliest permanent settlements in the region -- villages with stone architecture, storage facilities, and evidence of early plant and animal management, dating to roughly 8500-7000 BCE. Çayönü's inhabitants were among the first farmers in the world, and their village includes a remarkable building known as the "Skull Building," which contained numerous human skulls arranged on a stone surface -- evidence of a skull cult or ancestor veneration practice. The relationship between the ceremonial centers like Göbekli Tepe and the emerging farming villages like Çayönü is a central question in the archaeology of the region: did the ceremonial tradition evolve into the village tradition, or did both develop simultaneously, serving different functions for the same population?

Klaus Schmidt died in 2014, while swimming near Şanlıurfa during the excavation season. He was sixty-one years old. He had spent nearly two decades excavating the site that he recognized as one of the most important archaeological discoveries of the twentieth century. The excavations continue under the direction of the German Archaeological Institute, led by Lee Clare and a team of specialists. Each season reveals more of the site, and each revelation raises new questions. Schmidt's original insight -- that Göbekli Tepe challenges fundamental assumptions about the origins of civilization -- has only been confirmed and deepened by subsequent work.
