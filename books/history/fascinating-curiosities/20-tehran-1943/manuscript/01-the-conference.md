# Part One: The Conference

## Chapter 1 -- Why Tehran

By the autumn of 1943, the war had turned. The German Sixth Army had surrendered at Stalingrad in February. The Wehrmacht's last major offensive on the Eastern Front had failed at Kursk in July. Allied forces had invaded Sicily in July and mainland Italy in September. Mussolini had been deposed and Italy had surrendered, though German forces still held most of the peninsula. In the Atlantic, the U-boat menace was diminishing as Allied air power and code-breaking shifted the balance. The question facing the Allied leaders was no longer whether they would win, but how -- and what the postwar world would look like.

![Map showing the strategic situation in late 1943](../images/placeholders/08-strategic-situation-1943.png)

Roosevelt and Churchill had met repeatedly since the war began -- at Placentia Bay in 1941, at Casablanca and Quebec in 1943. Stalin had attended none of these meetings. He communicated by telegram, through ambassadors, and through special envoys like Averell Harriman. His persistent refusal to leave the Soviet Union frustrated Roosevelt, who believed that personal diplomacy could build trust between the wartime allies and lay groundwork for postwar cooperation.

Stalin's stated reason for refusing to travel was his role as supreme commander. The Soviet Union was fighting the largest land war in history, and Stalin insisted he could not be far from Moscow and the General Staff. The real reasons were likely more layered. Stalin feared flying. He distrusted situations he could not control. And meeting on foreign territory, surrounded by foreign security services, ran against every instinct of a man who had survived decades of political violence by controlling his environment absolutely.

![Stalin's travel route from Moscow to Tehran](../images/placeholders/07-stalin-travel-constraints.png)

The compromise was Tehran. Iran was under joint Allied occupation -- British forces in the south, Soviet forces in the north, American supply operations running through the Persian Corridor. Tehran sat in the Soviet occupation zone, close enough to Soviet territory that Stalin could travel by train through Baku and across the Iranian border. The city was already secured by Allied military forces. The Soviet embassy compound was large enough to host the conference, and it sat directly adjacent to the British legation, separated by a shared wall.

Roosevelt initially preferred Basra or Cairo. Stalin rejected both. Churchill suggested several alternatives. Stalin held firm: Tehran or nothing. As Keith Eubank documented in *Summit at Teheran* (1985), the location debate consumed weeks of diplomatic correspondence. Roosevelt eventually yielded. The President of the United States would travel 7,000 miles -- by battleship to Oran, by plane to Cairo for a preliminary meeting with Churchill and Chiang Kai-shek, then by plane again over Iraq and Iran to Tehran -- because one man refused to fly farther than Baku.

The choice of location had consequences beyond logistics. Tehran was a city that the Allies controlled militarily but where multiple intelligence services operated. German agents had been active in Iran throughout the war, as described in Volume 19. The city was a crossroads -- Persian, Soviet, British, American, and German intelligence networks overlapped in its streets, bazaars, and diplomatic compounds. Holding the most important summit of the war in this environment created both security challenges and intelligence opportunities that would shape the conference itself.


## Chapter 2 -- The Big Three

Franklin Roosevelt arrived in Tehran on November 27, 1943. He was sixty-one years old, in declining health, and serving an unprecedented third term as President. He had not walked unaided since contracting polio in 1921. He traveled with a small delegation, including Harry Hopkins, his closest advisor, and the Joint Chiefs of Staff. Roosevelt's primary goal at Tehran was to secure Stalin's commitment to a coordinated strategy: the cross-Channel invasion of France, timed with a Soviet offensive on the Eastern Front. His secondary goal was more ambitious -- to begin building the personal relationship with Stalin that Roosevelt believed would be essential for postwar peace.

![Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin at the Tehran Conference](../images/placeholders/02-big-three-portrait.png)

Roosevelt was a pragmatist in foreign policy. He believed that the postwar order would be maintained by the great powers acting in concert -- a system he envisioned through the United Nations, with the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and China serving as the "Four Policemen." This vision required Stalin's cooperation, which meant it required Stalin's trust. Roosevelt calculated that building that trust justified significant concessions, including some that would alarm Churchill.

Winston Churchill arrived with a different agenda. At sixty-nine -- he would celebrate his birthday during the conference on November 30 -- Churchill was the eldest of the three and the one whose power was most visibly waning. Britain had been fighting the longest of any of the Big Three, since September 1939, and the cost was immense. Churchill understood that the war was producing a shift in global power away from the British Empire and toward the United States and the Soviet Union. His strategic priorities reflected this awareness.

Churchill favored a Mediterranean strategy: continued operations in Italy, an invasion of the Balkans, and support for resistance movements in southeastern Europe. This approach would advance Allied positions in regions where Britain had traditional interests and, crucially, would place Western forces in Eastern Europe before the Red Army arrived. Stalin and Roosevelt both understood the strategic implication of Churchill's preference, and both opposed it -- Stalin because he wanted the Western Allies committed to a cross-Channel invasion that would draw German forces away from the Eastern Front, and Roosevelt because he saw the Mediterranean strategy as a diversion from the fastest route to Berlin.

Josef Stalin was sixty-four, at the peak of his wartime authority, and the leader with the strongest bargaining position. The Soviet Union had borne the overwhelming burden of ground combat against Germany. Soviet casualties already numbered in the millions. The Red Army's victories at Stalingrad and Kursk had demonstrated that the Wehrmacht could be beaten, but the war was far from over, and Stalin's most urgent demand was simple and concrete: a firm date for the cross-Channel invasion. The Western Allies had been promising a second front since 1942. Stalin wanted the promise kept.

Stalin also arrived with territorial objectives. Poland's postwar borders, the fate of the Baltic states, the future of Germany, and Soviet influence in Eastern Europe were all on his agenda. He negotiated from a position of military strength and moral leverage -- the Soviet Union was doing the fighting and the dying, and Stalin would remind his allies of this fact repeatedly during the conference.

![Timeline of the Tehran Conference, November 28 to December 1, 1943](../images/placeholders/10-conference-timeline.png)

The dynamics between the three men were complex. Roosevelt sought to position himself as a mediator between Churchill and Stalin, sometimes siding with Stalin against Churchill in ways that startled the British delegation. Churchill fought to preserve British strategic priorities while recognizing that Britain's leverage was declining with every month of American industrial production and Soviet military success. Stalin listened, probed, and pressed his advantages with a combination of bluntness and tactical patience that both Western leaders found formidable.

The conference began on the afternoon of November 28. The first plenary session was held at the Soviet embassy. Roosevelt, who had initially stayed at the American legation several blocks away, accepted Stalin's invitation to move into quarters within the Soviet compound -- a decision whose intelligence implications would become clear only later.
