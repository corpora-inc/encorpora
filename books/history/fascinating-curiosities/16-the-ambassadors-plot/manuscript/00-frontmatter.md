# A Note to the Reader

In the summer of 1918, a young British diplomat in Moscow found himself at the center of a conspiracy to overthrow the Bolshevik government. The plot failed. Whether it was ever real -- or whether the Cheka manufactured it from the beginning -- remains one of the central debates in intelligence history.

Robert Hamilton Bruce Lockhart was thirty years old when he arrived in revolutionary Russia in January 1918. He carried no formal diplomatic credentials. Britain had not recognized the Bolshevik government, and the Bolsheviks had not requested a British ambassador. Lockhart went as an unofficial representative -- a deliberately ambiguous status that gave both sides deniability and gave Lockhart freedom to operate in ways a credentialed diplomat could not.

Within eight months, Lockhart's mission had transformed from cautious diplomacy into active conspiracy. The plan, as it emerged in August 1918, was breathtaking in scope: bribe the Latvian Riflemen who guarded the Kremlin, arrest the Bolshevik leadership, and install a government more amenable to the Allied war effort. Working alongside him were Sidney Reilly -- the "Ace of Spies" covered in Volume 14 -- and Captain George Hill of military intelligence, the subject of Volume 15. Three British operatives, three separate accounts, three versions of what happened that cannot all be true.

The Cheka, Felix Dzerzhinsky's secret police, claimed they controlled the plot from the start -- that the Latvian officers Lockhart and Reilly thought they had recruited were in fact Cheka agents running a provocation. If true, the entire conspiracy was theater, and the British walked into a trap designed to justify a crackdown on foreign interference. The historical evidence supports elements of both versions. The full truth, if it exists as a single coherent narrative, has not yet emerged from the archives.
