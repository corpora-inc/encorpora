# Part Two: The Plot

## Chapter 3 -- The Latvian Riflemen

The plot that bears Lockhart's name turned on a specific military unit: the Latvian Riflemen, known in Russian as the Latviyskiye Strelki. Understanding who they were and why they mattered is essential to understanding what happened in August 1918.

![Latvian Riflemen in formation, 1918](../images/placeholders/04-latvian-riflemen.png)

The Latvian Rifle regiments had been formed during World War I from ethnic Latvians within the Russian Imperial Army. Latvia was then part of the Russian Empire, and when the German army invaded the Baltic provinces in 1915, Latvian volunteers fought with particular intensity -- they were defending their homeland. By 1917, the Latvian units had a reputation as among the most disciplined and battle-hardened formations in the disintegrating Russian army.

When the Bolsheviks seized power in October 1917, the Latvian Riflemen sided with them. This was partly ideological -- many Latvian soldiers had been radicalized by the war -- and partly pragmatic. The Bolsheviks promised peace, and the Latvian units had suffered grievously. More importantly, the Bolsheviks offered something the Provisional Government had not: a path to Latvian autonomy within a new political order.

The Riflemen became the Bolsheviks' most reliable military force. While the old Russian army dissolved and the Red Army was still being organized, the Latvian units provided the disciplined, battle-tested troops the Bolsheviks needed to hold power. They guarded the Kremlin. They formed the core of Dzerzhinsky's Cheka enforcement units. They suppressed counter-revolutionary uprisings. In the chaotic months of early 1918, the Latvian Riflemen were the backbone of Bolshevik military power in Moscow.

This made them an obvious target for anyone planning to overthrow the Bolshevik government. If the Latvian units could be turned, the regime's most effective military force would become its greatest vulnerability. The Kremlin guard itself would arrest the Bolshevik leadership.

The approach to the Latvian commanders began in the summer of 1918. The details are contested. In Lockhart's account, he was contacted by Latvian officers who were disillusioned with the Bolsheviks and willing to switch sides if the Allies intervened militarily. In the Cheka's version, the officers who approached Lockhart were agents provocateurs -- Cheka operatives sent to draw the British diplomat into a conspiracy that could then be exposed.

The key figure was Colonel Eduard Berzin, commander of a Latvian unit guarding the Kremlin. Berzin approached Lockhart -- or was sent to approach him -- in the summer of 1918. He claimed that his men were demoralized, underpaid, and willing to turn against the Bolsheviks for the right price. He asked for money and for guarantees of Latvian independence after the Bolsheviks fell.

Lockhart gave him money. The exact amounts are uncertain -- Lockhart's memoirs are vague, and the archival record is incomplete. Estimates in the secondary literature range from tens of thousands to over a million rubles, though the ruble's value in 1918 was collapsing so rapidly that large sums in nominal terms had diminishing real value.

The critical question -- the one the entire historiography of the Lockhart Plot turns on -- is whether Berzin was genuine or whether he was a Cheka agent from the beginning. The answer determines whether the plot was a real conspiracy that the Cheka discovered and exploited, or a provocation that the Cheka manufactured to entrap the British.


## Chapter 4 -- The Conspiracy

By August 1918, the outlines of the conspiracy had taken shape. The plan, as reconstructed from multiple and contradictory sources, envisioned a coordinated action: the Latvian Riflemen would arrest the Bolshevik leadership during a session of the All-Russian Congress of Soviets, while Allied forces intervened from the north (Archangel) and east (Vladivostok). A new government, presumably drawn from anti-Bolshevik socialists or liberals, would take power and rejoin the war against Germany.

![Diagram of the Lockhart Plot -- key participants and connections](../images/placeholders/05-lockhart-plot-diagram.png)

The scope of the plan was ambitious to the point of fantasy. Even if the Latvian units turned and the Bolshevik leaders were arrested, holding power in a country convulsed by revolution and civil war would have required military resources the Allies did not have in Russia. The intervention forces at Archangel and Vladivostok were small, poorly supplied, and thousands of miles from Moscow. The plan assumed a chain of events -- military coup, popular acquiescence, rapid Allied reinforcement -- that had little basis in Russian realities.

Sidney Reilly was the most aggressive proponent of direct action. Reilly's version of the plot, as reconstructed from fragments, was more radical than Lockhart's -- he envisioned the Bolshevik leaders being paraded through the streets in their underclothes to destroy their authority. Whether Reilly actually proposed this, or whether it grew in the telling, is uncertain. Reilly was a man who improved his stories with each retelling.

George Hill's role was more focused. Hill ran intelligence networks and was responsible for sabotage operations against German interests in Russia. His involvement with the Latvian plot appears to have been peripheral -- he provided intelligence and communication channels rather than operational planning.

Lockhart occupied the ambiguous middle ground. He was the senior British civilian representative, and his involvement gave the plot a quasi-official character. London's knowledge and approval of the conspiracy remains debated. Lockhart's memoirs imply he acted largely on his own initiative. Keith Jeffery's authorized history of MI6 suggests the institutional involvement was deeper than Lockhart acknowledged. Jonathan Schneer argues that the War Cabinet was aware of the broad outlines but maintained deniability.

Two events in July 1918 complicated the conspiracy and accelerated the crisis.

On July 6, Yakov Blumkin, a Left Socialist Revolutionary and Cheka agent, assassinated Count Wilhelm von Mirbach, the German ambassador to Moscow. The assassination was part of a Left SR uprising intended to provoke a renewal of war with Germany and derail the Brest-Litovsk peace. The uprising failed -- the Bolsheviks suppressed it within days -- but it demonstrated the fragility of the regime and the possibility of sudden, violent change.

![The assassination of German Ambassador Mirbach, July 6, 1918](../images/placeholders/08-mirbach-assassination.png)

The Mirbach assassination also terrified the Bolshevik leadership. If the Left SRs could penetrate the Cheka itself -- Blumkin was a Cheka employee -- then the security apparatus had been compromised. Dzerzhinsky launched a purge of the Cheka and intensified surveillance of all foreign representatives.

The second event was the expansion of Allied intervention. British forces landed at Archangel on August 2, 1918. This was the military operation that the Lockhart Plot was supposed to coordinate with. The landing pushed the Bolsheviks from suspicion of Allied intentions to certainty that the Western powers were trying to overthrow them.

Into this atmosphere of paranoia, violence, and genuine foreign military intervention, the Lockhart Plot moved toward its climax.
