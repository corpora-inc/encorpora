# What We Still Don't Know

**What language did Teotihuacan speak?** Nahuatl, Totonac, Otomí, and Mixe-Zoquean have all been proposed. Without a deciphered writing system or clear linguistic evidence, the language of the city's builders remains unknown.

**Did Teotihuacan have a writing system?** Karl Taube and others have identified what appears to be a notational system -- standardized signs for names, places, and dates -- but whether this constitutes a writing system capable of recording speech is debated. The absence of long texts may reflect a cultural choice (collective rule discouraging personal inscriptions), preservation failure (plaster and bark paper do not survive 1,500 years), or a genuinely limited notation system.

**Who ruled?** No royal tombs, no named rulers, no personal portraits. Was Teotihuacan governed by a council, by anonymous kings who chose not to depict themselves, or by rulers whose evidence has been destroyed? The Great Goddess figure may represent a ruling concept, a deity, or an actual ruler depicted in divine form. The question is fundamental and unresolved.

**What triggered the burning of ~550 CE?** Internal revolution, foreign invasion, and ritual destruction have all been proposed. The selectivity of the fire (targeting the ceremonial core while sparing residential areas) suggests an internal uprising, possibly triggered by environmental stress from the volcanic winter of 535-536 CE. The exact sequence of events is unknown.

**What was the Entrada at Tikal?** The arrival of Siyaj K'ahk' at Tikal on January 16, 378 CE, and the coincident death of Tikal's king, is documented in Maya inscriptions. Whether this was a military conquest, a diplomatic takeover, or an internal coup facilitated by Teotihuacan connections is debated. The event reshaped Maya politics for centuries.

**What is in the tunnel's deepest chambers?** Sergio Gómez's excavation of the tunnel beneath the Temple of the Feathered Serpent has recovered over 75,000 objects, but excavation continues. The possibility of a royal burial -- or a ritual deposit that explains the tunnel's purpose -- remains open. The mercury, the pyrite mirrors, and the miniature landscapes suggest a representation of the underworld, but the full meaning of the tunnel's deposits is still being analyzed.

**What was the city's name?** "Teotihuacan" is Nahuatl, applied by the Aztecs seven centuries after the city's fall. The Maya glyph *puh* ("Place of Reeds") may be the original name, or it may be a generic title for a great city. The city's own name for itself -- the word its 125,000 residents used in daily life -- is lost.

These are open questions. The pyramids still hold secrets.


# Key Names and Terms

**Apartment compound** -- The standard residential unit at Teotihuacan: a walled enclosure containing multiple rooms around interior courtyards, housing 20-100 people. Over 2,000 compounds have been identified.

**Avenue of the Dead** -- The central ceremonial axis of Teotihuacan, running roughly north-south for 2.5-5 kilometers. Named by the Aztecs, who mistook its flanking platforms for tombs.

**Carballo, David** -- American archaeologist specializing in Teotihuacan's social organization and urbanization.

**Cerro Gordo** (SEHR-roh GOR-doh) -- The mountain north of Teotihuacan, whose shape is echoed by the Pyramid of the Moon.

**Chicomoztoc** (chee-koh-MOHS-tohk) -- "Seven Caves" in Nahuatl; the mythological origin place of the Nahua peoples, possibly connected to the cave beneath the Pyramid of the Sun.

**Cholula** (choh-LOO-lah) -- A city east of the Valley of Mexico containing the largest pyramid by volume in the world. May have absorbed some of Teotihuacan's population after 550 CE.

**Ciudadela** (syoo-dah-DEH-lah) -- "The Citadel"; the large enclosed plaza at the southern end of the Avenue of the Dead, containing the Temple of the Feathered Serpent.

**Cowgill, George** -- American archaeologist; author of *Ancient Teotihuacan* (Cambridge, 2015), the leading modern synthesis.

**Entrada** -- "The Entrance"; the arrival of the Teotihuacan-affiliated figure Siyaj K'ahk' at Tikal on January 16, 378 CE, coinciding with the death of Tikal's king and the installation of a new dynasty.

**Epiclassic** -- The transitional period (~550-900 CE) following Teotihuacan's decline, characterized by political fragmentation and the rise of new regional powers.

**Gómez, Sergio** -- Mexican archaeologist who discovered and excavated the tunnel beneath the Temple of the Feathered Serpent beginning in 2003.

**Great Goddess** -- A female figure depicted in Teotihuacan murals, wearing an elaborate headdress with water imagery and spider associations. Interpreted variously as a deity, a ruling concept, or a representation of political authority.

**Kaminaljuyu** (kah-mee-nahl-HOO-yoo) -- A highland Guatemalan site rebuilt in Teotihuacan style during the Early Classic period.

**Kukulkan** (koo-kool-KAHN) -- The Maya name for the Feathered Serpent deity, equivalent to the Aztec Quetzalcoatl.

**Millon, René** (mee-YOHN) -- American archaeologist who produced the definitive map of Teotihuacan, completed in 1973.

**Oaxacan barrio** -- A neighborhood in western Teotihuacan inhabited by Zapotec people from Oaxaca, identified by Zapotec-style pottery and tomb construction.

**Pachuca obsidian** -- Distinctive green obsidian from a source near Teotihuacan, whose distribution across Mesoamerica traces the city's economic network.

**Pasztory, Esther** -- Art historian; author of *Teotihuacan: An Experiment in Living* (1997), influential analysis of the city's art and ideology.

**Pyramid of the Moon** -- The pyramid at the northern end of the Avenue of the Dead, built in at least seven construction phases. Echoes the shape of Cerro Gordo behind it.

**Pyramid of the Sun** -- The largest structure at Teotihuacan and the third-largest pyramid in the world by volume. Built over a natural cave system.

**Quetzalcoatl** (keht-sahl-KOH-ahtl) -- The Aztec name for the Feathered Serpent deity. The Teotihuacan name for this figure is unknown.

**Siyaj K'ahk'** (see-YAHJ kah-AHK) -- "Born of Fire"; the Teotihuacan-affiliated figure who arrived at Tikal in 378 CE.

**Sugiyama, Saburo** -- Japanese-Mexican archaeologist; excavator of the Temple of the Feathered Serpent sacrifices and the Pyramid of the Moon burials.

**Talud-tablero** (tah-LOOD tah-BLEH-roh) -- A distinctive architectural style combining a sloping lower panel (talud) with a rectangular framed upper panel (tablero). Teotihuacan's architectural signature.

**Taube, Karl** -- American scholar of Mesoamerican iconography; leading analyst of Teotihuacan's notational system and religious symbolism.

**Temple of the Feathered Serpent** -- The pyramid at the southern end of the Ciudadela, whose facade depicts feathered serpents and a goggle-eyed deity. Contains over 200 sacrificial victims in its foundations and a sealed tunnel beneath.

**Teotihuacan** (teh-oh-tee-WAH-kahn) -- "The place where the gods were created" in Nahuatl. The largest city in the pre-Columbian Americas, located in the Valley of Mexico. Flourished ~100-550 CE.

**Tezontle** (teh-SOHN-tleh) -- A lightweight volcanic rock used as a building material at Teotihuacan.

**Xochicalco** (soh-chee-KAHL-koh) -- A fortified hilltop city that rose to prominence after Teotihuacan's decline, combining Teotihuacan, Maya, and Zapotec artistic elements.


# Further Reading

**For the general reader:**

- Cowgill, George L. *Ancient Teotihuacan: Early Urbanism in Central Mexico*. Cambridge University Press, 2015. -- The best modern synthesis, comprehensive and authoritative.

- Pasztory, Esther. *Teotihuacan: An Experiment in Living*. University of Oklahoma Press, 1997. -- A stimulating interpretation of the city's art, architecture, and political ideology.

- Mann, Charles C. *1491: New Revelations of the Americas Before Columbus*. Vintage, 2006. -- An outstanding survey of pre-Columbian civilizations with excellent sections on Teotihuacan.

**For the archaeology:**

- Sugiyama, Saburo. *Human Sacrifice, Militarism, and Rulership: Materialization of State Ideology at the Feathered Serpent Pyramid, Teotihuacan*. Cambridge University Press, 2005. -- The definitive account of the mass sacrifice discovery.

- Millon, René. *Urbanization at Teotihuacan, Mexico. Vol. 1: The Teotihuacan Map*. University of Texas Press, 1973. -- The foundational spatial study of the city.

- Carballo, David M. *Urbanization and Religion in Ancient Central Mexico*. Oxford University Press, 2016. -- A recent analysis of the relationship between urban form and religious practice.

**For the Mesoamerican context:**

- Marcus, Joyce, and Kent V. Flannery. *Zapotec Civilization*. Thames & Hudson, 1996. -- Essential background for understanding Monte Albán's relationship with Teotihuacan.

- Stuart, David. *"The Arrival of Strangers: Teotihuacan and Tollan in Classic Maya History."* In *Mesoamerica's Classic Heritage*, edited by David Carrasco et al. University Press of Colorado, 2000. -- The leading analysis of the Entrada and its consequences.
