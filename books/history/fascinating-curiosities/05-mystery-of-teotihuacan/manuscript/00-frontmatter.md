# A Note to the Reader

Fifty kilometers northeast of present-day Mexico City, in a broad valley at an altitude of 2,300 meters, lie the ruins of one of the largest cities in the ancient world. At its peak, around 450 CE, it held between 125,000 and 200,000 people -- larger than any contemporary city in Europe, comparable in size to Alexandria or Constantinople, rivaling the great cities of Han Dynasty China. Its pyramids are among the largest ever built. Its grid of streets and apartment compounds covered more than twenty square kilometers. Its influence reached from the deserts of northern Mexico to the tropical forests of the Maya lowlands, over a thousand kilometers to the south.

We do not know what this city was called. We do not know what language its builders spoke. We do not know the name of a single one of its rulers. We do not possess a confirmed writing system for this civilization -- or if one existed, we cannot yet read it.

The city is called Teotihuacan (teh-oh-tee-WAH-kahn). The name is Nahuatl (NAH-wahtl), the language of the Aztecs, who named the ruins roughly seven hundred years after the city had burned and been abandoned. The name means "the place where the gods were created." The Aztecs believed that the sun and the moon had been born here, at the beginning of the current age of the world. They walked among the ruins and called the pyramids the homes of gods. They were wrong about what the pyramids were -- they were built by humans, for purposes we can partly reconstruct but not fully understand -- but they were right about the scale. This was a city fit for gods.

This is the story of the anonymous metropolis -- the city that was, for centuries, one of the most powerful places on Earth, and that remains, despite a century of archaeology, one of the most mysterious.
