# Part Two: What Lay Beneath

## Chapter 4 — The Sacrifices

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, archaeologists led by Saburo Sugiyama and Rubén Cabrera Castro conducted systematic excavations of the Temple of the Feathered Serpent. What they found was one of the most disturbing discoveries in Mesoamerican archaeology.

Buried within and beneath the pyramid, in carefully arranged pits, were the remains of over two hundred sacrificial victims. The bodies were arranged in groups, oriented to cardinal directions, and deposited in what appears to have been a single massive dedicatory event -- a mass sacrifice carried out at the time of the pyramid's construction, probably in the late second century CE.

The arrangement was precise. Groups of individuals were placed at the four corners and along the four sides of the pyramid, in a pattern that mirrored the four cardinal directions. The north side contained one group, the south side another, and so on, with the largest deposits at the center and the corners. The spatial organization was cosmological: the sacrifices were not random; they were a map of the universe, rendered in human bodies. The four-directional arrangement echoes the cross-in-circle motif that pervades Mesoamerican symbolism -- the same cosmological framework that would later structure the Mississippian world at Cahokia, thousands of kilometers to the north. The victims were the foundation of the building in the most literal sense -- the pyramid was built on top of them.

The ages and sexes of the victims have been analyzed through skeletal examination. Most were young adults, predominantly male, though both sexes are represented. The consistency of age and sex in specific burial groups suggests that the victims were selected according to criteria -- youth, sex, physical condition -- that we can identify but not fully explain. The selection was not random. Specific kinds of people were chosen for specific positions within the cosmological plan.

![Cross-section showing the burial deposits beneath the Temple of the Feathered Serpent](../images/placeholders/05-sacrifice-cross-section.png)

The details are specific and haunting. Many of the victims had their hands bound behind their backs, indicating that they were restrained at the time of death. Some wore necklaces made from human jawbones -- real mandibles, strung on cord, worn as ornaments. Others wore imitation jawbones carved from shell, replicas of the real ones. The combination of real and artificial human jawbones as personal adornment is unlike anything found at other Mesoamerican sites. Its meaning is debated. Were the jawbone necklaces trophies of warfare, displaying the remains of defeated enemies? Were they ritual objects, worn by the victims themselves as part of the sacrificial regalia? The jawbones make the sacrifice visceral in a way that bare bones do not -- these people wore the remains of other people around their necks.

Among the victims, one group stands out: rows of individuals in full military regalia. These were not naked prisoners or stripped captives. They wore elaborate shell ornaments, obsidian projectile points arranged in patterns across their bodies (probably originally attached to clothing or belts), and slate discs that may have been parts of mirrors or military insignia. Their teeth showed the dental modification practices common among high-status Mesoamerican individuals -- filed, inlaid, or shaped according to aesthetic conventions that marked social rank.

Sugiyama has argued that the spatial distribution of victims within the pyramid was not random but followed a precise numerical pattern. The groups of victims are arranged in multiples of specific numbers -- particularly 4, 9, 13, and 20, all of which are significant in the Mesoamerican calendrical system. The 260-day ritual calendar, used across Mesoamerica, is composed of cycles of 13 and 20. The placement of sacrificial victims in numerical groups that correspond to calendrical cycles suggests that the sacrifice was an act of time-keeping as much as an act of devotion -- the pyramid was a calendar in human flesh, embedding the structure of time into the structure of space.

The military regalia raises a profound question: were these enemy soldiers, captured and sacrificed? Or were they Teotihuacan's own warriors, volunteering or being selected for sacrifice at the dedication of the city's most important religious building? If they were enemies, the sacrifice was an act of domination -- the defeated literally becoming the foundation of the victor's temple. If they were Teotihuacan's own soldiers, the sacrifice was something different and more complex -- an act of dedication in which the city offered its best to the gods. The question is unresolved. Both interpretations have scholarly support.

The number of victims -- over two hundred -- and the precision of their arrangement indicate an event that required extensive planning, coordination, and authority. Two hundred people do not die by accident or in a moment of spontaneous violence. They are gathered, prepared, restrained, killed, and arranged according to a plan. The plan at the Feathered Serpent Pyramid involved cosmological symbolism (the cardinal directions), military display (the warriors in regalia), and a theology of foundation (the building literally resting on human bodies). The sacrifice was simultaneously an act of architecture, an act of war, and an act of worship.

The discovery forced scholars to revise their understanding of Teotihuacan. Before the Feathered Serpent excavations, the dominant image of Teotihuacan was of a relatively peaceful, trade-oriented city -- a cosmopolitan metropolis that spread its influence through commerce and cultural prestige rather than military force. Esther Pasztory's influential work had emphasized the city's "corporate" character -- its emphasis on collective identity over individual power, its relatively egalitarian apartment compounds, its art that depicted gods and processions rather than conquests and captives. The mass sacrifice revealed a different dimension: a city capable of organized violence on a scale that matched anything in Mesoamerica. Teotihuacan was not merely a market and a temple. It was also a power that could kill hundreds of people in a single organized event and embed their bodies in the foundations of its most sacred building.

The jawbone necklaces deserve a moment's further reflection. They are so unusual -- so specific, so disturbing -- that they resist easy categorization. In many Mesoamerican cultures, body parts of the dead carried specific spiritual power. Among the later Aztecs, the bones of the dead were sacred objects, repositories of the vital force that animated living beings. A necklace of jawbones may have been a device for carrying the spiritual power of the dead -- a portable concentration of supernatural energy, worn by individuals who were themselves about to become offerings. The real jawbones and the shell imitations worn side by side suggest that the symbolic function mattered as much as the physical object: the shell replicas performed the same ritual work as the real ones. The distinction between real and fake, so important in modern thinking, may have been irrelevant in Teotihuacan's ritual logic.


## Chapter 5 — The Tunnel

In 2003, a heavy rainstorm at Teotihuacan caused a section of earth near the base of the Temple of the Feathered Serpent to collapse, revealing a previously unknown opening. The archaeologist Sergio Gómez, who was working at the site, investigated and found something extraordinary: a tunnel, sealed for nearly two thousand years, extending roughly one hundred meters beneath the temple.

The tunnel had been deliberately sealed in antiquity. Its entrance was blocked with massive stones and rubble, packed tight, and hidden. Whatever was inside had been placed there and then locked away, intentionally, by the people of Teotihuacan. When Gómez's team began the painstaking process of clearing the tunnel -- a project that would take over a decade -- they entered a space that no human being had seen since roughly 250 CE.

The tunnel descended about fourteen meters below the surface, then ran horizontally for approximately one hundred meters, ending in a series of chambers beneath the center of the pyramid. The descent was steep -- a narrow ramp plunging into the earth at an angle that would have required torches or other light sources. At the bottom, the tunnel leveled out and ran straight, its ceiling low enough to require crouching in places, its walls rough-cut from the volcanic earth of the valley floor.

The excavation was one of the most painstaking in the history of Mesoamerican archaeology. Gómez's team used remote-sensing equipment -- ground-penetrating radar and a small robot called Tlaloc II-TC -- to explore the tunnel before entering it. The robot, equipped with cameras and infrared sensors, crawled through the sealed passage and transmitted images of a space undisturbed for nearly two millennia. When human excavators finally entered, they worked in shifts, centimeter by centimeter, documenting every object in place before removing it. The process took over a decade.

The floor was covered with a thick layer of offerings -- thousands of objects deposited over what may have been decades or centuries before the tunnel was sealed.

![The tunnel beneath the Temple of the Feathered Serpent during excavation](../images/placeholders/06-tunnel-excavation.png)

The offerings were extraordinary. Gómez's team recovered over 75,000 objects from the tunnel. Among them: finely worked obsidian knives; greenstone figurines; pyrite mirrors (flat discs of iron pyrite, polished to a reflective surface, used in divination and ritual); pottery in forms unique to the tunnel; shells from the Gulf of Mexico and the Pacific coast; rubber balls (probably for the Mesoamerican ball game); animal bones (including those of jaguars, which do not naturally inhabit the Valley of Mexico and must have been brought from the tropical lowlands); and seeds, including remnants of plants that do not grow at Teotihuacan's altitude.

The most startling discovery was mercury. The tunnel's walls and floor were dusted with traces of liquid mercury -- a substance that does not occur naturally in the Valley of Mexico and that, in the ancient world, was rare, valuable, and symbolically potent. Mercury's silvery, reflective quality would have made it a powerful ritual material -- a liquid mirror, a substance that caught and reflected light in the darkness of a subterranean chamber. Mercury has been found at a small number of other Mesoamerican sites, always in ritual contexts, suggesting that it carried deep cosmological significance. In the tunnel, mercury may have been used to create the impression of a subterranean lake or river -- a body of shimmering, reflective liquid in the darkness, suggesting the watery underworld that Mesoamerican cosmology placed beneath the surface of the earth.

The chambers at the tunnel's end contained miniature landscapes -- small-scale arrangements of objects that appeared to represent features of the natural and supernatural worlds. Tiny mountains of pyrite, pools of mercury, arrangements of greenstone objects suggesting trees or vegetation. The interpretation, proposed by Gómez and supported by several other scholars, is that the tunnel was a physical representation of the underworld -- a journey from the surface of the earth downward into the realm of the dead, recreated in the tunnel's architecture and its offerings. Walking through the tunnel would have been a ritual journey, a descent into the cosmos below, with each chamber and each deposit marking a stage of the passage.

The pyrite mirrors deserve particular attention. Dozens were found in the tunnel, some intact, some deliberately broken. Pyrite mirrors are found across Mesoamerica, always in elite or ritual contexts. They were used for divination -- staring into the reflective surface to receive visions or communicate with the supernatural world. The mirrors in the tunnel, placed in the deepest and most sacred space of the city, may have been instruments for communicating with the underworld directly -- mirrors pointed downward into the earth rather than upward at the sky.

The tunnel has yielded no royal tombs -- a significant and frustrating absence. Many scholars expected that the tunnel would contain the burial of one of Teotihuacan's rulers, finally answering the question of who governed the city. As of the most recent excavation reports, no royal burial has been found. The tunnel appears to have been a ritual space rather than a mortuary one -- a place where offerings were made, visions were sought, and the underworld was accessed, but where the dead were not permanently interred.

The greenstone figurines found in the tunnel are worth particular attention. Greenstone -- jade and related minerals -- was the most precious material in Mesoamerican culture, more valued than gold (which was rarely used in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica). The figurines are small, stylized human forms, smooth and polished, with the characteristic Teotihuacan face: wide-set eyes, slightly parted lips, and a serene, almost mask-like expression. They were deposited in the tunnel with care, positioned upright in some cases, as if standing in the darkness. Whether they represent deities, ancestors, or offerings to the beings of the underworld is unknown, but their material -- the most precious substance available -- signals the ritual importance of the space.

The rubber balls found in the tunnel are a small but significant detail. The Mesoamerican ball game -- played across the region for over three thousand years, from the Olmec to the Aztecs -- was one of the most important ritual activities in Mesoamerican civilization. Ball courts have been found at hundreds of sites. The presence of rubber balls in the tunnel connects this subterranean space to the cosmological drama of the ball game, which in Mesoamerican mythology was played by the Hero Twins against the lords of the underworld. The tunnel may have been understood as a place where the boundary between the human world and the underworld was thin enough for the ball game's cosmic contest to be reenacted in ritual.

The absence of royal tombs in the tunnel echoes the absence of royal tombs everywhere at Teotihuacan. Unlike the Maya, who buried their kings beneath pyramids in elaborate tombs filled with jade, pottery, and painted texts, Teotihuacan has produced no confirmed burial of a ruler. Either Teotihuacan's leaders were buried in a manner that has not yet been discovered, or they were not buried at all (cremation is one possibility), or the concept of a single supreme ruler -- a king, a pharaoh, a paramount chief -- does not apply to Teotihuacan's political system. The tunnel, for all its wonders, deepened the mystery rather than solving it.


## Chapter 6 — Who Ruled?

The absence of identifiable rulers at Teotihuacan is one of the most debated questions in Mesoamerican archaeology. At every other major Mesoamerican city -- Palenque, Tikal, Copán, Monte Albán, Tenochtitlan -- the rulers left their marks. They carved their names on stelae. They depicted themselves in sculpture and painting. They built tombs filled with treasures and inscribed with their genealogies. The material record screams their names.

Teotihuacan's material record does nothing of the kind. The city's art is rich, sophisticated, and extensive -- thousands of murals, hundreds of sculptures, countless pieces of decorated pottery -- and virtually none of it depicts an identifiable individual human being. The murals show gods, priests, processions of identical figures in ritual regalia, animals, plants, and abstract symbols. The sculptures show standardized deity images. The pottery bears symbolic motifs. The art is communal, institutional, impersonal. It celebrates the collective -- the rituals, the gods, the cosmic order -- rather than the individual.

This is so different from the Maya pattern that it demands explanation. The Classic Maya were obsessed with individual identity. Their rulers proclaimed their names, their parents' names, their victories, their accession dates, and their divine connections in elaborate inscriptions carved on stone monuments. A Maya city without royal portraits and inscriptions would be unthinkable. Teotihuacan is a city entirely without them.

Three main hypotheses compete to explain this absence.

The first is that Teotihuacan was governed by a collective -- a council of elders, a ruling committee, an oligarchy of powerful lineages that shared power and deliberately suppressed individual glorification. The impersonal art, in this view, reflects a political philosophy that valued the institution over the individual. Power was shared, rotated, or held collectively, and no single person was permitted to dominate the public image of the city. Some scholars have drawn parallels with the Mesoamerican concept of collective rule described in later sources -- the Aztec *cihuacoatl* and *tlatoani* dual leadership, or the council governance documented among some Puebloan peoples of the American Southwest. The apartment compounds themselves, with their emphasis on communal living and shared space, may reflect this collective ethos at the household level.

The second hypothesis is that Teotihuacan had individual rulers, but that those rulers deliberately chose an artistic program that emphasized the divine and the cosmic rather than the personal. In this view, the rulers were real, powerful, and probably autocratic, but they presented themselves through religious imagery rather than personal portraits. The "Great Goddess" -- a female figure who appears repeatedly in Teotihuacan's murals, adorned with an elaborate headdress, streams of water, and spider imagery -- may be one such representation: a ruler depicted as a deity, or a deity who represented the ruling principle. The goggle-eyed storm deity (sometimes called "Tlaloc" after the Aztec rain god, though the Teotihuacan name is unknown) may be another.

![Mural of the Great Goddess figure with elaborate headdress and water imagery](../images/placeholders/07-great-goddess-mural.png)

The third hypothesis is simple: the evidence has not survived. Teotihuacan's buildings were plastered, and murals were painted on plaster. The city was burned. Plaster is fragile. Fifteen hundred years of weathering, looting, and reuse have destroyed most of the city's murals. The royal portraits may once have existed -- in murals now gone, on bark-paper codices now dust, in textile banners now decomposed. The absence of evidence, in this view, is an accident of preservation rather than a reflection of political structure.

The Great Goddess figure warrants closer attention. She appears in murals at Tepantitla, one of the best-preserved apartment compounds, and in other locations across the city. She is depicted frontally, with an enormous headdress from which streams of water flow, carrying seeds, jade, and other symbols of abundance. Spiders and spider webs appear in her imagery. Her mouth is sometimes covered by a nose ornament that obscures her face, reinforcing the anonymity that characterizes Teotihuacan's art.

The very term "Great Goddess" is a scholarly construction. Pasztory coined it in the 1970s, and it has been debated ever since. Some scholars question whether the murals depict a single figure at all -- the images grouped under the "Great Goddess" label vary in their details, and they may represent multiple deities or concepts rather than a single entity. Others have argued that the figure is male, not female, or that gender as we understand it does not apply. The debate illustrates a broader challenge at Teotihuacan: without texts, without names, and without the interpretive framework that writing provides, scholars must construct their understanding of the city's religion from visual evidence alone. A mural can be read in multiple ways, and without a key to the code, disagreement is inevitable.

Esther Pasztory, the art historian who did the most to analyze the Great Goddess, has argued that she represents a distinctly Teotihuacano concept of authority -- power as nourishment, as the provision of water, fertility, and abundance. If Pasztory is right, then Teotihuacan's political ideology was fundamentally different from the warrior-king model that dominated the Maya world and later Aztec society. The ruler was not a conqueror or a warrior but a provider -- a figure whose authority rested on the ability to ensure the flow of rain, the growth of crops, and the continuity of the cosmic order.

David Carballo, in his work on Teotihuacan's social organization, has argued that the apartment compounds themselves provide evidence for a more distributed form of power than the Maya model. The compounds are remarkably similar in size and quality -- there are wealthier and poorer compounds, but the difference between them is not as extreme as the difference between a Maya royal palace and a commoner's hut. The absence of a clearly dominant residential compound -- a palace that towers over its neighbors the way Monks Mound towers over Cahokia -- suggests a political system in which power was distributed among multiple elite families rather than concentrated in a single ruler. The apartment compounds may have been the seats of lineages, clans, or corporate groups, each with its own sphere of authority, together forming a governing coalition that ran the city collectively.

This interpretation is consistent with a broader pattern in Mesoamerican political organization. The Aztecs, who came much later, had a dual leadership system -- the *tlatoani* (ruler) and the *cihuacoatl* (co-ruler or prime minister). The Zapotecs of Monte Albán may have practiced a form of shared rulership. The idea of collective governance was not foreign to Mesoamerican political thought. If Teotihuacan was governed by a council, it was not uniquely strange -- it was one expression of a Mesoamerican tradition that recognized alternatives to single-ruler monarchy.

The question remains open. Each excavation season at Teotihuacan carries the possibility of discovering a royal tomb, a personal inscription, or an artifact that resolves the question. Until then, the city keeps its rulers as anonymous as its name -- a metropolis of 125,000 people whose leaders are invisible, their power expressed through architecture and ritual rather than name and portrait. The gods have faces at Teotihuacan. The rulers do not.
