# Part Four: The Web

## Chapter 9 — The Teotihuacan Network

Teotihuacan's influence reached far beyond the Valley of Mexico. During its centuries of dominance (roughly 100-550 CE), Teotihuacan-style architecture, art, and ritual objects appeared at sites across Mesoamerica -- from the deserts of northern Mexico to the tropical forests of Guatemala and Honduras, from the Pacific coast to the Gulf. The city was the center of a web of connections that stretched over a thousand kilometers in every direction.

The nature of these connections is one of the most debated questions in Mesoamerican archaeology. Was Teotihuacan an empire, controlling distant territories through military force? A trade network, linking distant markets through commerce? A religious franchise, spreading a set of beliefs and symbols that other communities adopted voluntarily? Or a cultural prestige system, in which local leaders imitated Teotihuacan's style to enhance their own authority? All four interpretations have been proposed, and the answer probably varies by region and by period. Teotihuacan's relationship with Monte Albán was different from its relationship with Tikal, and both were different from its relationship with the communities of western Mexico.

The connection to Monte Albán, discussed in Volume 1, is the most clearly documented diplomatic relationship. On the South Platform at Monte Albán, carved stone slabs depict figures in Teotihuacan-style dress -- goggle-eyed headdresses, rectangular shields, the distinctive butterfly-shaped nose ornaments that characterize Teotihuacan military regalia. These figures are widely interpreted as ambassadors or diplomats from Teotihuacan, depicted at Monte Albán as part of a diplomatic exchange between the two cities. The relationship appears to have been one of mutual recognition -- two major powers acknowledging each other across a distance of three hundred kilometers.

The connection to the Maya world is more dramatic and more controversial.

On January 16, 378 CE -- one of the few precise dates we have in Teotihuacan's history, preserved in Maya inscriptions at Tikal -- a figure called Siyaj K'ahk' (see-YAHJ kah-AHK, "Born of Fire") arrived at the great Maya city of Tikal in what is now Guatemala. On the same day, Tikal's ruling king, Chak Tok Ich'aak I, died -- almost certainly killed. Within a short time, a new dynasty was installed at Tikal, with clear connections to Teotihuacan. The new king, Yax Nuun Ahiin I (YAHSH noon ah-EEN), was apparently the son of a Teotihuacan lord, and his regnal imagery combined Maya and Teotihuacan elements.

This event, known as the *Entrada* (the "Entrance"), is documented in Maya inscriptions at Tikal, Uaxactún (wah-shahk-TOON), and other sites. It describes what appears to be a military intervention or regime change directed from Teotihuacan -- a figure associated with the Valley of Mexico arriving in the Maya lowlands and replacing the existing royal dynasty.

The Entrada is one of the most discussed events in Mesoamerican archaeology. Was it a military invasion -- a Teotihuacan army conquering a Maya city over a thousand kilometers away? A diplomatic takeover -- a political marriage or alliance that gave a Teotihuacan-affiliated figure the throne? An internal coup -- a faction within Tikal using Teotihuacan connections to seize power? The Maya inscriptions describe the event in terms of Teotihuacan's arrival and the old king's death, but the inscriptions are written from the perspective of the new dynasty, which had every reason to emphasize its Teotihuacan connections.

The distance involved is staggering. Tikal is over one thousand kilometers from Teotihuacan, across some of the most difficult terrain in Mesoamerica -- the highlands of central Mexico, the lowland jungles of Tabasco and the Petén, with no roads, no wheeled vehicles, and no draft animals. If Siyaj K'ahk' led a military expedition from Teotihuacan to Tikal, the logistics would have been formidable: weeks or months of travel through hostile territory, carrying weapons and supplies on human backs, crossing rivers and mountain passes. Some scholars have proposed that the Entrada was not a direct military expedition from the Valley of Mexico but rather the action of Teotihuacan-affiliated groups already present in the Maya lowlands -- merchants, diplomats, or settlers who had established themselves in the region over preceding decades. The Maya inscriptions do not specify whether Siyaj K'ahk' marched from Teotihuacan or from a closer staging point.

The consequences of the Entrada were far-reaching. After 378 CE, Tikal's art and architecture incorporated Teotihuacan elements: talud-tablero (tah-LOOD tah-BLEH-roh) architecture (a distinctive sloping-panel-and-frame style that is Teotihuacan's architectural signature), Teotihuacan-style pottery, and iconographic motifs drawn from the Valley of Mexico tradition. The new dynasty ruled Tikal for generations, and the Teotihuacan connection became part of Tikal's identity. Other Maya cities responded to Tikal's enhanced prestige by seeking their own Teotihuacan connections or by allying against the newly empowered Tikal. The ripple effects of the Entrada reshaped the political landscape of the Maya lowlands for centuries.

Further south, the highland Guatemalan site of Kaminaljuyu (kah-mee-nahl-HOO-yoo) was rebuilt in what appears to be pure Teotihuacan style during the Early Classic period. Tombs at Kaminaljuyu contained individuals with Teotihuacan-style grave goods -- talud-tablero architecture, cylinder tripod pottery (a distinctive Teotihuacan ceramic form), and green obsidian from the Pachuca source near Teotihuacan. Whether Kaminaljuyu was a Teotihuacan colony, a trading post, or a local community that adopted Teotihuacan culture wholesale is debated. The degree of Teotihuacan influence is striking -- this is not casual cultural exchange but a thorough transformation of the site's material culture.

Obsidian provides one of the clearest tracers of Teotihuacan's economic network. Obsidian -- volcanic glass, the sharpest material available to ancient Mesoamericans for tools and weapons -- was quarried from several sources in central Mexico. The Pachuca source, located northeast of the Valley of Mexico, produced a distinctive green obsidian that is easily identifiable in the archaeological record. Green Pachuca obsidian appears at sites across Mesoamerica during the Classic period, and its distribution tracks the reach of Teotihuacan's economic connections. The city appears to have controlled the Pachuca obsidian source and distributed the material through its trade network, using green obsidian as both a commodity and a symbol of Teotihuacan affiliation.

On the Gulf Coast, the site of Matacapan (mah-tah-kah-PAHN) in southern Veracruz shows a concentration of Teotihuacan-style materials -- talud-tablero architecture, green Pachuca obsidian, Teotihuacan ceramic forms -- that suggests a direct Teotihuacan presence, possibly a colony or trading outpost, deep in the tropical lowlands. Matacapan's location, near the Tuxtla Mountains (the same region that supplied basalt for the Olmec colossal heads discussed in Volume 3), gave it access to both Gulf Coast marine resources and highland volcanic materials. If Teotihuacan maintained an outpost here, it was plugged into the economic networks of both the highlands and the lowlands -- a strategic position that would have given the city access to goods from across the Mesoamerican world.

The talud-tablero architectural style is another tracer. This distinctive form -- a sloping lower panel (talud) surmounted by a rectangular framed panel (tablero) -- is Teotihuacan's most recognizable architectural feature. Talud-tablero structures appear at sites across Mesoamerica during the Classic period, from Kaminaljuyu in Guatemala to Matacapan on the Gulf Coast to sites in western Mexico. The style's distribution does not necessarily indicate political control -- it may reflect cultural imitation, trade connections, or the presence of Teotihuacan-affiliated communities. But it marks the reach of Teotihuacan's cultural influence as clearly as a brand logo marks the reach of a modern corporation.

The city's craft production was itself a form of influence. Teotihuacan was a manufacturing center, producing goods in quantities that suggest export as well as local consumption. Obsidian workshops in the city produced prismatic blades -- long, thin, razor-sharp blades struck from prepared cores in a technique that required extraordinary skill. A master knapper could produce a dozen blades from a single core in minutes, each one sharper than a modern surgical scalpel. These blades were traded across Mesoamerica and have been found at sites from the American Southwest to Honduras. Ceramic workshops produced the distinctive "thin orange" pottery -- a fine, unslipped ware whose clay source has been traced to southern Puebla but whose production and distribution were linked to the Teotihuacan network. Theater-type censers -- elaborate ritual incense burners assembled from multiple ceramic components -- were another Teotihuacan specialty, found at sites across the Mesoamerican world.

The total picture is of a city that projected power, influence, and culture across one of the largest geographic ranges of any pre-Columbian polity in the Americas. Whether this projection was achieved through military conquest, diplomatic alliance, trade dominance, religious proselytism, or some combination of all four, the result was the same: for over four centuries, to be connected to Teotihuacan was to be connected to the most prestigious, most powerful, and most important place in Mesoamerica.

The comparison with modern global systems is tempting but imprecise. Teotihuacan was not an empire in the Roman sense -- it did not administer distant provinces through governors and garrisons (as far as we know). It was not a franchise in the modern corporate sense -- there was no formal licensing of the Teotihuacan "brand." It may have been something more like the cultural and economic gravitational field of a great city: a center so powerful, so wealthy, and so prestigious that communities across a vast region oriented themselves toward it, adopted its symbols, sought its goods, and imitated its forms. The influence was real, but the mechanism was complex -- a mixture of trade, diplomacy, military pressure, religious authority, and sheer cultural prestige that defies easy categorization.

When Teotihuacan burned, this gravitational field collapsed. Sites that had oriented themselves toward the great city were suddenly without a center. The result was the political fragmentation of the Epiclassic period -- a time when multiple smaller powers competed to fill the vacuum that Teotihuacan's fall had created. The fact that the vacuum existed -- that Teotihuacan's collapse destabilized political systems across Mesoamerica -- is the strongest evidence for the extent of its influence. A city whose fall disrupts an entire region was a city that held that region together.


## Chapter 10 — What Was Happening Elsewhere

Teotihuacan's peak -- roughly 200 to 550 CE -- spans one of the most transformative periods in world history. Placing the city in its global context reveals both its extraordinary achievement and its total isolation from the civilizations that would later claim to be the only ones that mattered.

When Teotihuacan was growing into a major city in the first and second centuries CE, the Roman Empire was at its height. The population of Rome itself was over one million -- vastly larger than Teotihuacan. The Roman road network, the Roman legal system, and the Roman military machine connected an empire that stretched from Britain to Mesopotamia. In China, the Han Dynasty governed a civilization of comparable scale, with advances in paper, porcelain, and the civil service system that would shape Chinese society for millennia. In India, the Kushan Empire linked Central Asia to the Indian subcontinent, facilitating the spread of Buddhism along the Silk Road.

Teotihuacan knew nothing of any of this. The oceans that separated the Americas from Eurasia and Africa were, for all practical purposes, absolute barriers. The civilizations of the Old World and the New World developed in complete independence, arriving at parallel solutions to parallel problems -- monumental architecture, urban planning, astronomical observation, writing systems, long-distance trade -- without any knowledge of each other's existence. The independence of these achievements is, in itself, one of the most profound facts about human civilization. When two civilizations on opposite sides of the planet both build pyramids, both organize cities on grids, and both develop systems of astronomical observation, the conclusion is not that one copied the other. The conclusion is that these are things human beings do when they build complex societies. The parallel development is evidence of shared human capacity, not hidden contact.

When Teotihuacan reached its maximum size around 450 CE, the city's population of 125,000-200,000 made it one of the half-dozen largest cities on Earth. The Roman Empire had split into eastern and western halves, and the western half was in its final decades. The last Roman emperor in the West, Romulus Augustulus, would be deposed in 476 CE. The eastern half -- the Byzantine Empire -- was thriving, its capital Constantinople growing into one of the world's great cities. In India, the Gupta Empire (roughly 320-550 CE) presided over a golden age of art, literature, mathematics, and science -- the period that produced the concept of zero, the decimal system, and some of the masterpieces of Sanskrit literature. In China, the collapse of the Han Dynasty (220 CE) had led to a period of fragmentation known as the Six Dynasties, a time of political turmoil but also of remarkable cultural creativity, including the flowering of Buddhist art and the development of landscape painting. In India, the Gupta golden age (roughly 320-550 CE) produced some of the most celebrated achievements of Indian civilization: the concept of zero and the decimal number system in mathematics, the plays of Kalidasa in literature, the cave paintings of Ajanta and the stone carvings of Sarnath in art. The Gupta period is sometimes called the "Classical Age of India" -- and it coincided almost exactly with Teotihuacan's peak. Two golden ages, on opposite sides of the planet, flourishing simultaneously in complete ignorance of each other.

![Timeline showing Teotihuacan alongside global civilizations](../images/placeholders/10-teotihuacan-world-timeline.png)

The parallel with Rome is instructive. Both Rome and Teotihuacan were the largest cities in their respective hemispheres. Both were centers of networks that extended over enormous distances. Both were multi-ethnic, drawing people from diverse cultural backgrounds into a shared urban system. Both had monumental architecture that expressed political and religious authority. Both declined and eventually fell, their populations dispersing, their institutions collapsing, their memories transformed into legend by the civilizations that followed.

There is one difference that makes the comparison particularly poignant. Rome and Teotihuacan fell at almost exactly the same time. The last Roman emperor in the West was deposed in 476 CE. Teotihuacan burned around 550 CE. The two largest cities in their respective hemispheres both collapsed within a century of each other, for reasons that, in both cases, involved a combination of internal political crisis, environmental stress, and external pressure. The parallel is striking -- and entirely coincidental. The Roman Empire and Teotihuacan had no knowledge of each other's existence. They rose, flourished, and fell in complete independence, like two experiments run simultaneously in separate laboratories, arriving at similar results.

The comparison also highlights differences. Rome had writing -- extensive, detailed, and preserved in vast quantities. Teotihuacan had, at best, a notational system whose extent and content are debated. Rome's history can be told through the words of Roman writers. Teotihuacan's history can be told only through its buildings and its objects. Rome's fall is one of the most analyzed events in world history, debated by historians for 1,500 years. Teotihuacan's fall is a set of hypotheses, each supported by partial evidence, none confirmed. The difference in our knowledge reflects the difference in the surviving record, not a difference in the importance of the events.

The parallel between the falls of Rome and Teotihuacan extends to their afterlives. Rome became a legend -- the standard against which all subsequent European civilizations measured themselves. Charlemagne sought to revive it. The Holy Roman Empire claimed its mantle. The Renaissance drew its inspiration from Roman models. Teotihuacan played an analogous role in Mesoamerica: the Toltecs claimed to be its heirs, and the Aztecs treated its ruins as a sacred site where the cosmos itself had been born. Both cities became mythologies, shaping the civilizations that followed them for centuries after their fall.

When Teotihuacan burned around 550 CE, the world was in the grip of upheaval. The Plague of Justinian (541-542 CE) was devastating the Byzantine Empire -- the first pandemic of bubonic plague, which killed an estimated 25 to 50 million people across Europe, North Africa, and the Middle East. In China, the Sui Dynasty (581-618 CE) was about to reunify the country after centuries of division, setting the stage for the Tang Dynasty's golden age. In Arabia, the Prophet Muhammad would be born around 570 CE, and the religion he founded would reshape the world within a century of his death. In Mesoamerica, the Maya were entering the Late Classic period, their city-states flourishing in the power vacuum that Teotihuacan's collapse had created.

The sixth century was, by any measure, one of the great hinges of world history. Empires fell and rose. Plagues devastated populations. New religions transformed societies. And in the Valley of Mexico, one of the largest cities in the world burned, its ceremonial center consumed by fire, its population dwindling, its name and language fading into silence.

Teotihuacan's absence from conventional world histories is not a reflection of its insignificance. A city of 125,000 to 200,000 people, sustained for over five centuries, projecting influence across thousands of kilometers, is one of the most remarkable achievements of urban civilization anywhere on Earth. It belongs in any serious account of the ancient world. That it is absent from most such accounts reflects the biases of the historians, not the achievements of the city.

The pyramids still stand. The tunnel still descends beneath the Temple of the Feathered Serpent, its offerings undisturbed for nearly two millennia before their discovery. The murals that survive -- fragments of what must have been an extraordinary artistic tradition -- depict a cosmology of water, serpents, jaguars, and flowering trees. The apartment compounds that housed 125,000 people are still visible, their walls outlining the grid of a city that was, for centuries, one of the most extraordinary places on Earth.

The ongoing archaeological work at the site continues to produce revelations. Each season brings new discoveries from the tunnel, new evidence from residential compounds, new data from remote sensing surveys that reveal features invisible on the surface. In 2015, researchers using muon tomography -- a technique that uses cosmic ray particles to create images of the interior of large structures, the same technology used to discover a hidden chamber in the Great Pyramid of Giza -- began surveying the Pyramid of the Sun, searching for previously unknown internal chambers. The work is ongoing. Teotihuacan may still contain surprises large enough to reshape our understanding of the city.

The city has no name, no voice, and no face. It has only its bones -- its pyramids, its plazas, its tunnels, and its dead. And those bones tell a story that deserves to be heard.
