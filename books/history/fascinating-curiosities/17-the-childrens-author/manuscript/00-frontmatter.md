# A Note to the Reader

The man who wrote *Swallows and Amazons* -- the most beloved children's sailing story in English -- once carried Bolshevik diamonds across the Baltic in his luggage and played chess with the head of Lenin's secret police. He befriended Trotsky. He married Trotsky's secretary. British intelligence used him as a source, and British counterintelligence suspected him of treason. Both had reasons.

Arthur Ransome lived two lives, and the seam between them is one of the strangest biographical facts of the twentieth century. For the first life, he was a journalist and foreign correspondent who spent a decade in revolutionary Russia, reporting on the Bolsheviks with an intimacy that alarmed his own government. For the second, he was the gentle author of twelve children's novels set in the Lake District and the Norfolk Broads, books about sailing and camping and the uncomplicated adventures of childhood. The transition from one life to the other was so complete that for decades almost nobody connected them.

The intelligence files were released in 2005. Roland Chambers' biography, *The Last Englishman*, published in 2009, drew them together with Ransome's own papers to produce the fullest account of the double life. What emerged was a portrait of a man who was simultaneously less sinister and more complicated than the spy legend suggested. Ransome was a journalist who became entangled in a revolution, an informant who genuinely believed in much of what he was informing about, and a man who escaped the wreckage of his early adulthood by retreating into the world of childhood imagination.

This book tells the story of both lives and the gap between them. Where the evidence is clear, we say so. Where it is ambiguous -- and in espionage history, ambiguity is the default -- we present the competing interpretations and let the reader decide.
