# A Note to the Reader

At an altitude of 3,850 meters -- nearly thirteen thousand feet -- on a vast, treeless plain between the Andes and the shore of Lake Titicaca (tee-tee-KAH-kah), stand the ruins of one of the most extraordinary cities ever built. The air is thin. The nights are cold -- freezing is common even in summer. The landscape is brown and flat, stretching to a horizon rimmed by snow-capped peaks. It is one of the least hospitable environments on Earth for a major urban center.

And yet, for over seven centuries -- from roughly 300 to 1000 CE -- a civilization flourished here. The people of Tiwanaku (tee-wah-NAH-koo) built monumental stone architecture of astonishing precision. They carved monoliths and gateways from blocks of andesite and sandstone weighing up to 130 tons. They developed an agricultural system so ingenious that when modern archaeologists reconstructed it experimentally, it outperformed contemporary farming methods. They created an economic network that stretched from the Pacific coast to the Amazon lowlands, spanning one of the most extreme ranges of altitude and climate on the planet.

When the Inca (ING-kah) arrived at Tiwanaku's ruins centuries later, they declared that their own civilization had been born here -- that the creator god Viracocha (vee-rah-KOH-chah) had emerged from the waters of Lake Titicaca to bring the world into being. The Inca, like the Aztecs with the Toltecs, claimed a predecessor's ruins as the birthplace of their own greatness.

This is the story of the city at the top of the world -- the civilization that solved the problem of living where life should have been impossible, and whose solutions were so effective that they may hold lessons for a warming planet.
