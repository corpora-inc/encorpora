# Part One: The City at the Top of the World

## Chapter 1 — Tiwanaku at 12,600 Feet

The Altiplano (ahl-tee-PLAH-noh) -- the high plateau of the central Andes -- is one of the great extremes of the inhabited world. It lies between the Eastern and Western Cordilleras of the Andes, at an average altitude of 3,700 meters, stretching across southern Peru, western Bolivia, northern Chile, and northwestern Argentina. The air holds roughly 40 percent less oxygen than at sea level. Temperatures swing wildly: a summer afternoon can reach 20 degrees Celsius, while the same night drops below freezing. Rainfall is seasonal and unreliable. The soil is thin. The growing season is short. The landscape is open, wind-scoured, and treeless -- a vast brown expanse under an enormous sky.

In this improbable environment, roughly twenty kilometers south of the southern shore of Lake Titicaca, the people of Tiwanaku built a city.

Lake Titicaca itself is part of the equation. It is the largest lake in South America by surface area -- over 8,300 square kilometers, roughly the size of Corsica -- and the highest navigable lake in the world, sitting at 3,812 meters above sea level. The lake moderates the Altiplano's extreme climate: its massive water body absorbs heat during the day and releases it at night, creating a zone of relative warmth along its shores. The lake also supports totora (toh-TOH-rah) reeds, which grow in dense stands along its margins and have been used for millennia to build boats, houses, and floating islands. The Uru (OO-roo) people still live on floating islands of totora reeds in the lake today. Titicaca is not just a geographic feature; it is an ecosystem, a resource, and, for the Andean peoples, a sacred origin point.

Tiwanaku's population at its peak -- roughly 500-800 CE -- is debated. Estimates for the urban core range from 10,000 to 40,000 people. The surrounding Tiwanaku Valley supported a much larger population -- Alan Kolata has estimated up to 365,000 people in the greater Tiwanaku region, sustained by the intensive agricultural system that will be discussed in Part Two. Whether one uses the conservative or generous estimate, Tiwanaku was a major urban center by any pre-industrial standard, and the largest city in the southern Andes for centuries.

The city's monumental core covers approximately four square kilometers. The principal structures include the Akapana (ah-kah-PAH-nah), a massive stepped pyramid roughly 17 meters high and 200 meters on a side, originally faced with sandstone blocks and surmounted by a sunken court at its summit; the Kalasasaya (kah-lah-sah-SAH-yah), a large rectangular platform enclosure with walls of upright sandstone pillars interspersed with smaller blocks, measuring roughly 120 by 130 meters; the Semi-Subterranean Temple, a sunken court whose walls are decorated with carved stone heads protruding from the masonry; and Puma Punku (POO-mah POON-koo), a separate temple complex roughly one kilometer southwest of the main ceremonial center, whose precision-cut stonework is among the most remarkable in the ancient world.

![The ruins of Tiwanaku on the Altiplano, with snow-capped Andes in the background](../images/placeholders/01-tiwanaku-panorama.png)

The stone used in Tiwanaku's construction came from two primary sources. Red sandstone was quarried from hills roughly ten kilometers south of the site. Andesite -- a hard, gray-green volcanic rock -- was quarried from the Copacabana (koh-pah-kah-BAH-nah) Peninsula on the opposite shore of Lake Titicaca, over ninety kilometers away. The andesite blocks were transported across the lake and then overland to the construction site -- a logistical operation that required boats capable of carrying loads of many tons, roads or trackways, and a labor force organized on a scale that implies a powerful central authority. Totora reed boats, still used on the lake today, can be built large enough to carry substantial loads -- the Norwegian explorer Thor Heyerdahl (HAY-er-dahl) demonstrated this in the 1970s with his reed boat experiments. The overland transport from the lakeshore to the site, a distance of roughly twenty kilometers, would have required ramps, rollers, or human hauling on a massive scale. The logistical challenge was comparable to that of transporting the bluestones to Stonehenge or the moai (MOH-eye) statues across Easter Island -- a feat that reveals as much about social organization as about engineering.

The Akapana pyramid dominates the ceremonial center. Its original form was a seven-tiered stepped platform with a T-shaped sunken court at its summit. The court collected rainwater, which was channeled through an elaborate internal drainage system -- stone-lined channels that carried the water down through the pyramid's interior and out at its base. The drainage system was sophisticated enough that it still functions when water is poured into the summit court today, over a thousand years after its construction. The water management may have had ritual significance: the pyramid channeling rainwater from the sky through the body of the mountain and back to the earth, enacting a cosmological cycle of water, sky, and earth.

The Semi-Subterranean Temple is one of Tiwanaku's most distinctive structures. It is a rectangular court sunk into the ground, roughly 28 by 26 meters, whose walls are inset with over 170 carved stone heads -- human faces in a variety of styles, some realistic, some stylized, some apparently depicting individuals from different ethnic groups. The heads protrude from the walls like masks mounted on a gallery, staring inward at the court's center. The variation in styles has led some scholars to suggest that the heads represent the diverse peoples of Tiwanaku's domain -- a gallery of the conquered or the allied, displayed in the sacred center of the imperial capital. At the court's center stands the Bennett Monolith, a massive carved figure over seven meters tall, discovered in 1932 by the American archaeologist Wendell Bennett.

The Bennett Monolith -- named for its excavator, not for its original identity -- is one of the largest stone sculptures in the Andes. It depicts a standing human figure holding a kero (KEH-roh), a ritual drinking cup, in one hand and a staff in the other. The figure's face is flat and mask-like, with rectangular eyes and a straight mouth. Its body is covered with elaborate carved designs: condors, pumas, fish, and geometric patterns that scholars interpret as calendrical or cosmological symbols. The monolith was originally positioned in the center of the Semi-Subterranean Temple, where it would have been the focal point of whatever rituals took place in the sunken court.

John Janusek, in his 2004 study *Identity and Power in the Ancient Andes*, argued that Tiwanaku was not a single unified culture but a complex assemblage of different social groups -- ethnic communities, occupational specialists, religious orders -- brought together in a shared urban space but maintaining distinct identities. The diversity of the stone heads in the Semi-Subterranean Temple may reflect this social complexity: a city that drew people from across the Andes and gave them a place -- literally, a face on the wall -- in its sacred architecture.

The Ponce Monolith, another major carved figure at Tiwanaku, stands in the Kalasasaya courtyard. Roughly three meters tall, it depicts a standing figure holding a kero and a snuff tablet -- a flat tray used for inhaling hallucinogenic substances, particularly the seeds of the vilca (VEEL-kah) tree (*Anadenanthera colubrina*), which produces a powerful psychoactive compound. The presence of snuff tablets and snuff tubes in Tiwanaku's archaeological record -- both in monumental sculpture and in burial goods -- indicates that hallucinogenic substances played a role in Tiwanaku's religious practice. The consumption of vilca may have been part of the rituals conducted in the ceremonial center -- priests or rulers inhaling the substance to achieve visionary states, to communicate with the supernatural world, or to enact cosmological journeys.

The snuff paraphernalia found at Tiwanaku and in Tiwanaku-affiliated burials across the Andes is remarkably consistent: small carved tablets (often decorated with animal imagery -- condors, pumas, serpents), narrow tubes for inhalation, and small spoons or spatulas for handling the powdered seeds. The consistency of the kit suggests a standardized ritual practice, exported from the capital along with the architecture and the pottery. The religious experience itself -- the altered state produced by vilca -- was part of what Tiwanaku spread across its domain.

The modern visitor to Tiwanaku encounters a site that has been heavily disturbed. Centuries of looting, agricultural reuse, and colonial-era quarrying have stripped the monuments of much of their original stone facing. The Akapana pyramid, originally a precisely faced seven-tiered structure, now looks like a weathered hill with exposed adobe core. The Kalasasaya's walls, once made of massive standing stones, have been partially reconstructed -- in some cases inaccurately, with stones placed where restoration teams guessed they should go rather than where they originally stood. Arthur Posnansky's early twentieth-century reconstructions, in particular, have been criticized by modern archaeologists for prioritizing visual impressiveness over archaeological accuracy. The site as it appears today is a palimpsest of original construction, colonial destruction, and modern reconstruction -- layers of human activity obscuring the original form.

The altitude itself was part of Tiwanaku's identity. Living at nearly four thousand meters required physiological adaptation -- larger lung capacity, higher red blood cell counts, more efficient oxygen use. The Andean peoples who have lived at altitude for millennia have evolved genetic adaptations that allow them to thrive where lowlanders struggle to breathe. The altitude was not merely a hardship to be endured; it was a source of identity and, perhaps, of spiritual authority. The city sat closer to the sky than almost any other inhabited place on Earth. Its temples reached toward a sky that was already, by lowland standards, impossibly close.

The Andean concept of *ayllu* (EYE-yoo) -- a kinship group that served as the basic social unit of Andean life -- was almost certainly the foundation of Tiwanaku's social organization. An ayllu is a community of related families who share land, labor obligations, and ritual practices. Ayllus persist in Andean communities today, and ethnographic evidence suggests that the ayllu system is ancient -- predating the Inca and likely predating Tiwanaku as well. In the Tiwanaku context, different ayllus may have occupied distinct neighborhoods within the city, each with its own residential area, its own productive specialization, and its own ritual space within the broader ceremonial complex. Janusek's excavations of residential areas at Tiwanaku revealed distinct neighborhoods with different pottery styles, different food remains, and different burial practices -- evidence that the city's population was organized into discrete social groups, each maintaining its own identity within the urban whole.

The evidence for Tiwanaku's political structure remains ambiguous. The monumental architecture -- the massive pyramids, the carved monoliths, the elaborate drainage systems -- implies centralized authority: someone had the power to mobilize the labor required for these constructions. But whether that authority was vested in a single ruler, a ruling lineage, a council of ayllu leaders, or a priestly class is unknown. No royal tombs have been identified at Tiwanaku. No inscriptions name individual rulers. The problem echoes Teotihuacan, discussed in Volume 5: a major urban center whose political structure remains invisible because the rulers left no personal marks. At Tiwanaku, as at Teotihuacan, the art is impersonal -- it depicts gods and symbols, not named individuals. The city's power is visible; the people who wielded it are not.


## Chapter 2 — The Gate of the Sun

The Gate of the Sun is a single block of andesite, roughly three meters tall and four meters wide, weighing approximately ten tons. It was carved from a single piece of stone -- a feat of quarrying and carving that represents an enormous investment of labor and skill. The gate stands today in the northwest corner of the Kalasasaya platform, but it was not originally located there; it was found fallen and broken in the nineteenth century and re-erected in its current position, which may or may not be its original location.

The gate's upper portion is carved in elaborate relief. At the center stands a frontal figure -- a humanoid form with a rectangular head, radiating appendages ending in circles and animal heads, and a staff held in each hand. This figure is commonly called the "Staff God" or the "Gateway God." It wears an elaborate headdress, and its face is framed by a rectangular border from which rays or appendages project in all directions, some ending in condor heads, others in circles that may represent the sun or other celestial bodies.

![The Gate of the Sun at Tiwanaku with its central Staff God figure](../images/placeholders/02-gate-of-the-sun.png)

Flanking the central figure, arranged in three horizontal rows, are forty-eight smaller figures -- winged attendants or "angels," each depicted in profile, running or genuflecting toward the central figure. Each attendant carries a staff and wears a distinctive headdress. The lower row of attendants has human heads; the upper two rows have condor heads. All face the central figure, their postures expressing movement, urgency, and deference.

The meaning of the Gate of the Sun's relief has been debated for over a century. The interpretations fall into several categories.

The calendrical interpretation, proposed most influentially by Arthur Posnansky (pohs-NAHN-skee) in the early twentieth century and refined by later scholars, argues that the forty-eight attendant figures represent a calendrical system. The figures may encode information about the solar year, the agricultural seasons, or the ritual calendar. Posnansky proposed that the gate functioned as a kind of astronomical instrument, its iconography encoding the movements of the sun, moon, and stars. His specific calculations have been largely rejected by modern scholars, but the general idea that the gate's imagery encodes calendrical information retains adherents.

The political interpretation reads the gate as a charter of authority. The central figure is the supreme deity or the supreme ruler (or both -- the distinction may not apply). The attendants are the subordinate peoples, communities, or spiritual forces that owe allegiance to the center. The gate is a visual declaration of political and cosmic order: one power at the center, all others oriented toward it, the whole arrangement carved in stone and displayed at the heart of the imperial capital.

The cosmological interpretation sees the gate as a map of the universe. The central figure is the creator deity -- possibly an early form of the god the Inca later called Viracocha -- and the attendants represent the forces and beings that populate the cosmos. The condor-headed figures may represent the celestial realm (the condor being the bird of the upper world in Andean cosmology), while the human-headed figures represent the earthly realm. The gate encodes the structure of reality itself.

These interpretations are not mutually exclusive. The gate may be simultaneously a calendar, a political charter, and a cosmological map -- in Andean thought, these categories are not separate. The agricultural calendar governs political authority (a ruler who ensures good harvests is legitimate), and the political order mirrors the cosmic order (the ruler at the center of the state, as the creator is at the center of the cosmos). The gate may express all of these ideas at once, in a visual language whose specific meanings are lost to us.

The Staff God figure has a history that extends far beyond Tiwanaku. Similar frontal figures holding staffs appear in Andean art as early as the Chavin (chah-VEEN) culture, roughly 900-200 BCE -- over a thousand years before Tiwanaku's peak. The persistence of the Staff God motif across Andean cultures and millennia suggests a religious tradition of extraordinary durability -- a core concept in Andean cosmology that each successive civilization adopted and reinterpreted. Tiwanaku's Gate of the Sun is the most elaborate expression of this tradition, but it is not the earliest.

The tears falling from the Staff God's eyes are a notable detail. The central figure appears to weep -- streams flowing down its cheeks, sometimes interpreted as tears, sometimes as rainwater, sometimes as both. In Andean cosmology, water is sacred: it falls from the sky, runs through the earth, collects in lakes and rivers, and sustains all life. A weeping god may be a god who gives water -- whose tears are rain, whose grief is abundance. The association of divine emotion with agricultural fertility is not unique to the Andes (the Greek myth of Demeter's mourning causing winter follows a similar logic), but the specific image of the weeping Staff God is distinctly Andean, and it persists across Andean art for centuries after Tiwanaku's fall.

The forty-eight attendant figures may encode more information than meets the eye. Several scholars have noted that 48 is a significant number in calendrical terms: it is the product of 12 and 4, and it can also be divided into groups that correspond to agricultural seasons and solar positions. Whether these numerical properties are coincidental or intentional is part of the broader question about whether the gate functions as a calendar. The problem is circular: if you assume the gate is calendrical, you can find calendrical patterns; but finding patterns does not prove that the gate was designed as a calendar. The gate's imagery is rich enough to support multiple readings, and without a key to the code, none can be definitively confirmed or ruled out.

The gate was found broken -- a crack runs diagonally across its upper right portion. Whether the break was accidental (the gate falling when the city was abandoned) or deliberate (ritual destruction, comparable to the pattern observed at Mesoamerican sites in earlier volumes) is unknown. If deliberate, the breaking of the Gate of the Sun would represent the same pattern of ritual termination seen at San Lorenzo, Teotihuacan, and Tula -- the destruction of the symbol of power at the end of an era. If accidental, it is simply the result of time and gravity acting on a massive stone in a seismically active region. The answer, like so many at Tiwanaku, is uncertain.


## Chapter 3 — Puma Punku

Puma Punku stands apart from the rest of Tiwanaku -- physically, architecturally, and in the imagination. Located roughly one kilometer southwest of the main ceremonial center, it is a separate temple complex whose stonework achieves a level of precision that has made it famous far beyond the world of Andean archaeology.

The site consists of a massive terraced platform, originally measuring roughly 167 by 117 meters, constructed from enormous blocks of red sandstone and gray andesite. The largest single block at the site weighs an estimated 130 tons. The platform was built using a combination of earth fill and stone retaining walls, with the finest stonework concentrated at the summit, where a series of smaller structures once stood.

The precision of Puma Punku's stonework is genuinely extraordinary. The andesite blocks are cut with flat surfaces, sharp right angles, and uniform dimensions that seem impossible without metal tools. The edges are clean. The surfaces are smooth. Some blocks feature interlocking H-shaped grooves -- precisely cut channels that allowed blocks to be joined using metal clamps, probably copper or bronze. The H-shaped joints are so regular in their dimensions that they appear to have been cut using a template or standardized measuring system. Other blocks have complex stepped profiles -- surfaces with multiple levels and right-angle cuts that fit together like three-dimensional puzzle pieces.

![Precision-cut stone blocks at Puma Punku showing H-shaped joints](../images/placeholders/03-puma-punku-stones.png)

The site's name, Puma Punku, means "Door of the Puma" in Aymara. The puma was one of the three sacred animals in Andean cosmology, along with the condor (representing the upper world) and the serpent (representing the lower world). The puma represented the terrestrial world -- the earth, the physical realm, the place where human beings live. A temple named for the puma was a gateway to the earthly realm of power and authority.

The engineering is real. The fringe theories are not.

Puma Punku has become a favorite of pseudoarchaeological television programs and websites, which attribute its construction to extraterrestrial visitors, lost advanced civilizations, or technologies unknown to mainstream science. The reasoning, such as it is, runs: the stonework is too precise to have been achieved by people with Bronze Age tools, therefore it must have been done by someone or something more advanced. This reasoning fails on two counts. First, the stonework, while impressive, is within the capabilities of skilled stoneworkers using the tools available in the ancient Andes -- stone hammers, bronze chisels, abrasive sand, and enormous amounts of patient, skilled labor. Second, the premise that "primitive" people could not achieve impressive results reflects a bias about human capability that is not supported by the evidence. As every volume in this series has demonstrated, human beings are extraordinary builders. They do not need extraterrestrial assistance to cut stone.

Alexei Vranich's excavations at Puma Punku in the early 2000s revised scholarly understanding of the site. Vranich demonstrated that the platform was not a single unified construction but was built in stages, with evidence of redesign and modification during construction. Some blocks were cut and prepared but never placed in their intended positions -- suggesting that the project was abandoned before completion. The site as it exists today is not a finished monument but a work in progress, interrupted by whatever forces led to Tiwanaku's collapse.

Vranich also proposed that the H-shaped blocks were not structural elements but rather components of a modular construction system -- standardized pieces that could be assembled and disassembled, allowing the reconfiguration of the temple's interior spaces. If correct, this interpretation makes Puma Punku's engineering even more impressive: not merely the construction of a stone building, but the design of a system of interchangeable components that allowed the building itself to be changed. The concept is closer to modern modular architecture than to traditional monolithic construction.

The transportation of the andesite blocks from the Copacabana quarry -- over ninety kilometers away, across the lake and overland -- raises questions about the labor organization and logistical capacity of the Tiwanaku state. Experiments with moving large stone blocks using traditional Andean techniques -- log rollers, earthen ramps, human labor organized in large teams -- suggest that the task was feasible but required coordination on a massive scale. The movement of a single 130-ton block would have required hundreds of workers, working in shifts, over a period of weeks or months. The total effort represented by Puma Punku's construction -- quarrying, transporting, cutting, fitting, and erecting hundreds of massive blocks -- implies a state capable of mobilizing and directing labor on a scale comparable to the great building projects of Egypt or Mesopotamia.

Some of the blocks at Puma Punku have interior right-angle cuts -- grooves and recesses that extend into the body of the stone -- that would be difficult to achieve by abrasion from the outside. How the builders cut internal angles in hard andesite remains a question. One possibility is that they used pointed stone or bronze tools hammered into the stone along a scored line, removing material in small chips. Another is that they used a combination of scoring and controlled fracturing -- cutting a groove to a precise depth, then striking the stone to snap along the groove. Whatever the method, the precision of the results speaks to a level of craft skill that was developed over generations and transmitted through training. No written manual survived, but the skills of the master stonecutters were encoded in the stones themselves -- each precisely cut block a lesson in what human hands can achieve.

The cutting of the blocks themselves was achieved through a combination of techniques. Stone hammers -- heavy rocks used to pound and shape the softer sandstone -- did the rough shaping. For the harder andesite, bronze tools and abrasive techniques were used: sand and water applied to the stone surface while a harder stone or bronze tool was drawn back and forth, gradually grinding the surface to the desired shape. The process was slow -- grinding through a centimeter of andesite might take hours -- but the results, as the surviving blocks attest, were extraordinary. The technology was simple. The skill was not.

The metal clamps that held the blocks together are themselves significant. Analysis of the residual metal in the H-shaped grooves shows that the clamps were made of a copper-arsenic-nickel alloy -- a type of bronze. The clamps were not prefabricated and inserted; they were cast in place. Molten metal was poured into the grooves while the blocks were positioned, and the metal solidified to form a rigid connection. This technique -- pouring molten metal into prepared joints -- is sophisticated metallurgy, indicating that the builders of Puma Punku had both advanced stone-working skills and advanced metalworking capabilities. The combination of stone and metal construction at Puma Punku is unique in the ancient Americas.

Vranich's work also raised questions about the dating of Puma Punku. Earlier scholars had assumed that Puma Punku was among the latest constructions at Tiwanaku, built during the city's final period of monumental activity. Vranich's radiocarbon dates suggested that the construction began earlier than previously thought, possibly as early as 600 CE, and continued over several centuries. If correct, Puma Punku was not a late addition but a long-term project, begun during Tiwanaku's expansion and still under construction when the city collapsed. The unfinished state of the platform -- blocks prepared but never placed, foundations laid but never built upon -- supports this interpretation.

The scattered state of Puma Punku's stones today -- blocks lying at odd angles, toppled and displaced across the site -- is partly the result of centuries of looting. After Tiwanaku's collapse, local communities and, later, Spanish colonial builders used the site as a quarry, carting away blocks for use in churches, bridges, and other construction. The railroad that now runs near the site was built partly from Tiwanaku stone. The destruction was thorough enough that reconstructing the original appearance of Puma Punku's summit buildings is difficult -- the pieces have been scattered, reused, or broken, and the puzzle cannot be fully reassembled.
