# What We Still Don't Know

**What does the Gate of the Sun mean?** The central Staff God figure flanked by forty-eight winged attendants is the most famous image in Andean archaeology, and its meaning remains debated. Calendar, political charter, cosmological map -- all have been proposed. Without a writing system to provide context, the gate's imagery can be interpreted but not deciphered.

**How was Puma Punku's stonework achieved?** The precision-cut andesite blocks with their interlocking H-shaped joints represent extraordinary engineering. The techniques -- stone hammers, bronze tools, abrasive grinding -- are understood in principle, but the level of standardization and precision achieved remains difficult to explain without assuming a sophisticated system of measurement and quality control whose details are unknown.

**What was the relationship between Tiwanaku and Wari?** Two contemporary empires, sharing religious iconography but separated by six hundred kilometers. Rivals, allies, or two political expressions of a shared religious movement? The question is fundamental to understanding the Middle Horizon and remains unresolved.

**Could the raised field system be revived?** Experimental reconstructions in the 1980s showed that ancient raised fields outperformed modern Altiplano agriculture. Revival projects have had mixed results, partly because the social organization required to maintain the fields is difficult to reconstruct. The question has implications for modern food security in highland environments.

**What triggered Tiwanaku's expansion?** The city existed for centuries before its period of maximum expansion (~500-900 CE). What changed? Population growth, political innovation, religious fervor, environmental opportunity -- all are plausible. The specific triggers for the transition from regional center to major power are unclear.

**What was Tiwanaku's political structure?** Was it an empire in the conventional sense, with a single ruler governing distant territories? A confederation of allied communities? A religious network centered on the Staff God cult? The evidence supports elements of all three models, and the answer may vary by period and by region.

**Did Tiwanaku have a recording system?** The Inca used khipu -- knotted strings that recorded numerical and possibly narrative information. Did Tiwanaku or its predecessors use something similar? No khipu have been found at Tiwanaku (they would not survive in the Altiplano's climate), but the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

These are open questions. The ruins still hold secrets.


# Key Names and Terms

**Akapana** (ah-kah-PAH-nah) -- The largest structure at Tiwanaku; a stepped pyramid roughly 17 meters high with an internal drainage system.

**Altiplano** (ahl-tee-PLAH-noh) -- The high plateau of the central Andes, at approximately 3,700 meters altitude.

**Aymara** (ay-MAH-rah) -- The indigenous language and people of the Lake Titicaca region; modern descendants of the Tiwanaku-era population.

**Bennett Monolith** -- A carved stone figure over seven meters tall, found in Tiwanaku's Semi-Subterranean Temple in 1932 by Wendell Bennett.

**Caral** (kah-RAHL) -- The oldest known urban center in the Americas (~3000-1800 BCE), located in the Supe Valley of coastal Peru.

**Chavin** (chah-VEEN) -- An early Andean civilization (~900-200 BCE) that established many of the religious traditions, including the Staff God motif, that Tiwanaku later adopted.

**Chiripa** (chee-REE-pah) -- An early culture (~1400-100 BCE) on the southern shore of Lake Titicaca; precursor to Tiwanaku.

**Chuno** (CHOO-nyoh) -- Freeze-dried potato; an Andean preservation technique that allows potatoes to be stored for years.

**Copacabana** (koh-pah-kah-BAH-nah) -- A peninsula on Lake Titicaca; source of the andesite used in Tiwanaku's finest stonework.

**Erickson, Clark** -- American archaeologist; conducted raised field reconstruction experiments at Lakaya.

**Gate of the Sun** -- A monolithic andesite gateway at Tiwanaku, carved with the Staff God figure and forty-eight winged attendants.

**Goldstein, Paul** -- American archaeologist; specialist in Tiwanaku colonies, particularly the Omo site in the Moquegua Valley.

**Isbell, William** -- American archaeologist; leading scholar of the Wari empire.

**Janusek, John** -- American archaeologist; author of *Identity and Power in the Ancient Andes* (2004).

**Kalasasaya** (kah-lah-sah-SAH-yah) -- A large rectangular platform enclosure at Tiwanaku, location of the Gate of the Sun.

**Kero** (KEH-roh) -- A ritual drinking cup, used for the consumption of chicha (corn beer) in Andean ceremonies.

**Khipu** (KEE-poo) -- Knotted-string recording devices used by Andean civilizations, particularly the Inca.

**Kolata, Alan** -- American archaeologist; author of *The Tiwanaku* (1993) and *Tiwanaku and Its Hinterland* (1996, 2003).

**Late Intermediate Period** -- The archaeological period (~1000-1400 CE) between Tiwanaku/Wari's collapse and the Inca expansion.

**Middle Horizon** -- The archaeological period (~600-1000 CE) corresponding to the expansion of Tiwanaku and Wari.

**Murra, John** (MOOR-rah) -- American anthropologist who described the "vertical archipelago" economic model.

**Nazca Lines** (NAHS-kah) -- Enormous geoglyphs on the coastal desert of southern Peru, created 500 BCE-500 CE.

**Pukara** (poo-KAH-rah) -- An early ceremonial center on the northern shore of Lake Titicaca; earliest Staff God imagery in the region.

**Puma Punku** (POO-mah POON-koo) -- A temple complex at Tiwanaku known for its precision-cut stonework and H-shaped interlocking joints.

**Puquio** (poo-KEE-oh) -- An underground aqueduct used by the Nazca to tap subterranean water sources.

**Quelccaya glacier** (kehl-KAH-yah) -- A glacier in the Peruvian Andes whose ice cores provide key paleoclimate data for the region.

**Quinoa** (KEEN-wah) -- An Andean grain domesticated ~3000 BCE; provides complete protein and grows at high altitude.

**Semi-Subterranean Temple** -- A sunken court at Tiwanaku whose walls are inset with over 170 carved stone heads.

**Spondylus** (spon-DIH-luhs) -- A thorny oyster whose red and orange shells were prized ritual objects throughout the ancient Andes.

**Staff God** -- A frontal figure holding staffs, flanked by profile attendants; the central religious icon of Tiwanaku and the broader Andean tradition.

**Stanish, Charles** -- American archaeologist; author of *Ancient Titicaca* (2003).

**Suka kollu** (SOO-kah KOH-yoo) -- Raised agricultural field; Tiwanaku's signature farming technology.

**Tiwanaku** (tee-wah-NAH-koo) -- A major Andean civilization and its capital city, located at 3,850 meters near Lake Titicaca; flourished ~300-1000 CE.

**Totora** (toh-TOH-rah) -- A reed that grows along the shores of Lake Titicaca, used for boats, houses, and floating islands.

**Vertical archipelago** -- The Andean economic strategy of maintaining colonies at different altitudes to access diverse ecological resources.

**Viracocha** (vee-rah-KOH-chah) -- The Inca creator deity, believed to have emerged from Lake Titicaca.

**Vranich, Alexei** -- American archaeologist; excavator of Puma Punku.

**Wari** (WAH-ree) -- A contemporary empire to Tiwanaku, centered near Ayacucho in central Peru; flourished ~600-1000 CE.

**Yaya-Mama** (YAH-yah MAH-mah) -- An early religious tradition in the Titicaca basin, characterized by carved stelae with paired male-female figures.


# Further Reading

**For the general reader:**

- Kolata, Alan L. *The Tiwanaku: Portrait of an Andean Civilization*. Blackwell, 1993. -- The best single-volume introduction to Tiwanaku.

- Stanish, Charles. *Ancient Titicaca: The Evolution of Complex Society in Southern Peru and Northern Bolivia*. University of California Press, 2003. -- Comprehensive regional history from the earliest cultures to the Inca.

- Mann, Charles C. *1491: New Revelations of the Americas Before Columbus*. Vintage, 2006. -- Excellent survey with strong sections on Andean civilizations and the raised field system.

**For the archaeology:**

- Kolata, Alan L., ed. *Tiwanaku and Its Hinterland: Archaeology and Paleoecology of an Andean Civilization*. 2 vols. Smithsonian Institution Press, 1996 and 2003. -- The definitive multi-volume study.

- Janusek, John Wayne. *Identity and Power in the Ancient Andes: Tiwanaku Cities through Time*. Routledge, 2004. -- Social organization and identity at Tiwanaku.

- Isbell, William H., and Helaine Silverman, eds. *Andean Archaeology*. 3 vols. Springer, 2002-2008. -- Comprehensive reference for Andean archaeology including Tiwanaku and Wari.

**For the Andean context:**

- Murra, John V. *The Economic Organization of the Inka State*. JAI Press, 1980. -- Essential for understanding the vertical archipelago model that Tiwanaku pioneered and the Inca formalized.

- Moseley, Michael E. *The Incas and Their Ancestors*. Thames & Hudson, 2001. -- Accessible survey of Andean civilizations from Caral to the Inca.


# About This Series

**Fascinating Curiosities of Human History** is a series of short, intense books about the most interesting things people have done, built, written, and wondered about. Each volume begins with a genuine mystery and uses it as a doorway into a world of knowledge -- the people, the places, the context, and the open questions.

The series is designed for curious readers of all ages. Every sentence should be accessible to a bright ten-year-old and fascinating to an adult. We never talk down, we never pad, and we are always honest about what we know and what we do not.
