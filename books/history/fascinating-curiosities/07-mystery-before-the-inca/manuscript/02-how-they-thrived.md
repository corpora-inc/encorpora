# Part Two: How They Thrived

## Chapter 4 — Farming the Impossible

The Altiplano's altitude creates a fundamental agricultural problem: frost. At nearly four thousand meters, freezing temperatures can occur on any night of the year, even during the growing season. A single hard frost can destroy an entire crop of potatoes or quinoa (KEEN-wah) -- the two staple crops of Andean highland agriculture. The growing season is short, the rainfall unpredictable, and the soil thin. Agriculture at this altitude is not merely difficult; it is an ongoing negotiation with an environment that does not want crops to grow.

The people of Tiwanaku solved this problem with an agricultural technology so effective that it remained unmatched for a thousand years.

The solution was the *suka kollu* (SOO-kah KOH-yoo) -- the raised field system. The concept is deceptively simple: instead of planting crops on flat ground, farmers constructed elevated planting platforms -- raised beds of earth, typically one to two meters high, several meters wide, and tens of meters long -- separated by channels of water. The planting surfaces were built up from layers of clay, gravel, and topsoil, creating a deep, well-drained growing medium elevated above the water table.

The water channels between the raised fields were the key innovation. The channels, typically one to three meters wide, were kept filled with water during the growing season. During the day, the water absorbed heat from the sun. At night, when air temperatures plunged below freezing, the warm water radiated heat back into the air -- creating a microclimate around the raised fields that was several degrees warmer than the surrounding landscape. The temperature difference was enough to protect the crops from frost. The raised field system did not merely grow food; it created an artificial climate zone in which food could be grown.

![Reconstructed suka kollu raised fields near Lake Titicaca](../images/placeholders/04-raised-fields.png)

The raised fields provided additional benefits beyond frost protection. The water channels accumulated organic sediment -- decomposing plant material, algae, and fish waste -- that could be dredged and applied to the planting surfaces as fertilizer. The channels also supported aquatic life: fish, freshwater snails, and algae that provided additional food sources. The raised beds themselves, elevated above the water table, were better drained than flat fields, preventing waterlogging during the rainy season. The system was a complete agricultural package: frost protection, drainage, fertilizer production, and aquaculture, all integrated into a single landscape design.

The most remarkable evidence for the raised fields' effectiveness came from experimental archaeology. In the 1980s, the archaeologist Clark Erickson, working with Alan Kolata and local Aymara (ay-MAH-rah) farmers near the village of Lakaya, reconstructed a section of ancient raised fields using traditional techniques. The results were extraordinary. The reconstructed raised fields produced potato yields of ten tons per hectare -- roughly seven times the average yield from conventional Altiplano agriculture, and comparable to yields from modern industrial farming using chemical fertilizers and irrigation. The ancient technology outperformed modern methods.

The experimental results stunned the archaeological community. Here was a case where ancient agricultural engineering was not merely adequate but superior -- a pre-Columbian technology that, in a specific environment, produced better results than its modern replacement. The implications extended beyond archaeology into contemporary development: if raised fields worked better than modern farming on the Altiplano, why not rebuild them? Erickson and Kolata promoted the idea of reviving the raised field system as a development strategy for Altiplano communities, and several projects were initiated in the 1980s and 1990s to reconstruct raised fields and return them to production.

The revival projects had mixed results. Some succeeded -- producing good harvests and demonstrating the system's viability. Others failed, often because the social organization required to maintain the fields -- the cooperative labor, the water management, the long-term commitment to maintaining the channels -- was difficult to sustain in modern communities with different economic structures and incentive systems. The raised fields were not just a technology; they were a social system. Building the fields required coordinated labor on a scale that implied communal organization -- teams of workers constructing and maintaining hundreds of kilometers of raised platforms and channels. The technology and the society that supported it were inseparable. You could rebuild the fields, but rebuilding the social structure that maintained them was harder.

The scale of the original raised field system was immense. Satellite imagery and archaeological survey have identified remnants of raised fields covering approximately 120 square kilometers in the Tiwanaku Valley alone -- a landscape of organized agricultural infrastructure that, at its peak, would have supported the entire urban population of Tiwanaku and its surrounding settlements. Similar raised field systems have been identified in other parts of the Lake Titicaca Basin, in the Llanos de Mojos (YAH-nohs deh MOH-hohs) in the Bolivian lowlands, and in other Andean locations, suggesting that the technology was widespread in the pre-Columbian Andes.

The comparison with modern agriculture is worth emphasizing. The Altiplano today is one of the poorest regions in South America. Subsistence farmers struggle with the same challenges that confronted Tiwanaku's builders: frost, poor soil, unreliable rainfall. Modern yields from conventional farming average roughly 1.4 tons of potatoes per hectare. The ancient raised field system, as reconstructed by Erickson and Kolata, produced ten tons per hectare. The ancient technology was not merely adequate; it was seven times more productive than its modern replacement. This is one of the few cases in world history where an ancient agricultural system can be demonstrated to have outperformed modern methods in the same environment.

The frost protection mechanism deserves closer examination. On the Altiplano, the critical danger period is the growing season night, when clear skies allow heat to radiate rapidly into space, dropping temperatures below freezing. The water channels between the raised fields absorb solar energy during the day -- water's high thermal mass means it heats slowly but also cools slowly. At night, the stored heat radiates from the water surface into the air above the adjacent planting platforms. Measurements taken during the Lakaya experiments showed that temperatures on the raised field surfaces were 2 to 4 degrees Celsius higher than on adjacent flat ground. The difference is small in absolute terms but enormous in agricultural terms: it is the difference between a surviving crop and a dead one.

The channels also supported a productive aquatic ecosystem. Nitrogen-fixing blue-green algae grew in the channels, enriching the water with nutrients. When channel sediment was dredged and applied to the planting surfaces -- a practice the experimental teams adopted from archaeological evidence of how the ancient fields were maintained -- it provided a rich organic fertilizer. The channels also harbored small fish that fed on the algae, providing protein for the farming families. The raised field system was not a single technology but an integrated ecosystem, each component supporting the others.

The raised field system was abandoned after Tiwanaku's collapse around 1000 CE. When the prolonged drought that contributed to the collapse reduced the water available for the field channels, the system failed -- the frost protection depended on the water, and without it, crops were exposed to the killing cold. The fields were abandoned, their platforms eroding over the centuries into the low mounds and shallow depressions that archaeologists can still identify in the landscape. The technology was lost, replaced by simpler dryland farming methods that produced lower yields but did not require the elaborate water management that the raised fields demanded.

The Andean peoples also developed other extraordinary agricultural technologies. The potato, domesticated in the Lake Titicaca region around 8000 BCE, is one of the world's most important crops -- today it feeds over a billion people. The Andean peoples developed over three thousand varieties of potato, adapted to different altitudes, climates, and soil conditions. They invented *chuno* (CHOO-nyoh), a freeze-dried potato product made by exposing potatoes to the Altiplano's freezing nights and then drying them in the sun -- a preservation technique that allowed potatoes to be stored for years. Chuno is one of the oldest examples of freeze-drying technology in the world.

The terracing of hillsides was another critical agricultural technology. Andean terraces -- stone-walled platforms built into steep slopes, converting a hillside into a series of level planting surfaces -- served multiple purposes. They prevented erosion, capturing soil that would otherwise wash away. They created flat growing surfaces on terrain too steep for conventional agriculture. And they modified the local microclimate: the stone walls absorbed heat during the day and radiated it at night, providing frost protection similar to the raised fields' water channels. The combination of raised fields in the lowlands and terraces on the hillsides created a comprehensive agricultural system that exploited every available surface in the landscape.

The Andean peoples also domesticated the llama and the alpaca -- the only domesticated pack and fiber animals in the pre-Columbian Americas. The llama, domesticated from the wild guanaco (gwah-NAH-koh) around 4500 BCE, served as a pack animal, a source of meat, and a source of coarse wool. The alpaca, domesticated from the wild vicuna (vee-KOO-nyah), produced finer wool -- soft, warm, and lightweight, ideal for the textiles that were among the Andes' most prestigious craft products. Llama and alpaca herding was practiced at the highest altitudes, above the agricultural zone, where the animals grazed on the sparse grasses of the *puna* (POO-nah) -- the highest habitable zone of the Andes, between roughly 4,000 and 5,000 meters. The herders who tended the animals at these extreme altitudes occupied the uppermost tier of the vertical archipelago, contributing wool, meat, and transportation to the integrated Andean economy.

Quinoa, domesticated in the Andes around 3000 BCE, is another Andean crop that has recently gained global recognition. It grows at altitudes where most cereals fail, tolerates poor soil and low rainfall, and provides a complete protein -- containing all nine essential amino acids -- making it nutritionally superior to most grains. The Andean peoples who domesticated and improved quinoa over millennia created one of the world's most nutritious food plants, adapted to one of the world's most difficult growing environments.

The Andean contribution to global food production is, in fact, enormous. The potato, quinoa, the tomato, the pepper, and dozens of other crops that now feed billions of people worldwide were domesticated by Andean and South American peoples. The Andes are one of the world's great centers of plant domestication -- comparable in importance to the Fertile Crescent (wheat, barley, lentils), China (rice, millet, soybeans), and Mesoamerica (maize, squash, beans). The agricultural knowledge that Tiwanaku's farmers applied to the raised fields was the product of millennia of experimentation, observation, and selective breeding -- a scientific tradition conducted without microscopes, without laboratories, and without written records, but yielding results that transformed the human diet worldwide.

The coca leaf, chewed or brewed into tea by Andean peoples for at least eight thousand years, deserves mention. At Tiwanaku's altitude, coca serves a practical function: it suppresses hunger, reduces the symptoms of altitude sickness, and provides a mild stimulant that helps sustain physical labor in thin air. Coca leaves were among the goods transported from the lowland colonies to the highland capital. The leaf's importance was not merely pharmacological; it was ritual. Coca was offered to the earth, to the mountains, and to the dead. It was an essential component of Andean ceremony, and its trade connected the lowland growing regions to the highland centers of power. The vertical archipelago was sustained not just by potatoes and maize but by the leaves of a small, unprepossessing shrub that grew in the warm eastern valleys.


## Chapter 5 — The Tiwanaku Network

Tiwanaku was not an isolated city on a plateau. It was the center of a network that stretched from the Pacific coast, at sea level, to the eastern slopes of the Andes, where the mountains descend into the tropical forests of the Amazon basin -- a vertical range of over four thousand meters.

This network operated on a principle that the anthropologist John Murra (MOOR-rah) described as the "vertical archipelago." The concept is distinctly Andean: instead of controlling a continuous territory at a single altitude, Andean states established colonies at different altitudes, each producing the resources available in its particular ecological zone. A community based at four thousand meters on the Altiplano might maintain colonies at lower altitudes to grow maize (which does not mature above about 3,400 meters), cotton, and coca (the source of cocaine, but in pre-Columbian times a mild stimulant chewed as a leaf, essential for life at altitude because it reduces the effects of altitude sickness). At higher altitudes, the same community would maintain herds of llamas (YAH-mahs) and alpacas (ahl-PAH-kahs), which provided wool, meat, and -- critically -- transportation. The llama is the only domesticated pack animal in the pre-Columbian Americas, capable of carrying loads of up to thirty kilograms across the high passes and steep trails of the Andes.

![Map showing Tiwanaku's vertical archipelago network across different ecological zones](../images/placeholders/05-vertical-archipelago-map.png)

Paul Goldstein's work on Tiwanaku colonies has identified several lowland sites with clear Tiwanaku affiliation. The Omo site in the Moquegua (moh-KEH-gwah) Valley of southern Peru, roughly three hundred kilometers southwest of Tiwanaku and at an altitude of only 1,100 meters, contains Tiwanaku-style architecture, pottery, and burial practices that indicate a permanent Tiwanaku community -- not merely a trade post but a colony, with families living, working, and burying their dead in a landscape far removed from the Altiplano. The Moquegua Valley, with its warm climate and reliable irrigation water, was ideal for growing maize, peppers, and other crops that could not be grown at Tiwanaku's altitude. The colony provided these products to the highland capital, which in return provided the high-altitude goods -- dried llama meat, chuno, quinoa, wool, and the prestige goods manufactured at the capital itself.

The exchange was not simply economic. Tiwanaku-style temples and ritual spaces at lowland colonies indicate that the ideological system of the capital was exported to the periphery. The colonies were not merely economic outposts; they were extensions of the Tiwanaku world, complete with its architecture, its pottery styles, its burial customs, and its religious practices. The vertical archipelago was simultaneously an economic system and a cultural one -- a way of organizing space, production, and identity that integrated diverse ecological zones into a single political and spiritual whole.

The concept of *ayni* (EYE-nee) -- reciprocal labor exchange -- governed how work was organized in Andean societies. Under ayni, community members contributed labor to collective projects with the expectation that the favor would be returned. A family that helped build a neighbor's house could expect the neighbor's help when their own house needed building. Extended to the state level, ayni became a system in which communities contributed labor to the state (for construction projects, agricultural work, military service) in exchange for the state's provision of security, ritual services, and redistribution of goods. The raised field system, with its enormous labor requirements, was likely maintained through a state-organized version of ayni -- communities contributing workers in rotation to the fields that fed the entire population.

The Inca would later formalize this system as *mit'a* (MEE-tah) -- a labor tax in which communities were required to contribute a set number of work-days per year to state projects. The Inca mit'a built the road system, the storehouses, and the great stone architecture of Cusco and Machu Picchu (MAH-choo PEEK-choo). The system's origins, however, almost certainly lie in the pre-Inca Andean tradition that Tiwanaku embodied: the idea that the state provides and the people build, bound together by reciprocal obligation.

The maritime connection is less well documented but no less important. The Pacific coast, roughly two hundred kilometers to the west and four thousand meters below, provided marine resources: fish, shellfish, seaweed, and guano (bird droppings accumulated on coastal islands, a powerful fertilizer). Shells from the warm Pacific waters -- particularly Spondylus (spon-DIH-luhs) shells, prized for their red and orange colors -- appear at Tiwanaku and were important ritual objects throughout the Andes. The long-range acquisition of Spondylus shells, which originate from the waters off Ecuador, thousands of kilometers to the north, indicates that Tiwanaku's economic network extended far beyond its directly controlled territories. Spondylus was called *mullu* (MOO-yoo) in Quechua (KECH-wah) and was associated with water, fertility, and the sea. It was offered to mountain springs, buried in agricultural fields, and placed in tombs. The shell's red and orange colors linked it to the warm, life-giving properties of the tropical ocean -- a world utterly different from the cold, dry Altiplano. Possessing Spondylus was possessing a piece of the distant sea, a fragment of warmth and abundance brought to a land of cold and scarcity.

Llama caravans were the arteries of this system. Caravans of dozens or hundreds of llamas, each carrying a load of goods, traveled the routes between the coast, the highlands, and the eastern slopes. The caravans followed established trails that had been used for millennia -- paths worn into the rock by generations of human and animal feet. The caravan routes were the predecessors of the Inca road system, which would later formalize and expand them into one of the most impressive road networks in the pre-modern world. The Inca did not invent Andean long-distance transportation; they systematized a network that Tiwanaku and its predecessors had already created.

The eastern slopes of the Andes -- the *yungas* (YOONG-ahs) -- provided yet another set of resources. The yungas are a zone of transition between the high Andes and the Amazon basin, where altitude drops rapidly and the climate shifts from cold and dry to warm and humid. The yungas produce coca, tropical fruits, hardwoods, feathers from tropical birds, and other products unavailable on the Altiplano. Tiwanaku maintained connections with yungas communities, though the nature of these connections -- direct control, trade relationships, or something in between -- is debated.

The Tiwanaku heartland itself was organized for maximum productivity. Satellite imagery reveals that the landscape around Tiwanaku was densely terraced on hillsides and covered with raised fields in the lowland areas, creating a patchwork of agricultural infrastructure that transformed the natural landscape into a engineered food-production system. The scale of landscape modification was enormous -- comparable, in its way, to the landscape engineering of the Nile Valley or the rice terraces of Southeast Asia. The Altiplano around Tiwanaku was not a natural landscape with some farming; it was a designed landscape, engineered from the ground up for agricultural production.

Chicha (CHEE-chah), a fermented corn beer, was central to Tiwanaku's social and political life. The kero cups depicted on monoliths and found in archaeological deposits were used for chicha consumption in ritual feasting. Large-scale chicha production required substantial quantities of maize -- which, crucially, does not grow at Tiwanaku's altitude. The corn had to come from the lowland colonies. The feasting economy thus depended on the vertical archipelago: the maize that fed the feasts came from colonies hundreds of kilometers away and thousands of meters lower. Political authority, ritual practice, and economic organization were woven together into a single system -- each supporting the others, each dependent on the others.

Tiwanaku's religious influence traveled with its economic connections. Tiwanaku-style pottery, textiles, and religious objects have been found at sites far from the capital, in contexts that suggest not merely trade but the active spread of religious practices. The kero cup -- the ritual drinking vessel depicted on monoliths and used in feasting ceremonies -- appears at Tiwanaku colonies and affiliated sites across the region. The snuff-taking paraphernalia associated with vilca consumption has been found in burials from the Pacific coast to the eastern slopes. The Staff God motif, originally associated with the Gate of the Sun, appears on pottery, textiles, and wood carvings across the entire range of Tiwanaku influence. The religion and the economy were one system: to participate in Tiwanaku's trade network was to participate in its ritual life, and vice versa.

The total picture is of a state organized around the principle of complementarity -- the idea that no single environment provides everything a society needs, and that survival requires access to multiple ecological zones. The vertical archipelago was not unique to Tiwanaku; it is a distinctly Andean strategy, practiced to some degree by communities throughout the Andes from before Tiwanaku's time to the present. But Tiwanaku operated the system on a scale that was unprecedented in the southern Andes -- coordinating production across thousands of meters of altitude and hundreds of kilometers of distance, binding coast, highland, and jungle into a single economic and political system.
