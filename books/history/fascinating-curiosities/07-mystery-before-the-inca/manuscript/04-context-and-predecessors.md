# Part Four: Context and Predecessors

## Chapter 8 — Before Tiwanaku

Tiwanaku did not emerge from nothing. The Lake Titicaca basin has one of the longest records of human habitation in the Americas, and the traditions that Tiwanaku drew upon were already ancient when the city's first stones were laid.

The earliest complex societies in the Titicaca basin belong to the Chiripa (chee-REE-pah) culture, which flourished from roughly 1400 to 100 BCE on the southern shore of Lake Titicaca, near the site that would later become Tiwanaku. Chiripa produced distinctive pottery, built rectangular stone buildings around a sunken court, and practiced a religion that scholars call the Yaya-Mama (YAH-yah MAH-mah) tradition. The Yaya-Mama tradition is named for carved stone stelae that depict paired figures -- male and female, father and mother -- on opposite sides of a single stone. The pairing of complementary opposites -- male and female, above and below, left and right -- is a fundamental principle of Andean thought that persists to this day. The Yaya-Mama tradition established the conceptual vocabulary that Tiwanaku would later elaborate on a monumental scale.

Charles Stanish, in his 2003 study *Ancient Titicaca*, traced the development of political complexity in the basin over several millennia. Stanish argued that the Titicaca basin was one of the independent centers of civilization in the Americas -- a place where complex society developed not through contact with other centers but through the internal dynamics of a growing, increasingly stratified population exploiting a rich lacustrine (lake-based) environment. The lake's fish, reeds, and water birds provided reliable food sources. The surrounding terraces supported potato and quinoa agriculture. The combination of aquatic and agricultural resources created a surplus that supported craft specialization, trade, and eventually the emergence of political elites.

The site of Pukara (poo-KAH-rah), located on the northern shore of Lake Titicaca in present-day Peru, was the first major ceremonial center in the region, flourishing from roughly 500 BCE to 200 CE. Pukara's art includes some of the earliest Staff God imagery in the southern Andes -- a tradition that Tiwanaku would later make its own. The carved stone stelae at Pukara show frontal figures holding staffs, flanked by attendant figures, in compositions that foreshadow the Gate of the Sun. The religious tradition that culminated at Tiwanaku was already centuries old when Tiwanaku's first builders began their work.

Between Pukara's decline and Tiwanaku's rise, the site that would become Tiwanaku was already occupied. The earliest phases of settlement at the site date to roughly 200 BCE, when it was a modest village. Over the following centuries, the village grew, its architecture became more elaborate, and its pottery developed the distinctive styles that would later characterize the full Tiwanaku state. The transition from village to city was gradual, spanning several centuries -- not a sudden foundation but a slow accumulation of people, buildings, rituals, and prestige. The monumental construction that defines Tiwanaku today began in earnest around 300-400 CE and continued for over five centuries.

The ceramics of the early Tiwanaku period show clear connections to the Yaya-Mama tradition and to Pukara, but they also show innovations -- new vessel forms, new decorative motifs, new firing techniques -- that distinguish Tiwanaku from its predecessors. The kero cup, which would become the defining ceramic form of the Tiwanaku tradition, appears in the early phases as a simple cylindrical vessel and becomes increasingly elaborate over time, with painted and incised decoration depicting Staff God figures, condors, pumas, and geometric patterns. The kero was not merely a container; it was a ritual object, a vehicle for the consumption of chicha in the feasting ceremonies that cemented social bonds and reinforced political authority.

Far to the northwest, on the Pacific coast of Peru, an even older civilization raises questions about the origins of Andean complexity. Caral (kah-RAHL), located in the Supe (SOO-peh) Valley roughly two hundred kilometers north of present-day Lima, is the oldest known urban center in the Americas. Radiocarbon dates place Caral's monumental construction at approximately 3000-1800 BCE -- making it roughly contemporary with the great pyramids of Egypt. Caral had six large platform mounds, a sunken circular court, residential areas, and evidence of a substantial population. The site had no pottery -- its inhabitants lived before the invention of ceramics in the region -- and no evidence of warfare. It appears to have been sustained by a combination of maritime resources (anchovies and shellfish from the nearby coast) and irrigated agriculture (cotton and gourds).

The discovery of Caral's antiquity, established by the Peruvian archaeologist Ruth Shady (SHAH-dee) through radiocarbon dating in the late 1990s, was a watershed moment in American archaeology. It pushed the timeline for complex society in the Americas back by over a millennium, demonstrating that monumental architecture in the Americas was as old as in the Old World. Caral's builders constructed their pyramids without pottery, without writing, without metal tools, and apparently without warfare -- the site has no fortifications and no weapons in its archaeological record. The absence of war-related artifacts at Caral challenges the assumption that organized violence is a prerequisite for state formation -- an assumption that the evidence from other early civilizations (Mesopotamia, Egypt, China) tends to support.

Caral is not directly ancestral to Tiwanaku -- the two sites are separated by over a thousand kilometers and three thousand years. But Caral demonstrates that the Andean tradition of monumental architecture and urban planning is among the oldest in the world. The impulse to build pyramids, to organize communities around ceremonial centers, to invest enormous collective labor in the construction of monuments -- this impulse was present in the Andes five thousand years ago, making the Andean architectural tradition as old as Mesopotamia's and Egypt's.

The Nazca (NAHS-kah) Lines, located on the coastal desert of southern Peru roughly five hundred kilometers northwest of Tiwanaku, represent another dimension of Andean achievement. The Nazca Lines are geoglyphs -- enormous figures drawn on the desert surface by removing the dark surface stones to reveal the lighter ground beneath. The figures include animals (a spider, a hummingbird, a monkey, a condor), geometric shapes (spirals, trapezoids, straight lines extending for kilometers), and abstract designs. The largest figures are over three hundred meters across -- visible in their entirety only from the air.

The Nazca Lines cover an area of roughly 450 square kilometers -- a vast canvas in the desert. They were created between roughly 500 BCE and 500 CE by the Nazca culture, which was contemporary with the early phases of Tiwanaku but culturally distinct. The lines were not created for aerial viewing -- the Nazca had no aircraft. They were created as paths: ceremonial routes walked during rituals, possibly associated with water and fertility. The straight lines may point toward water sources in the mountains. The animal figures may represent constellations or deities associated with water and agricultural abundance. The lines are carved into one of the driest deserts on Earth, a landscape where water is the most precious resource, and their iconography consistently points toward water -- the obsession of a people living in a land where rain almost never falls.

The Nazca Lines, like Puma Punku, have attracted pseudoarchaeological speculation -- claims that they were landing strips for ancient aircraft, signals to extraterrestrial visitors, or the products of technologies unknown to conventional science. These claims fail for the same reasons that similar claims about Puma Punku fail: the lines are entirely within the capabilities of organized human labor using simple tools. Experimental archaeology has demonstrated that a small team of people with wooden stakes and cord can lay out large geometric figures on the desert surface in a matter of days. The lines are impressive because of their scale and their persistence (the desert climate preserves them nearly indefinitely), not because they required extraordinary technology. They are evidence of human organization, not alien intervention.

The connection between the Nazca Lines and Tiwanaku is indirect but real. Both civilizations confronted the same fundamental Andean challenge: how to sustain human life in an extreme environment. Tiwanaku solved the problem of cold and frost through the raised field system. The Nazca solved the problem of aridity through underground aqueducts called *puquios* (poo-KEE-ohs) -- spiral-shaped wells that tapped into underground aquifers and channeled water to the surface for irrigation. Both solutions demonstrate the Andean genius for environmental engineering -- the creation of habitable landscapes in places where habitability is not given but must be constructed.


## Chapter 9 — What Was Happening Elsewhere

Tiwanaku's peak -- roughly 500 to 900 CE -- coincided with a period of extraordinary dynamism across the world.

In East Asia, the Tang Dynasty (618-907 CE) presided over what is often called the golden age of Chinese civilization. The Tang capital, Chang'an (chahng-AHN), was the largest city in the world, with a population of over one million. Tang China was cosmopolitan, drawing merchants, diplomats, and scholars from across Asia. Tang poetry -- the work of Li Bai (lee BY), Du Fu (doo FOO), and hundreds of others -- remains the standard of literary excellence in the Chinese tradition. The Tang invented woodblock printing, refined porcelain production, and maintained a civil service examination system that selected government officials based on merit rather than birth. The sophistication of Tang civilization was unmatched anywhere in the contemporary world.

In the Islamic world, the Umayyad (oo-MY-yad) and Abbasid Caliphates were transforming the intellectual and political landscape of the Middle East, North Africa, and Central Asia. The translation movement in Baghdad -- a centuries-long project to translate Greek, Persian, and Indian scientific and philosophical texts into Arabic -- preserved and transmitted knowledge that might otherwise have been lost. Islamic scholars made original contributions to mathematics (algebra, trigonometry), astronomy (improved astrolabes, star catalogs), medicine (hospitals, pharmacology), and optics (Ibn al-Haytham's (IB-uhn ahl-HAY-tahm) work on light and vision). The intellectual achievements of the Islamic Golden Age laid the foundations for the European Renaissance.

In Europe, the Carolingian Empire under Charlemagne (SHAHR-leh-mayn) united much of Western Europe for the first time since the fall of Rome. Charlemagne was crowned Emperor of the Romans by Pope Leo III in 800 CE -- a date that falls squarely within Tiwanaku's peak period. The Carolingian Renaissance -- a revival of learning and literacy centered on monastic scriptoria -- preserved many of the classical texts that would later inspire the Italian Renaissance. After Charlemagne's death in 814, his empire fragmented, leading to the political divisions that would eventually produce the nation-states of modern Europe.

In Mesoamerica, the Maya Classic period was at its height. The great Maya cities of the Late Classic -- Palenque (pah-LEHN-keh), Tikal, Copan (koh-PAHN), Calakmul (kah-lahk-MOOL) -- were producing the extraordinary art, architecture, and hieroglyphic inscriptions that define the Classic Maya in the popular imagination. Teotihuacan, discussed in Volume 5, had already fallen, and the Maya city-states were flourishing in the power vacuum. The Maya were literate -- they had a full writing system capable of recording speech -- while Tiwanaku, like most Andean civilizations, communicated through non-textual media: textiles, ceramics, stone carving, and the knotted-string recording devices called *khipu* (KEE-poo). The difference in media -- text versus textile, inscription versus knot -- means that Maya history can be told in the words of the Maya, while Tiwanaku's history can be told only through its material remains.

In Japan, the Nara (NAH-rah) and Heian (HAY-ahn) periods saw the flowering of classical Japanese culture. The construction of the great Buddhist temples at Nara, the development of Japanese writing systems, and the literary achievements of the Heian court -- including *The Tale of Genji*, written around 1000 CE by Murasaki Shikibu (moo-rah-SAH-kee shee-KEE-boo), considered the world's first novel -- all coincided with Tiwanaku's peak. The refined aesthetics of Heian court culture and the monumental stone austerity of Tiwanaku's architecture could hardly be more different in sensibility, yet both represent the heights of their respective civilizations at exactly the same moment in time.

In sub-Saharan Africa, the Kingdom of Aksum (ahk-SOOM), in what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea, was one of the great powers of the ancient world. Aksum controlled trade routes linking the Red Sea to the Indian Ocean, traded with Rome, Persia, and India, and was one of the earliest kingdoms to adopt Christianity (in the fourth century CE). The Aksumite stone stelae -- towering carved obelisks, some over twenty meters tall -- are among the most impressive monuments of the ancient world. Tiwanaku and Aksum were exact contemporaries, separated by the Atlantic Ocean and the breadth of Africa, building monumental stone architecture in complete ignorance of each other.

In the Americas itself, the period of Tiwanaku's peak was one of extraordinary diversity. While Tiwanaku was building its raised fields on the Altiplano, the Maya were carving hieroglyphic inscriptions in the jungles of Guatemala, the people of Cahokia were beginning the construction of Monks Mound in the Mississippi Valley, and the Ancestral Puebloans were building cliff dwellings in the canyons of the American Southwest. Each of these civilizations developed independently, in isolation from the others, producing unique solutions to unique environments -- and each, in its own way, reached a peak of achievement and then experienced decline. The pattern of rise and fall, of construction and abandonment, of brilliance and collapse, is not specific to any one culture or environment. It is a pattern of human civilization itself.

The parallel between Tiwanaku's collapse and contemporary events elsewhere is less precise but still suggestive. The drought that devastated Tiwanaku around 1000 CE was a regional event, but the late first millennium CE saw environmental stress in many parts of the world. The Classic Maya collapse -- the abandonment of the great Maya cities between roughly 800 and 1000 CE -- was driven in part by drought, as paleoclimate evidence has increasingly confirmed. The two collapses -- Maya in Mesoamerica, Tiwanaku in the Andes -- were driven by similar environmental pressures and occurred within a century or two of each other. Whether they shared a common climatic cause (a broader pattern of tropical drought) or were independent events triggered by local conditions is debated.

The connections between Tiwanaku and other sites discussed in this series are worth noting. Cahokia, discussed in Volume 4, was a near-exact contemporary of Tiwanaku -- both peaked between roughly 500 and 1000 CE -- and both collapsed under environmental stress. Teotihuacan, discussed in Volume 5, preceded Tiwanaku and operated a different kind of urban system (apartment compounds in a valley rather than colonies across altitudes), but both cities projected influence over enormous distances through a combination of trade, religion, and political authority. The Toltecs, discussed in Volume 6, filled the post-Teotihuacan vacuum in Mesoamerica at the same time that the post-Tiwanaku vacuum was being filled by the Aymara kingdoms in the Andes. The parallel timing -- the rise and fall of civilizations on roughly the same schedule across the Americas, without contact -- is one of the patterns that makes pre-Columbian history so fascinating.

The modern study of Tiwanaku continues to produce new insights. Recent remote-sensing surveys using ground-penetrating radar and satellite-based lidar (light detection and ranging) have revealed previously unknown structures beneath the surface at the site -- buried walls, plazas, and channels that expand the known extent of the monumental center. The raised field remnants in the surrounding landscape are being mapped with increasing precision using satellite imagery, revealing the full scale of the agricultural system that sustained the city. Each new technology applied to the site reveals more of what was hidden, suggesting that Tiwanaku, like Teotihuacan with its tunnel, may still hold surprises.

The site was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000 -- a recognition of its importance to world cultural heritage. The modern Aymara communities that live near the ruins maintain a complex relationship with the site: it is both an archaeological monument and a living sacred place, a site of pilgrimage and ceremony as well as scientific study. The tension between archaeological investigation and indigenous spiritual practice is an ongoing challenge at Tiwanaku, as at sacred sites around the world.

Tiwanaku's place in world history is that of an extraordinary local achievement with global significance. A city built at the limits of human habitation, sustained by an agricultural technology that outperformed its modern successor, connected to colonies spanning thousands of meters of altitude -- Tiwanaku represents one of the most ambitious experiments in human adaptation to a hostile environment ever attempted. That the experiment ultimately failed -- that the drought of 1000 CE destroyed the agricultural system that sustained the city -- does not diminish the achievement. For seven centuries, human beings thrived at the top of the world. The ruins on the Altiplano testify to what they built, and the raised fields, barely visible under a thousand years of erosion, testify to the ingenuity that sustained them.
