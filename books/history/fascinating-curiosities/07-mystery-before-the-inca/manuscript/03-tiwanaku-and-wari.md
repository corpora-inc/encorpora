# Part Three: Tiwanaku and Wari

## Chapter 6 — The Other Empire

Tiwanaku was not alone. Six hundred kilometers to the northwest, in the highlands of central Peru near the modern city of Ayacucho (ah-yah-KOO-choh), another major state was expanding at roughly the same time: the Wari (WAH-ree) Empire.

Wari emerged around 600 CE and reached its peak between 700 and 1000 CE -- a timeline that overlaps almost exactly with Tiwanaku's floruit. The Wari capital, located near Ayacucho at an altitude of roughly 2,800 meters, covered over fifteen square kilometers and may have housed 20,000 to 40,000 people. The Wari built planned cities, road networks, and administrative centers across a territory that stretched from the coast of southern Peru to the highlands of central Peru -- an empire comparable in geographic extent to Tiwanaku's but organized differently.

The Wari capital itself is an imposing site. Its core area covers roughly 15 square kilometers of terraced hillside, with massive stone walls enclosing complexes of rooms, plazas, and corridors. Some walls stand over ten meters high. The architecture is dense and labyrinthine -- a maze of enclosed spaces that suggests a highly controlled, hierarchical society in which access to certain areas was restricted. The contrast with Tiwanaku's more open ceremonial spaces is striking. Tiwanaku's sunken courts and platform enclosures were designed to be entered; Wari's architecture was designed to control who entered and where they could go.

The Wari state was more overtly imperial than Tiwanaku. Where Tiwanaku's expansion appears to have operated through colonies, trade, and religious influence, Wari imposed its authority through administrative centers -- planned settlements built in a distinctive D-shaped layout, with high walls, regular room plans, and evidence of state-directed activities including storage, craft production, and feasting. The archaeologist William Isbell, the leading scholar of Wari, has argued that these administrative centers represent a form of imperial control that is without precedent in the Andes before the Inca: a centralized state that governed distant territories through standardized institutions imposed from the capital.

The similarity between Wari and Tiwanaku's religious iconography is one of the great puzzles of Andean archaeology. Both civilizations used the Staff God motif -- the frontal figure holding staffs, flanked by profile attendants. Both used similar geometric designs on their textiles and pottery. Both employed a distinctive, angular artistic style that scholars call the "Middle Horizon" style (the Middle Horizon being the archaeological period from roughly 600 to 1000 CE, corresponding to the period of Wari and Tiwanaku expansion).

The shared iconography raises the question: what was the relationship between Wari and Tiwanaku? Three interpretations have been proposed.

The first is that Wari and Tiwanaku were rival empires, competing for territory and influence. In this view, they shared iconography because they shared a common cultural heritage -- both emerging from the Andean highland tradition -- but they were politically independent and potentially hostile. The geographic gap between them, roughly six hundred kilometers of highland terrain, may have been a no-man's-land or buffer zone. Some sites in the region between the two empires show evidence of both Wari and Tiwanaku influence, suggesting contested territory.

The second is that they were allied powers, sharing a religious ideology while dividing the Andean world into complementary spheres of influence -- Tiwanaku controlling the southern highlands and the Lake Titicaca basin, Wari controlling the central highlands and the coastal valleys. The shared iconography, in this view, reflects a shared religion -- a pan-Andean cult of the Staff God that both states promoted. The relationship may have been formalized through diplomatic ties, intermarriage, or religious agreements.

The third is that Wari and Tiwanaku were two political expressions of the same underlying religious movement -- a spread of Staff God worship that originated at one site (probably Tiwanaku, given the deeper roots of the Staff God tradition in the Titicaca basin) and was adopted by political elites at the other. In this view, the shared iconography reflects religious transmission, not political alliance or rivalry. The religion came first; the states developed independently around a shared belief system.

The site of Cerro Baul (SEH-rroh bah-OOL) in the Moquegua Valley provides the most dramatic evidence for the Wari-Tiwanaku relationship. Cerro Baul is a flat-topped mountain, rising 600 meters above the valley floor, on which the Wari established an administrative center -- a planned settlement with D-shaped structures, storage facilities, and a brewery capable of producing large quantities of chicha for ritual feasting. The Wari settlement at Cerro Baul sits just twenty kilometers from Tiwanaku colonies in the same valley. The two empires maintained neighboring communities in the same fertile valley, apparently for centuries, without evidence of warfare between them. Whether this coexistence was peaceful by choice, enforced by a diplomatic agreement, or sustained by a shared religious framework that made conflict unthinkable is debated. The Moquegua Valley may have been a neutral zone, a shared resource, or a contested borderland where the two empires watched each other warily from their respective hilltops.

When the Wari abandoned Cerro Baul, they did so deliberately and ritually. The final act at the site was a massive feast: hundreds of large chicha vessels were filled, consumed, and then smashed on the ground. The buildings were then burned. The destruction was thorough, deliberate, and ceremonial -- a ritual termination event reminiscent of the burning of ceremonial centers at Mesoamerican sites discussed in earlier volumes. The Wari ended their presence at Cerro Baul with the same combination of feasting and destruction that marked the end of eras across the ancient Americas.

The question of Wari-Tiwanaku relations is difficult to resolve because the two civilizations left different kinds of evidence. Tiwanaku's monumental stone architecture has survived relatively well, but its organic materials -- textiles, wooden objects, food remains -- have largely decomposed in the damp Altiplano climate. Wari, by contrast, is known primarily through its textiles (preserved in the dry coastal valleys) and its ceramics, while its stone architecture is less imposing than Tiwanaku's. The two civilizations are visible through different archaeological windows, making direct comparison difficult.

Wari textiles are among the most technically accomplished in the ancient world. The finest examples -- tapestries woven from alpaca wool and cotton, dyed in vivid reds, blues, yellows, and greens -- achieve over 200 threads per centimeter, a density that exceeds most medieval European weaving. The designs include Staff God figures, geometric patterns, and abstracted animal imagery -- condors, pumas, snakes -- rendered in the angular, compressed style characteristic of the Middle Horizon. The textiles were not merely decorative; they were carriers of information. The patterns, colors, and compositions encoded political and religious messages that could be "read" by those familiar with the visual vocabulary. Wari textiles were a communication technology, a portable medium for the display of power and religious authority.

The Wari storage system was another innovation that foreshadowed the Inca. At Wari administrative centers, archaeologists have found rows of identical storage rooms -- small, rectangular, and built to standard dimensions. The rooms stored agricultural products, textiles, and other goods collected from subject communities and redistributed by the state. The Inca would later build their famous *qollqa* (KOHL-kah) storehouses on a much larger scale -- over 2,500 storerooms at the site of Huanuco Pampa alone -- but the concept of state-managed storage originated with the Wari (and possibly with Tiwanaku, though the evidence is less clear).

The Wari road network, while less famous than the later Inca roads, was a precursor to them. Wari built roads connecting their administrative centers, and these roads were maintained and used for centuries before the Inca incorporated them into their own system. The Inca did not invent the Andean road; they inherited and expanded it. The same is true of many Inca institutions -- the storage system, the administrative hierarchy, the vertical archipelago economic model -- all of which had precedents in Wari and Tiwanaku practices. The Inca genius was not invention but synthesis: they combined the innovations of their predecessors into a unified system of unprecedented scale.


## Chapter 7 — The Collapse

Around 1000 CE, Tiwanaku collapsed. The monumental construction ceased. The population of the city declined. The colonies at lower altitudes were abandoned or severed their ties to the capital. The raised field system fell into disuse. Within a few generations, the city that had been the center of the southern Andean world was reduced to a ruin.

The evidence for the cause is unusually clear. Ice cores extracted from the Quelccaya (kehl-KAH-yah) glacier in the Peruvian Andes -- one of the most important paleoclimate records in the Southern Hemisphere -- show a dramatic reduction in precipitation beginning around 1000 CE. The drought was severe and prolonged, lasting roughly three centuries. Lake Titicaca's water level dropped significantly, exposing shoreline that had been submerged for millennia and reducing the lake's moderating influence on the surrounding climate.

The drought was catastrophic for Tiwanaku's agricultural system. The raised fields required water in the channels between the planting platforms. The channels provided frost protection (through the radiation of stored heat), fertilizer (through the accumulation of organic sediment), and drainage (by controlling the water table). Without water in the channels, the system could not function. The raised fields, Tiwanaku's greatest innovation, became useless.

The relationship between the drought and the collapse illustrates a broader principle: the vulnerability of specialized systems to environmental change. Tiwanaku's raised field system was brilliantly adapted to the Altiplano's normal climate -- the seasonal rains, the frost-prone nights, the thin soil. But its very specialization made it fragile. The system depended on adequate water supply. When the water failed, the system failed with it. A less specialized agricultural system -- simple dryland farming, for example -- would have been less productive in normal times but more resilient in drought. Tiwanaku had optimized for productivity rather than resilience, and the optimization proved fatal when conditions changed.

The evidence from the raised fields themselves is telling. Paleoecological studies of the Tiwanaku Valley -- pollen analysis, soil chemistry, sediment cores from channels -- show that the raised fields were maintained for centuries during Tiwanaku's peak, with regular dredging of channels, application of organic fertilizer, and management of the aquatic ecosystem. After approximately 1000 CE, the maintenance stopped. Channels silted up. Planting surfaces eroded. The labor-intensive system that had sustained the population simply ceased to function. The failure was not gradual; it was relatively abrupt -- within a generation or two, the fields were abandoned and the landscape reverted to scrubland.

The decline was not instantaneous, but it was irreversible. Tiwanaku's monumental construction had already slowed before the drought reached its worst phase. The Akapana pyramid, for example, was never finished -- its upper tiers were left partially incomplete, with exposed adobe core that should have been faced with stone. The construction halt predates the full collapse, suggesting that the political system was already under strain before the drought delivered the final blow. The drought may have been the trigger, but the bullet was already loaded.

Alan Kolata has argued that the collapse was not merely an economic event but a political and religious crisis. Tiwanaku's rulers derived their authority, in part, from their ability to manage the agricultural system -- to ensure the food supply, to organize the labor that maintained the raised fields, to mediate between the human community and the forces of nature. When the drought struck and the fields failed, the rulers' authority was undermined. They could not deliver what they had promised. The social contract between rulers and ruled -- we organize, you eat -- was broken by forces that the rulers could not control. The collapse of Tiwanaku may have involved the same pattern seen at Teotihuacan, Tula, and other urban centers in the Americas: environmental stress leading to a loss of political legitimacy, leading to the disintegration of the political system that held the society together.

Wari collapsed at approximately the same time, under similar circumstances. The drought that devastated Tiwanaku's agricultural system also affected Wari's territories. The synchronous collapse of both major Andean states -- roughly simultaneously, in response to the same environmental trigger -- created a power vacuum across the central and southern Andes that lasted for centuries. The period from approximately 1000 to 1400 CE is known in Andean archaeology as the Late Intermediate Period -- a time of political fragmentation, regional autonomy, and, in some areas, conflict. The pattern mirrors the post-Teotihuacan Epiclassic in Mesoamerica: the collapse of the center, the fragmentation of the periphery, and the eventual emergence of a new unifying power.

The Lake Titicaca region after Tiwanaku's collapse entered a period that archaeologists have called balkanization -- the fragmentation of a formerly unified political space into competing, often hostile, smaller units. The drought that destroyed the raised fields did not kill the population; it scattered it. People moved to defensible positions, abandoned the valley-bottom settlements, and organized themselves into smaller, more localized communities. The cultural unity that Tiwanaku had imposed -- the shared pottery styles, the shared religious iconography, the shared ceremonial practices -- dissolved. Each community developed its own local variations, and the uniformity of the Tiwanaku era gave way to diversity.

The Aymara kingdoms that emerged in the Titicaca basin after Tiwanaku's collapse were smaller, more dispersed, and more militaristic than Tiwanaku had been. They built fortified hilltop settlements called *pukaras* (poo-KAH-rahs) -- defensive sites on elevated terrain, surrounded by walls and moats, designed to protect against the raids and conflicts that characterized the Late Intermediate Period. The contrast with Tiwanaku is stark: Tiwanaku's monumental center, located on open ground in the valley bottom, had no fortifications. It was a city of temples, not a city of walls. The shift from Tiwanaku's open, temple-centered urbanism to the Aymara kingdoms' fortified hilltop settlements reflects a fundamental change in the political landscape -- from centralized authority to fragmented competition, from ritual power to military power.

That new power was the Inca. Arising from the Cusco (KOOS-koh) Valley in the thirteenth century, the Inca would eventually build the largest empire in the pre-Columbian Americas -- stretching over four thousand kilometers from modern Colombia to central Chile. The Inca inherited much from Tiwanaku: the vertical archipelago economic model, the road network, the textile traditions, the Staff God religious iconography (transformed into the Inca creator deity Viracocha), and the political concept of a highland capital governing diverse ecological zones. The Inca also inherited Tiwanaku's ruins, which they incorporated into their own origin mythology.

According to the Inca creation myth, the god Viracocha emerged from the waters of Lake Titicaca at the beginning of time. He created the sun, the moon, and the stars. He then traveled to Tiwanaku, where he created the first human beings from stone -- forming them, painting them with different clothes and hairstyles to represent the different ethnic groups, and sending them underground to emerge at their respective homelands. Tiwanaku was thus the birthplace of all humanity, the place where the creator began his work. The Inca did not merely admire Tiwanaku's ruins; they claimed them as the origin of the world itself.

The Inca relationship to Tiwanaku is one of the most intriguing examples of appropriation in the ancient Americas. When the Inca ruler Pachacuti (pah-chah-KOO-tee), who led the great expansion of the Inca state in the fifteenth century, visited Tiwanaku's ruins, he reportedly ordered artisans to study the stone architecture and to use it as a model for Inca construction. The finest Inca stonework -- the precision-fitted, mortarless walls of Cusco and Sacsayhuaman (sahk-say-wah-MAHN) -- may owe something to the Tiwanaku tradition, transmitted across four centuries of intervening time. Whether the Inca actually learned stone-cutting techniques from studying Tiwanaku's ruins, or whether both traditions independently developed similar engineering solutions, is debated. But the Inca belief that they were the heirs of Tiwanaku shaped how they built, how they governed, and how they understood their place in the world.

The pattern is by now familiar from this series. The Aztecs claimed Toltec ancestry to legitimize their rule. The Toltecs may have claimed Teotihuacan's legacy. The Inca claimed Tiwanaku. In each case, a new power appropriated the ruins and the memory of its predecessor, weaving the older civilization into its own origin story. The ruins of great cities are political resources -- raw material for the construction of legitimacy.

The Spanish, arriving in the 1530s, found Tiwanaku already in ruins. The chronicler Pedro Cieza de Leon (see-EH-sah deh leh-OHN), writing in the 1550s, described the site as "the oldest antiquity in all of Peru" and marveled at its stonework. He noted that even the Inca could not explain who had built the ruins, attributing them to a time before the Inca -- before, in the Inca worldview, the current era of history had begun. The Spanish, like the Inca before them, recognized the site's power and antiquity. They did not study it scientifically -- that would wait until the nineteenth and twentieth centuries -- but they recorded their impression of a place where something extraordinary had once existed and then, for reasons no one could explain, had ended.

The ruins endure. The Gate of the Sun, cracked but legible, still displays its Staff God and attendant figures. The Akapana, stripped of its stone facing, still rises above the plain. The raised fields, barely visible under a thousand years of erosion and overgrazing, still mark the landscape with their geometry. And Lake Titicaca, sacred and immense, still reflects the sky and the mountains, as it did when the first builders chose this place -- this improbable, extreme, extraordinary place -- to construct a city at the top of the world.
