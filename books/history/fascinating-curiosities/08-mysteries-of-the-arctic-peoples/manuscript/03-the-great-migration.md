# Part Three: The Great Migration

## Chapter 5 — The Thule Expansion

Around 1000 CE -- give or take a century -- a cultural transformation began in northwestern Alaska that would reshape the entire Arctic world. The people archaeologists call the Thule (TOO-lee) developed a way of life centered on the hunting of the bowhead whale -- the largest animal in the Arctic, a creature that can weigh up to one hundred tons and provide enough meat, blubber, and oil to sustain a community for months. Armed with this capability, the Thule swept eastward from Alaska across the Canadian Arctic and into Greenland in one of the fastest large-scale migrations in human history.

The Thule are the direct ancestors of the modern Inuit. Their language, Inuktitut (ih-NOOK-tih-toot) and its dialects, is spoken today from Alaska to Greenland. Their technologies -- the kayak, the umiak (OO-mee-ahk), the dog sled, the toggling harpoon, the snow house -- became the foundation of Inuit material culture. Their migration is the event that created the cultural geography of the modern Arctic.

The Thule homeland was the coast of northwestern Alaska, particularly the region around the Bering Strait and the northern Alaska coast from Point Hope to Barrow (now Utqiagvik, oot-KAH-vik). This is whale country. Every spring, bowhead whales migrate northward through the leads (open channels) in the sea ice, moving from the Bering Sea into the Beaufort Sea and the Arctic Ocean. The whales travel close to shore as they navigate the narrow passages between the ice and the land, making them accessible to hunters stationed at promontories along the coast.

The Thule whale-hunting complex was a sophisticated technological and social system. The basic tool was the toggling harpoon -- a harpoon with a detachable head designed to rotate ninety degrees after penetrating the whale's skin, locking itself under the blubber and preventing withdrawal. Attached to the harpoon head by a braided sinew line was a series of inflated sealskin floats (avataq, AH-vah-tahk) that served two purposes: they created drag, slowing the wounded whale, and they prevented the whale from diving deep enough to escape. Multiple harpoons, each with its own set of floats, could be planted in a single whale, creating an accumulating burden of drag that eventually exhausted the animal.

The hunt was conducted from umiaks -- large, open skin boats, typically six to ten meters long, framed with driftwood or whale bone and covered with walrus or bearded seal skins. An umiak could carry a crew of eight to twelve hunters and was stable enough to approach a surfacing whale. The umiak was the essential platform for whale hunting; without it, the hunt was impossible. The Dorset, who had no umiaks, could not hunt whales. The Thule could.

The social organization required for whale hunting was as important as the technology. A bowhead whale hunt involved multiple boat crews, each with its own umiak, harpoons, and floats. The crews had to coordinate their approach, their strikes, and their recovery of the killed whale. After a successful hunt, the whale had to be towed to shore, hauled up on the beach, and butchered -- an operation requiring the labor of an entire community. The distribution of the whale's products (meat, blubber, oil, baleen, bone) followed elaborate rules that varied by community but generally ensured that every family received a share. The whale hunt was both an economic activity and a social institution -- it created the obligations, hierarchies, and rituals that structured Thule (and later Inuit) community life.

The umialik (oo-mee-AH-lik) -- the whaling captain, literally "the one who has an umiak" -- was the central figure in Thule social organization. The umialik was typically a skilled hunter, a man of wealth (measured in stored food, hunting equipment, and dog teams), and a generous host. His authority derived from his ability to organize successful hunts and to distribute the products generously -- leadership through competence and generosity, maintained by continuous performance. An umialik who failed to provide was replaced; there was no inherited position, only demonstrated ability.

The trigger for the Thule expansion is debated. Several factors likely contributed. The Medieval Warm Period -- a phase of relatively warm climate that lasted from roughly 900 to 1300 CE -- may have reduced sea ice extent in the Arctic, opening passages that had been blocked and extending the range of bowhead whale migration routes. Warmer conditions would have made travel easier and expanded the areas where whale hunting was productive. The Thule, already adapted to whale hunting in Alaska, followed the whales eastward as the warming climate opened new territory.

Population growth may also have been a factor. The whale-hunting economy was enormously productive -- a single bowhead whale could provide tens of thousands of kilograms of food. This abundance supported population growth, which in turn created pressure to expand into new territories. The expansion may have been driven less by a deliberate decision to migrate than by the gradual spread of a growing population along a moving frontier of opportunity.

Military superiority is another possibility. The Thule had bows, dogs, and the organizational structures of the whale-hunting complex. The Dorset, as described in Chapter 2, lacked all of these. In any confrontation between Thule and Dorset groups, the Thule held overwhelming advantages in mobility, weaponry, and social organization. Whether such confrontations actually occurred is unknown, but the asymmetry of capability was severe.

The speed of the Thule expansion was remarkable. Archaeological evidence suggests that the Thule crossed the entire Canadian Arctic -- from the Mackenzie Delta in the west to Greenland in the east, a distance of roughly five thousand kilometers -- within one to two centuries. By 1200 CE, Thule sites appear in the eastern Arctic and in northwestern Greenland. By 1300 CE, the Thule had reached the east coast of Greenland and the northern tip of Labrador. Within three hundred years of the expansion's beginning, the Thule had colonized the entire North American Arctic.

The expansion was not a single, directed migration but a series of movements -- families and small groups pushing east along the coastline, establishing settlements at productive hunting locations, and then sending further groups onward. The pattern is consistent with a "wave of advance" model, in which population growth at the frontier drives continuous expansion, with each generation moving slightly farther east than the last.

The Thule brought with them a complete technological package: dog sleds for winter travel over the ice, kayaks for individual hunting of seals and caribou at water crossings, umiaks for whale hunting and coastal travel, toggling harpoons, compound bows (constructed from multiple pieces of wood, antler, and sinew laminated together to create a weapon more powerful than any single-piece bow), snow houses for winter shelter, soapstone lamps for heating and lighting, and the social organization of the whale-hunting crew. This package was so effective that it remained the foundation of Inuit material culture until the introduction of European trade goods in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

The dog sled deserves particular attention. The Thule used large teams of dogs -- typically five to twelve animals -- harnessed in a fan formation (in the eastern Arctic) or a tandem formation (in western Alaska). The dogs pulled sleds (qamutiik, kah-MOO-teek) constructed of driftwood, bone, or antler runners, lashed together with sinew or rawhide. A dog team pulling a loaded sled could cover forty to sixty kilometers in a day over good ice -- a range that made the Arctic accessible in a way that foot travel could not. The dogs also served as pack animals in summer, as sentinels (barking to alert the camp to approaching bears), and, in emergencies, as food. The relationship between Inuit and their dogs was intimate, complex, and essential -- without dogs, the Thule way of life was impossible.

The archaeological signature of the Thule expansion is unmistakable. Thule sites are characterized by large semi-subterranean houses built with whale bone and stone, often of considerable size -- some Thule houses are large enough for multiple families, with separate sleeping platforms and storage areas. The use of whale bone as a structural material (ribs and jawbones serving as roof supports, vertebrae as building blocks) is diagnostic: only a whale-hunting culture would have access to the enormous bones of bowhead whales, and the presence of whale-bone architecture is evidence of successful whaling.

The material culture of the Thule is rich and diverse. In addition to their whale-hunting equipment, the Thule produced finely crafted harpoon heads (in a variety of styles, suggesting regional and temporal variation), snow goggles (narrow slits carved in bone or ivory, worn to prevent snow blindness), needle cases (decorated tubes of bone or ivory, containing sewing needles), combs, gaming pieces, and toys. Thule women's tools -- the ulu (OO-loo), a crescent-shaped knife used for cutting and scraping; the needle and thimble; the skin-softening tools -- are well represented in the archaeological record and reflect the central role of women in Thule economic life. A woman's sewing skills were as essential to family survival as a man's hunting skills: without properly made clothing, boots, and tent covers, the cold would kill.

Thule art is less elaborate than Dorset art -- the miniature masterpieces of ivory carving that characterize the Dorset have no parallel in the Thule tradition. Thule decorative art tends toward geometric patterns -- incised lines, dots, and circles on tool handles and personal objects. Whether this represents a different aesthetic sensibility, a different relationship between art and spiritual life, or simply a different allocation of time and effort (the demands of whale hunting and processing leaving less time for artistic elaboration) is debated. The contrast between Dorset artistic richness and Thule technological richness is one of the striking features of the Arctic archaeological record: the people with the simpler tools made the more complex art.

The contrast with the Dorset is stark. The Dorset had no dogs, no bows, no kayaks, no umiaks, and no whale-hunting technology. They were, in every measurable way, less technologically equipped for Arctic life than the Thule. And yet the Dorset had survived in the Arctic for three thousand years -- longer than the Thule have existed as a distinct culture. Survival is not solely a matter of technology; it is also a matter of adaptation, of finding a way of life that works within the constraints of the available tools and the environment. The Dorset found such a way. The arrival of the Thule simply confronted them with a competitor who had found a better one.


## Chapter 6 — The Norse Encounter

The Thule expansion into Greenland brought them into contact with another group of newcomers from the west: the Norse.

Erik the Red (Eirikr Thorvaldsson, AY-rik THOR-valds-son) established the first Norse settlement in Greenland around 985 CE, founding the Eastern Settlement (Eystribyggd, AY-stree-bigd) on the southwestern coast. A second settlement, the Western Settlement (Vestribyggd, VES-tree-bigd), was established farther north on the same coast. At their peak, the Norse settlements in Greenland supported a population of perhaps three thousand to five thousand people, living in farmsteads that raised cattle, sheep, and goats on the narrow coastal pastures.

The Norse settlements were located in the southwestern part of Greenland, in fjords where the climate was relatively mild (by Greenlandic standards) and where grass grew in sufficient quantities to support livestock. The Norse economy was based on animal husbandry supplemented by seal hunting, fishing, and the export of prestige goods -- walrus ivory, polar bear hides, and narwhal tusks -- to European markets. The settlements had churches (Greenland became a diocese of the Catholic Church in 1126), a cathedral (at Gardar, modern Igaliku, ee-GAH-lee-koo), and connections to the broader medieval European world through annual ship traffic to Norway and Iceland.

The Thule reached the areas occupied by the Norse sometime in the twelfth or thirteenth century. The evidence for contact between the two groups is tantalizing but sparse. Norse artifacts -- pieces of iron, bronze objects, fragments of woven cloth, chain mail links -- have been found at Thule sites in the High Arctic, hundreds of kilometers north of the Norse settlements. These objects were almost certainly obtained through trade, scavenging, or occasional direct encounters rather than through sustained interaction.

Peter Schledermann, a Danish-Canadian archaeologist who spent decades investigating Norse-Thule contact in the High Arctic, documented numerous Norse artifacts at Thule sites on Ellesmere Island and in the Smith Sound region -- the narrow strait between Ellesmere Island and northwestern Greenland. The artifacts include a fragment of a bronze balance arm (a weighing device), iron knife blades, fragments of chain mail, and pieces of woolen cloth. These objects are far from the Norse settlements, suggesting either long-distance Thule trade networks or Norse expeditions into the High Arctic (which are documented in Norse sagas -- the Greenlanders regularly traveled north to hunt walruses and bears in a region they called Nordrsetur, NORD-reh-seh-toor).

What is remarkable is how little interaction there appears to have been, given that the two groups occupied the same island for roughly three centuries. The Norse left almost no archaeological trace in Thule sites beyond scattered trade goods, and the Thule left almost no trace in Norse sites. There are no accounts of sustained trade relationships, no intermarriage, no cultural borrowing on a significant scale. The two groups appear to have maintained a careful distance from each other -- coexisting on the same enormous island while remaining, for the most part, separate.

The Norse sagas contain a few references to encounters with people they called *skraelingar* (SKRAY-ling-ar) -- a term they also applied to the Indigenous peoples they encountered in Newfoundland (probably the Beothuk or Mi'kmaq). The sagas describe the skraelingar as skilled hunters who used skin boats and lived very differently from the Norse. The accounts are brief and often hostile in tone, reflecting the mutual suspicion that characterized most Norse-Indigenous encounters.

One intriguing artifact from the Norse period is a small carving found at a Thule site near Baffin Island. The carving, made from walrus ivory, depicts a figure wearing what appears to be a long robe or cassock with a cross incised on the chest. The figure has been interpreted as a Thule carving of a Norse clergyman -- a visual record of an encounter between the two cultures, rendered in the Thule artistic tradition. If this interpretation is correct, it is one of the very few material records of face-to-face Norse-Thule contact.

The end of the Norse settlements in Greenland is itself a mystery. The Western Settlement was abandoned around 1350 CE. The Eastern Settlement survived longer but was also abandoned by the mid-fifteenth century -- the last documented contact with the Eastern Settlement was a letter from 1408 describing a wedding at the church in Hvalsey (KVAL-say). After that, silence. When European ships returned to Greenland in the early modern period, they found only ruins.

The causes of the Norse abandonment are debated. The onset of the Little Ice Age (a period of cooling that began around 1300 CE and lasted into the nineteenth century) made the already marginal Greenlandic climate even harsher. Growing seasons shortened. Pastures deteriorated. Sea ice expanded, making navigation to and from Europe more difficult and dangerous. The Norse economy, based on raising livestock and exporting Arctic luxury goods, was doubly vulnerable: the livestock suffered from the worsening climate, and the market for Arctic goods was disrupted by the Black Death (1346-1353), which killed a large portion of Europe's population and disrupted trade networks.

The Norse diet in Greenland tells the story of a slow, reluctant adaptation. Isotopic analysis of Norse bones from Greenland churchyards reveals a clear trend over the centuries of settlement. The earliest Norse settlers ate a diet dominated by terrestrial foods -- meat and dairy from their cattle and sheep, supplemented by some fish and game. By the later centuries, the proportion of marine food in the diet had increased dramatically: seal and fish became the staples, while cattle products declined. The shift was not a choice but a necessity. As the climate cooled and pastures deteriorated, the livestock that anchored the Norse economy could no longer be sustained in sufficient numbers. The Norse turned to the sea -- but they turned to it reluctantly, as a fallback rather than as a primary strategy, and they never developed the specialized marine-hunting technologies that the Inuit had perfected over millennia.

The Norse farmsteads themselves tell a similar story of decline. The earliest farms had byres (barns) large enough for dozens of cattle. Later farms had smaller byres. The last farms, from the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, show evidence of crowding and disrepair. Animal bones from the final occupation layers include dogs, horses, and even the hooves and lower leg bones of cattle -- portions normally discarded, consumed only in times of extreme scarcity. The picture is one of a community slowly starving, clinging to a way of life that the environment no longer supported.

The Norse, crucially, never fully adapted to the Arctic environment. They maintained a European farming way of life in a landscape that was increasingly hostile to it. They raised cattle and sheep instead of hunting seals and whales. They built timber-framed houses instead of the insulated structures used by the Inuit. They wore woven wool instead of the layered caribou-skin garments that the Inuit had perfected over millennia. The archaeological record shows that the Norse diet in Greenland shifted increasingly toward marine resources in the later centuries -- more seal, more fish, fewer cattle products -- but the shift was apparently too slow and too reluctant to prevent the settlements' decline.

The Inuit, meanwhile, thrived. Their technologies were designed for the Arctic; they did not need to adapt a temperate-climate way of life to polar conditions. When the climate cooled, the Inuit adjusted: they shifted hunting strategies, moved to better locations, and relied on their deep knowledge of ice conditions, animal behavior, and seasonal cycles. The Norse disappeared from Greenland. The Inuit are still there.

The irony is pointed. The Norse, with their iron tools, their written language, their Christian churches, and their connection to a broader European civilization, survived in Greenland for roughly four centuries. The Inuit, with their bone and ivory tools, their oral traditions, and their isolation from the wider world, have survived in the same environment for over eight hundred years and counting. The Norse failure in Greenland is a reminder that technological sophistication is not the same thing as ecological fitness. The technology that matters is the technology that works where you are.
