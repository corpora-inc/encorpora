# A Note to the Reader

At the top of the world, where the sun vanishes for months and winter temperatures plunge below minus fifty degrees Celsius, human beings have lived for over four thousand years. They built cities. They carved art of extraordinary beauty. They invented technologies so perfectly suited to their environment that modern engineers study them with admiration. And they left behind mysteries that are still being unraveled.

This volume tells three intertwined stories. The first is the story of the Sivullirmiut (see-VOOL-leer-mee-oot) -- the "first people" -- who crossed from Siberia to the Americas roughly four thousand years ago and spread across the Arctic from Alaska to Greenland in a migration that left its traces in tiny, exquisitely made stone tools. The second is the story of the Dorset (also called Tuniit, TOO-nee-eet), who survived three thousand years in the Arctic and then vanished. The third is the story of Ipiutak (IP-ee-oo-tahk), a city at the edge of the Arctic Ocean where the dead were given masks of ivory with eyes of jet, and where the art on the burial goods echoes cultures thousands of kilometers away across the Bering Strait.

These stories converge in one of the great questions of Arctic archaeology: what happened when the ancestors of the modern Inuit swept from Alaska to Greenland in one of the fastest migrations in human history -- and what became of the people who were already there?
