# Part One: The First Wave

## Chapter 1 — The Sivullirmiut: "First People"

The Arctic is the coldest inhabited region on Earth. Winter temperatures in the Canadian Arctic Archipelago routinely drop below minus forty degrees Celsius -- a temperature at which exposed skin freezes in minutes and exhaled breath forms crystals that drift and fall like snow. The sea ice extends for hundreds of kilometers in every direction, a white plain broken only by pressure ridges where tectonic forces have buckled the frozen surface into jagged walls. The sun disappears below the horizon in November and does not return until February. During the darkest weeks, the only natural light comes from the moon, the stars, and the shimmering curtains of the aurora borealis.

In summer, the transformation is dramatic. The sun circles the sky for twenty-four hours without setting. Temperatures can reach fifteen degrees Celsius -- warm enough for mosquitoes to emerge in clouds so dense they can suffocate caribou calves. The sea ice breaks apart, opening channels and leads of dark water where seals surface to breathe and whales migrate through the passages. The tundra, frozen solid for nine months, erupts in a brief, intense bloom of wildflowers, grasses, and lichens. Millions of migratory birds arrive -- snow geese, eider ducks, Arctic terns -- to breed and raise their young in the few weeks of warmth. The Arctic summer is a season of frantic abundance, a brief window in which all life -- human, animal, and plant -- must accomplish in weeks what temperate environments allow months to do.

![Arctic landscape showing summer tundra with distant sea ice](../images/placeholders/01-arctic-landscape.png)

Roughly four thousand years ago -- around 2000 BCE -- the first human beings moved into this environment from the west. They came across the Bering Strait from Siberia, following a route that their distant ancestors had used to enter the Americas thousands of years earlier. But these newcomers were different from the peoples already living in the Americas. Genetic analysis -- particularly the groundbreaking Saqqaq (SAH-kahk) genome study of 2010 -- has revealed that the first Arctic peoples were genetically distinct from both modern Inuit and from the Native American populations who had entered the Americas via Beringia during the last Ice Age. They represented a separate migration from Siberia, a distinct wave of human movement into the New World.

The archaeologists call their toolkit the Arctic Small Tool tradition -- one of the least poetic names in archaeology, but one that captures the essential fact about these people. They made tiny, precisely crafted stone tools: microblades (thin slivers of stone used as cutting edges, sometimes set into bone or antler handles), small triangular projectile points, miniature scrapers, and delicate burins (pointed tools for engraving bone and antler). The tools are small enough to fit in the palm of a hand, and their craftsmanship is extraordinary -- the flaking is controlled, symmetrical, and consistent, the work of people who had mastered the art of shaping stone to a degree that impresses modern knappers.

The Inuit word for these earliest people is *Sivullirmiut* -- "the first inhabitants" or "the very first people." The term is used loosely; the Inuit recognize that their own ancestors were not the first to live in the Arctic, and the Sivullirmiut represent the oldest layer of human memory in the region. In archaeological terms, the Sivullirmiut correspond to the Paleo-Eskimo cultures -- a broad category that includes the Arctic Small Tool tradition (also called the Denbigh Flint Complex in Alaska, after a site near Cape Denbigh on Norton Sound), the Pre-Dorset culture, and the Saqqaq culture of Greenland.

The spread of the Arctic Small Tool tradition was remarkably rapid. Within a few centuries of appearing in Alaska, the toolkit had spread across the entire North American Arctic -- from the Bering Strait to the northern coast of Greenland, a distance of over six thousand kilometers. The speed of this expansion implies either a single migrating population moving quickly across a landscape with few or no prior inhabitants, or the rapid adoption of a superior toolkit by existing populations. Most scholars favor the first interpretation: a relatively small founding population moving east along the Arctic coast, exploiting the rich marine and terrestrial resources of the tundra environment, and encountering very little competition for the land.

The people who carried the Arctic Small Tool tradition lived in small, mobile groups -- perhaps fifteen to thirty individuals -- following the seasonal movements of caribou, muskox, seals, and fish. Their campsites are typically small scatters of stone tools and fire-cracked rock, the remains of tent rings (circles of stones used to weigh down skin tents), and occasional middens (refuse heaps) containing animal bones. The sites are modest in scale: these were people who traveled light, carrying their essential tools and possessions from camp to camp as the seasons dictated.

They hunted with bows and arrows. This is significant because, as will become clear later in this volume, the people who succeeded them -- the Dorset -- appear to have abandoned or lost bow technology. The Arctic Small Tool people also used toggling harpoons (harpoon heads designed to rotate and lock under the skin of a marine mammal after penetration, preventing the prey from pulling free), indicating that they hunted seals at their breathing holes in the winter ice. The combination of bow-and-arrow technology for terrestrial hunting (caribou, muskox) and harpoon technology for marine hunting (seals) gave them a versatile subsistence strategy that allowed exploitation of both the tundra and the sea ice.

Their shelters were skin tents in summer and semi-subterranean houses in winter -- shallow pits dug into the ground and roofed with driftwood, whale bone, and skins, with a central hearth for warmth and cooking. The houses were small, typically three to four meters across, designed for a single family. Heat was provided by burning driftwood, animal fat (seal blubber, caribou fat), or, in treeless regions, moss and heather. The challenge of staying warm in the Arctic winter was the fundamental problem of Arctic life, and the solutions these early peoples developed -- insulated shelters, layered clothing, efficient hearths -- were the foundation of all subsequent Arctic adaptations.

The seasonal round -- the annual cycle of movement between resource locations -- structured every aspect of Paleo-Eskimo life. In spring, the people gathered at the floe edge (the boundary between fast ice and open water) where seals hauled out to bask in the strengthening sun. In summer, they moved inland to intercept caribou at river crossings, fishing in lakes and streams along the way. In autumn, they cached food for the winter -- dried caribou meat, rendered fat, dried fish -- in stone-lined pits dug into the permafrost, which served as natural freezers. In winter, they returned to the coast, where they hunted ringed seals at breathing holes in the sea ice. This seasonal rhythm, dictated by the movements of animals and the availability of resources, was the organizing principle of Arctic life for over four thousand years.

The Independence I culture -- a variant of the Arctic Small Tool tradition found in the extreme High Arctic of Greenland and Ellesmere Island -- provides a vivid picture of life at the absolute edge of human habitation. Named after Independence Fjord in northeastern Greenland, where it was first identified by the Danish archaeologist Eigil Knuth (AY-gill KNOOTH) in the 1940s, the Independence I culture represents human occupation at latitudes above 80 degrees north -- the northernmost point at which human beings have ever lived on a sustained basis. The sites are tiny: a handful of tent rings, a scatter of tools, the fire-cracked rocks of a hearth. The people who left these traces lived in one of the most extreme environments on Earth -- a place where winter temperatures routinely drop below minus 50 degrees Celsius, where the sun is absent for four months of the year, and where the nearest tree is over two thousand kilometers to the south. That they survived at all is remarkable. That they left behind finely crafted stone tools of elegant workmanship is extraordinary.

The Saqqaq culture of Greenland, a regional variant of the Arctic Small Tool tradition, produced the most famous single artifact in Arctic archaeology. In 2010, a team led by Maanasa Raghavan (mah-NAH-sah RAH-gah-vahn) at the University of Copenhagen published the complete genome of a Saqqaq individual -- a man who had lived in Greenland approximately four thousand years ago, whose hair had been preserved in the permafrost at a site called Qeqertasussuk (keh-KER-tah-soo-sook) on the west coast of Greenland.

![The Saqqaq man's preserved hair tuft from Qeqertasussuk, Greenland](../images/placeholders/02-saqqaq-hair-sample.png)

The Saqqaq genome was the first ancient human genome ever sequenced -- a milestone in the history of genetics. The results were startling. The Saqqaq man was genetically most closely related to modern populations in eastern Siberia -- the Chukchi (CHOOK-chee) and Koryak (KOR-yak) peoples of the Russian Far East. He was genetically distinct from modern Inuit, who are descendants of the later Thule migration. He was also genetically distinct from all known Native American populations. The implications were clear: the first people to colonize the Arctic represented a separate migration from Siberia, unrelated to the earlier Paleo-Indian migrations that had populated the rest of the Americas.

The Saqqaq man had dark skin, brown eyes, and thick, dark hair. He was genetically predisposed to dry earwax (a trait common in East Asian populations), male-pattern baldness, and a stocky build. He was blood type A-positive. The hair sample from which the genome was extracted was a small tuft, perhaps five centimeters long, found in a layer of well-preserved organic material at the site. From this single tuft of four-thousand-year-old hair, scientists reconstructed the genetic portrait of an entire population -- a people who had crossed from Siberia to the Americas in a migration distinct from the one that produced the First Peoples of the Americas.

A 2014 study by Raghavan and colleagues extended this genetic picture dramatically. By analyzing ancient DNA from dozens of Paleo-Eskimo remains across the Arctic -- from Alaska to Greenland, spanning over three thousand years -- the team demonstrated that all Paleo-Eskimo populations (including the Saqqaq and the later Dorset people) belonged to a single genetic lineage, distinct from both Inuit and Native Americans. The Paleo-Eskimo represented a single migration that persisted in the Arctic for over four thousand years -- from roughly 3000 BCE to as late as 1300 CE -- before being replaced by the ancestors of the modern Inuit.

The word "replaced" is significant. The 2014 study found almost no evidence of genetic mixing between the Paleo-Eskimo and the Inuit. When the Thule (the ancestors of modern Inuit) expanded across the Arctic starting around 1000 CE, the Paleo-Eskimo populations did not intermarry with them in any significant way. They simply disappeared. The nature of that disappearance -- displacement, absorption, conflict, or avoidance -- is one of the central mysteries of Arctic archaeology.


## Chapter 2 — The Dorset: The Giants Who Vanished

The Dorset culture emerged around 500 BCE from the earlier Pre-Dorset tradition, in a transition that archaeologists can trace through changes in toolkit, housing, and subsistence strategy. The Pre-Dorset people -- descendants of the original Arctic Small Tool tradition migrants -- had lived in the Arctic for roughly fifteen hundred years by this point, adapting to the region's cycles of warmth and cold. Around 500 BCE, the climate entered a cooling phase. Sea ice expanded. Caribou migration routes shifted. The challenges of Arctic life intensified.

The Dorset response to these challenges was remarkable. They developed new technologies specifically adapted to the colder conditions: the snow house (what is commonly called an igloo, from the Inuit word *iglu*, "house"), soapstone lamps for burning seal oil (providing both heat and light in the long dark winter), ice creepers (bone or ivory spikes attached to footwear for traction on ice), and specialized ice-hunting equipment. They became supremely efficient hunters of ringed seals -- the most important prey animal in the central and eastern Arctic, a species that maintains breathing holes in the sea ice throughout the winter.

The Dorset hunting technique for ringed seals was patience itself. A hunter would locate a seal's breathing hole -- a narrow opening in the ice, perhaps fifteen centimeters across, maintained by the seal's regular visits to breathe -- and then wait. Motionless. In temperatures of minus thirty or minus forty degrees. For hours. Sometimes for an entire day. When the seal surfaced to breathe, the hunter struck with a harpoon through the narrow hole. The technique required extraordinary endurance, intimate knowledge of seal behavior, and clothing so effective that a human being could remain motionless in extreme cold for hours without losing the ability to strike quickly when the moment came.

The Dorset name comes from Cape Dorset (now called Kinngait, KING-ait) on Baffin Island, in the Canadian territory of Nunavut (NOO-nah-voot), where the archaeologist Diamond Jenness first identified the culture in the 1920s. Jenness recognized that the artifacts he was examining -- distinctive triangular harpoon heads, peculiar ground-stone tools, small carvings -- were different from anything produced by the modern Inuit. He proposed that they belonged to a distinct, earlier culture, and he was right.

The Inuit know the Dorset by another name: the Tuniit (TOO-nee-eet), also spelled Tunit or Tunnit. In Inuit oral tradition, the Tuniit were different from the Inuit: taller, stronger, and -- a recurring detail in multiple independently recorded traditions -- shy. They are described as gentle giants who fled rather than fought when the Inuit ancestors arrived. One widely recorded tradition describes the Tuniit as so strong that a single Tuniit man could haul a walrus carcass that would require a team of Inuit hunters to move. Another describes them as easily frightened, running away at the approach of strangers. A third describes Tuniit women as having such long, loose clothing that it trailed on the ground behind them.

These oral traditions are consistent enough across widely separated Inuit communities -- from Baffin Island to northern Greenland -- to suggest that they preserve genuine historical memory of a real people. The descriptions of the Tuniit as physically larger than the Inuit may reflect actual physical differences, or they may be a common feature of folk memory about predecessor populations (many cultures describe earlier inhabitants as giants). The description of the Tuniit as shy or easily displaced is more suggestive: it implies that the encounter between the Thule (Inuit ancestors) and the Dorset was characterized by avoidance rather than warfare, though this interpretation is debated.

![Dorset ivory miniature carving of a polar bear](../images/placeholders/03-dorset-ivory-carving.png)

The most extraordinary legacy of the Dorset is their art. Dorset carvers produced miniature sculptures in ivory, bone, antler, and soapstone that are among the finest small-scale artworks ever created by any culture. The carvings depict polar bears, caribou, seals, walruses, birds, and human figures -- all rendered with a naturalism and expressive power that is immediately striking to modern viewers. Dorset polar bear carvings, in particular, are masterpieces of animal sculpture: the bears are shown in motion, swimming, walking, rising on their hind legs, the musculature and posture captured with a precision that suggests intimate, daily observation of the animal.

The human figures in Dorset art are often depicted in what appears to be a state of transformation -- half-human, half-animal forms that suggest shamanic practices. Faces are sometimes shown with closed eyes, perhaps representing trance states. Some figures have skeletal markings incised on their surfaces -- x-ray style depictions showing the bones beneath the skin -- a feature also found in shamanic art from Siberia and other circumpolar regions. The art suggests a rich spiritual life organized around shamanic practices: the shaman as mediator between the human world and the animal world, between the living and the dead, between the visible and the invisible.

The Dorset also carved small, flat objects called "flying bear" or "swimming bear" figures -- polar bears depicted with outstretched limbs, as if flying or floating. These may represent a spiritual concept of the bear as a being that moves between worlds -- between land and sea, between the physical and the spiritual. The polar bear was almost certainly a central figure in Dorset spiritual life, as it remains in Inuit culture today: the most powerful and dangerous animal in the Arctic, capable of living on land and ice and in the water, a being that bridges environments in a way that humans cannot.

One of the most remarkable Dorset carvings is a wooden mask found at the Button Point site on Bylot Island, Nunavut. The mask is carved from driftwood, roughly life-sized, with an asymmetrical face -- one side smooth and rounded, the other angular and distorted. The asymmetry has been interpreted as representing a shamanic concept of dual identity: the human and the spirit, the visible and the invisible, the ordinary and the transformed. The mask's eye holes are narrow slits, and the mouth is a thin line, giving the face an expression that is simultaneously calm and unsettling. It is one of the oldest known masks from the Arctic, dating to roughly 500-1000 CE, and it suggests a ceremonial life of considerable complexity -- rituals in which masked performers enacted spiritual narratives, mediated between the human and the animal world, or embodied supernatural beings.

The Dorset also produced larger objects of uncertain function: carved driftwood wands, sometimes over a meter long, decorated with rows of carved faces; soapstone masks; and assemblages of small carved objects found together in what appear to be shamanic kits -- collections of carved animals, human figures, and geometric shapes bundled together, perhaps the working tools of a spiritual practitioner. Robert McGhee, the foremost scholar of Arctic archaeology, has described the Dorset as a people whose spiritual life -- as revealed through their art -- was more elaborate and more central to their culture than that of any other Arctic people. The art is not decorative; it is functional, in the sense that it served purposes within a spiritual system that governed the Dorset's relationship to the animals they hunted, the land they inhabited, and the forces they believed controlled their world.

The Dorset relationship to the Arctic environment was shaped by a phenomenon that archaeologists call the "Dorset adaptation" -- a suite of technological and behavioral traits specifically designed for life on the sea ice. The Dorset built snow houses (the earliest evidence of igloo construction comes from Dorset sites), used soapstone lamps for heat and light, developed ice creepers for traction, and created specialized tools for ice fishing (bone hooks, composite fishing lines made from sinew). Their entire economy was organized around the sea ice: they lived on it, hunted on it, and traveled across it. In years when the sea ice was extensive and stable, the Dorset thrived. In years when it was thin or unstable, they struggled.

This dependence on sea ice made the Dorset vulnerable to climate change in a way that the later Thule -- who had boats capable of open-water travel -- were not. During the Medieval Warm Period (roughly 900-1300 CE), reduced sea ice may have disrupted the Dorset subsistence strategy at the same time that the expanding Thule were arriving from the west. The combination of environmental stress and competition from a technologically superior culture may have been the double blow that ended the Dorset's three-thousand-year tenure in the Arctic.

What the Dorset did not have is as significant as what they did. They had no dogs. In the Arctic, dogs are essential for transportation (pulling sleds), hunting (locating seal breathing holes, tracking bears), and protection (warning of approaching predators). The absence of dogs in the Dorset archaeological record is puzzling. The earlier Pre-Dorset people may or may not have had dogs (the evidence is ambiguous), but the Dorset clearly did not. The absence of dogs meant that the Dorset transported all their possessions by human effort -- on their backs, or dragged on hand-pulled sleds.

The Dorset also had no bows and arrows. The earlier Arctic Small Tool tradition people used bows, and the later Thule used bows. The Dorset, in between, used only lances and harpoons thrown by hand. The loss of bow technology is one of the most discussed puzzles in Arctic archaeology. Bows are useful for hunting caribou (allowing a hunter to kill at a distance) and for warfare (giving an obvious advantage over opponents armed only with hand-thrown weapons). How did the Dorset lose this technology? One hypothesis is that the Dorset's increasing specialization in sea-ice hunting -- where the prey (ringed seals) surfaces in a small hole at close range -- made bows unnecessary. Harpoons, not arrows, are the weapon of choice for hunting seals at breathing holes. If the Dorset economy became focused on sea-ice seal hunting to the exclusion of other subsistence strategies, the bow may have fallen out of use simply because there was no occasion to use it.

Another hypothesis is that the loss of the bow was connected to population decline and isolation. Technologies can be lost when the population that maintains them becomes too small or too isolated to sustain the knowledge. Making a functional composite bow -- the type used in the Arctic, constructed from wood, antler, and sinew laminated together -- requires specialized knowledge, specific materials, and practice. If a community lost its bow-makers (through death, accident, or emigration) and had no contact with other bow-using communities, the technology could disappear within a generation.

The Dorset also lacked boats capable of open-water travel. They had no umiaks (large open boats capable of carrying a dozen people and used for whale hunting) and apparently no kayaks. This is a severe limitation in an environment where whales are the largest and most calorie-rich prey available, and where movement between islands requires crossing open water. The Dorset could hunt only what they could reach from the sea ice or from the shore -- seals, walruses (occasionally, and dangerously, from the ice edge), fish, caribou, muskox, and birds. They could not hunt bowhead whales, which can provide tons of meat, blubber, and oil from a single animal and which sustained the later Thule economy.

The Dorset persisted in the Arctic for roughly three thousand years -- from about 500 BCE to approximately 1300-1500 CE. During this time, they spread across an enormous range: from the coast of Labrador in the south to the northern tip of Ellesmere Island in the north, from the coasts of Hudson Bay in the west to the eastern coast of Greenland in the east. At their maximum extent, the Dorset occupied a territory spanning thousands of kilometers of Arctic coastline. Their population was never large -- perhaps a few thousand individuals at any one time, scattered in small groups across a vast landscape -- but their persistence across three millennia in one of Earth's harshest environments is a testament to extraordinary adaptability.

The end of the Dorset coincided with the arrival of the Thule -- the ancestors of the modern Inuit -- who swept across the Arctic from Alaska starting around 1000-1200 CE. The Thule had everything the Dorset lacked: dogs, bows, kayaks, umiaks, and the ability to hunt bowhead whales. The technological gap between the two cultures was enormous. The Thule could move faster (with dog sleds), hunt more effectively (with bows for caribou, harpoons from boats for whales), transport more goods (with dog teams and large boats), and sustain larger populations (with whale-based food supplies).

The fate of the Dorset after the Thule arrival is the central mystery of this volume. The genetic evidence from the 2014 Raghavan study is unambiguous: the Dorset and the Thule did not interbreed in any significant way. The Dorset genetic lineage simply ended. The oral traditions of the Inuit describe the Tuniit as fleeing rather than fighting. The archaeological record shows Dorset sites being abandoned and Thule sites appearing in the same regions, sometimes within decades of each other. In a few cases, Dorset and Thule artifacts are found in the same archaeological layers, suggesting a brief period of overlap -- but whether that overlap involved contact, trade, conflict, or simple coexistence in adjacent areas is unclear.

The Dorset survived longest in the most remote corners of the Arctic. The last known Dorset sites are on Southampton Island in Hudson Bay, on the Belcher Islands, and in northern Labrador -- peripheral areas, distant from the main routes of the Thule expansion. By roughly 1500 CE, the Dorset had disappeared from the archaeological record entirely. A people who had survived three thousand years in the Arctic -- who had developed an art tradition of haunting beauty, who had mastered the techniques of survival in one of the planet's most demanding environments -- simply vanished. Their genes are gone. Their art remains.
