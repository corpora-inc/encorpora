# What We Still Don't Know

**What happened to the Dorset?** The genetic evidence is clear: the Dorset did not interbreed with the Thule in any significant way. They disappeared as a distinct population within a few centuries of the Thule expansion. Were they displaced into increasingly marginal territories until their populations became unsustainable? Were they killed in conflicts that left no archaeological trace? Did they avoid contact so completely that they simply dwindled in isolation? The Inuit oral traditions describe the Tuniit as shy and easily frightened, but oral tradition is not a forensic record. The question remains open.

**Who were the Ipiutak people?** A city of several thousand at the edge of the Arctic Ocean, with burial art that echoes cultures across the Bering Strait. The Ipiutak people lived at one of the best whale-hunting locations in the Arctic but apparently did not hunt whales. Their art style has no clear predecessor in the Arctic archaeological record. Their relationship to the later cultures at Point Hope is unclear. The elaborate burial goods suggest social stratification, but the nature of the social hierarchy is unknown.

**What is the connection between Ipiutak art and the Scytho-Siberian animal style?** The curvilinear designs, the emphasis on animal transformation, the flowing lines and spirals -- the similarities are real. The mechanisms of transmission are debated. Direct contact across the Bering Strait? A shared circumpolar artistic tradition? Parallel development from a common ancestor? The question has implications for understanding the degree of cultural connectedness across the pre-modern Arctic.

**Why did the Dorset lose bow-and-arrow technology?** The Dorset's ancestors used bows. The Thule, who replaced the Dorset, used bows. The Dorset, in the middle, did not. The loss of a useful technology is a rare and puzzling event in human history. Increasing specialization in sea-ice seal hunting may have made bows unnecessary, but the loss left the Dorset at a severe disadvantage when the bow-using Thule arrived.

**How was the Thule expansion organized?** Was it a deliberate migration, a gradual population spread, or something in between? The speed -- five thousand kilometers in one to two centuries -- suggests a directed process, but the archaeological evidence is consistent with a wave-of-advance model in which population growth at the frontier drives continuous expansion. The social and political mechanisms that organized the expansion are unknown.

**What was the relationship between the Norse and the Thule in Greenland?** Three centuries of coexistence, almost no evidence of sustained interaction. The Norse sagas mention the *skraelingar* in passing. Norse artifacts appear in Thule sites. But the degree and nature of the contact remains poorly understood.

**How much of the Arctic archaeological record has been lost to climate change?** Permafrost thaw is accelerating. Coastal erosion is consuming sites. Organic materials preserved for millennia are decaying within years of exposure. The full extent of what has already been lost -- and what will be lost in the coming decades -- is unknown.

These questions define the frontier of Arctic archaeology. The Arctic is one of the last great archaeological unknowns -- a vast territory, sparsely studied, where every season of fieldwork yields surprises.


# Key Names and Terms

**Arctic Small Tool tradition** -- The earliest archaeological culture in the North American Arctic (~2000-800 BCE); characterized by tiny, finely made stone tools.

**Avataq** (AH-vah-tahk) -- Inflated sealskin float attached to a harpoon line; used in whale hunting to create drag and prevent a harpooned whale from diving.

**Baidarka** (by-DAR-kah) -- The kayak of the Aleut people; features a distinctive split bow.

**Caribou** -- A large deer species (Rangifer tarandus) of the Arctic and subarctic; a primary food source for Arctic peoples.

**Denbigh Flint Complex** -- An early Alaskan variant of the Arctic Small Tool tradition; named for a site near Cape Denbigh on Norton Sound.

**Dorset** -- An Arctic culture (~500 BCE-1500 CE) known for miniature ivory carvings and specialized sea-ice hunting techniques; succeeded the Pre-Dorset and was displaced by the Thule.

**Friesen, T. Max** -- Canadian archaeologist at the University of Toronto; specialist in western Arctic archaeology and the Dorset-Thule transition.

**Iglu** (IG-loo) -- Inuktitut word meaning "house"; commonly refers to the snow house (snow block dome).

**Inuit** (IH-noo-eet) -- The indigenous people of the Arctic, from Alaska to Greenland; descendants of the Thule culture. Singular: Inuk.

**Inuktitut** (ih-NOOK-tih-toot) -- The language of the Inuit; a family of dialects spoken across the Arctic.

**Inuksuk** (ih-NOOK-sook; plural inuksuit, ih-NOOK-soo-eet) -- Stone figure "in the likeness of a human"; used as navigation markers, caribou-drive guides, and sacred markers across the Arctic.

**Inupiaq** (ih-NOO-pee-ahk) -- The language and people of northwestern Alaska; dialect of the Inuit language family.

**Inuvialuit** (ih-NOO-vee-ah-loo-eet) -- The Inuit people of the western Canadian Arctic (Mackenzie Delta and Beaufort Sea coast).

**Ipiutak** (IP-ee-oo-tahk) -- A large ancient settlement at Point Hope, Alaska (~100 BCE-800 CE); known for elaborate ivory burial goods and death masks.

**Kamik** (KAH-mik) -- A traditional sealskin boot worn by Arctic peoples.

**Kayak** (qajaq, KAH-yahk) -- A small, enclosed, one-person boat made of a skin-covered frame; designed for hunting.

**Larsen, Helge** (HEL-geh LAR-sen) -- Danish archaeologist; co-discoverer of Ipiutak with Froelich Rainey.

**Mason, Owen** -- American archaeologist; specialist in Ipiutak and Norton tradition archaeology.

**McGhee, Robert** -- Canadian archaeologist; author of *Ancient People of the Arctic* and *The Last Imaginary Place*.

**Medieval Warm Period** -- A period of relatively warm climate (~900-1300 CE) that may have facilitated the Thule expansion.

**Norton tradition** -- An Alaskan archaeological culture (~500 BCE-1000 CE) that overlapped in time and space with Ipiutak; distinguished by pottery use.

**Paleo-Eskimo** -- Archaeological term for the pre-Thule Arctic peoples, including the Arctic Small Tool tradition, Pre-Dorset, Dorset, and Saqqaq cultures.

**Pre-Dorset** -- A Paleo-Eskimo culture (~2000-500 BCE) ancestral to the Dorset; part of the Arctic Small Tool tradition.

**Qamutiik** (kah-MOO-teek) -- A traditional Inuit sled pulled by a dog team.

**Qulliq** (KOO-lik) -- A crescent-shaped soapstone lamp fueled by seal oil; the primary source of heat and light in traditional Arctic dwellings.

**Raghavan, Maanasa** (mah-NAH-sah RAH-gah-vahn) -- Geneticist; led the 2010 Saqqaq genome study and the 2014 Paleo-Eskimo population genetics study.

**Rainey, Froelich** -- American archaeologist; co-discoverer of Ipiutak with Helge Larsen.

**Ringed seal** -- The most common seal species in the Arctic (Pusa hispida); a primary prey animal for Arctic peoples.

**Saqqaq** (SAH-kahk) -- A Paleo-Eskimo culture of Greenland (~2500-800 BCE); the source of the first ancient human genome ever sequenced.

**Schledermann, Peter** -- Danish-Canadian archaeologist; specialist in Norse-Inuit contact in the High Arctic.

**Scytho-Siberian animal style** -- The artistic tradition of the Eurasian steppe nomads (~700 BCE-300 CE); characterized by dynamic animal forms and curvilinear designs.

**Sivullirmiut** (see-VOOL-leer-mee-oot) -- Inuktitut term meaning "the first people" or "the very first inhabitants."

**Thule** (TOO-lee) -- The direct ancestors of the modern Inuit; developed whale-hunting technology in Alaska and expanded across the Arctic ~1000-1300 CE.

**Tikigaq** (tih-KEE-gahk) -- The Inupiaq name for Point Hope, Alaska.

**Toggling harpoon** -- A harpoon with a detachable head that rotates after penetration, locking under the skin; the primary weapon for marine mammal hunting.

**Tuilik** (TOO-ee-lik) -- A waterproof kayaking jacket with a skirt that seals to the kayak cockpit rim.

**Tuktu** (TOOK-too) -- Inuktitut word for caribou.

**Tuniit** (TOO-nee-eet) -- Inuit name for the Dorset people; described in oral traditions as taller and stronger than the Inuit.

**Umiak** (OO-mee-ahk) -- A large, open skin boat used for whale hunting and transportation; can carry a crew of 8-12.

**Umialik** (oo-mee-AH-lik) -- A whaling captain; literally "the one who has an umiak."

**Utqiagvik** (oot-KAH-vik) -- The Inupiaq name for the city formerly known as Barrow, Alaska; the northernmost city in the United States.


# Further Reading

**For the general reader:**

- McGhee, Robert. *The Last Imaginary Place: A Human History of the Arctic World*. Oxford University Press, 2005. -- The best single-volume introduction to Arctic human history, beautifully written.

- McGhee, Robert. *Ancient People of the Arctic*. University of British Columbia Press, 1996. -- Accessible survey of Arctic archaeology with excellent illustrations.

- Lopez, Barry. *Arctic Dreams: Imagination and Desire in a Northern Landscape*. Scribner, 1986. -- A lyrical exploration of the Arctic environment and the human relationship to it.

**For the archaeology:**

- Friesen, T. Max, and Owen K. Mason, eds. *The Oxford Handbook of the Prehistoric Arctic*. Oxford University Press, 2016. -- Comprehensive academic reference covering all periods and regions of Arctic archaeology.

- Rainey, Froelich G. "Ipiutak and the Arctic Whale Hunting Culture." *Anthropological Papers of the American Museum of Natural History* 42 (1947). -- The original Ipiutak excavation report.

- Raghavan, Maanasa, et al. "Upper Palaeolithic Siberian Genome Reveals Dual Ancestry of Native Americans." *Nature* 505 (2014): 87-91. -- The landmark genetic study of Paleo-Eskimo populations.

**For the Norse context:**

- Seaver, Kirsten A. *The Frozen Echo: Greenland and the Exploration of North America, ca. A.D. 1000-1500*. Stanford University Press, 1996. -- Norse Greenland and its connections to the broader North Atlantic world.

- Schledermann, Peter. *Voices in Stone: A Personal Journey into the Arctic Past*. Komatik Series, 1996. -- Schledermann's own account of his Norse-Thule contact investigations.
