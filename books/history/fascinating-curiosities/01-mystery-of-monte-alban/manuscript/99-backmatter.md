# What We Still Don't Know

The honest list is long. Here are the questions that remain genuinely open — not because scholars have been lazy, but because the evidence has not yet yielded answers.

**What did the Zapotecs call Monte Albán?** The names Danipaguache and Danibaan come from post-abandonment oral tradition. The original name — the one used during the city's twelve centuries of active life — has not been recovered from the inscriptions.

**Why was the mountaintop chosen?** Defensive alliance, religious movement, political consolidation, or something else? The synoikism hypothesis explains the *what* but not fully the *why*.

**What do the Danzantes say?** The glyphs carved beside the figures include information beyond the calendar dates and names we can partially read. The connecting text — the verbs, descriptions, and narrative — remains undeciphered.

**What is Building J?** Observatory, victory monument, archive, or something we have not thought of? Its unique shape and orientation have never been fully explained.

**Can the script be deciphered?** The logo-syllabic model is well-established, but the phonetic values of most syllabic signs remain unknown. A bilingual text — a Zapotec Rosetta Stone — has never been found.

**Why did the writing system die before the city was abandoned?** The devastating collapse of scribal conventions during Period IIIB preceded the final abandonment by at least a century. Something broke the chain of scribal transmission while the city was still occupied.

**Why did the people leave?** Environmental exhaustion, trade collapse, political fragmentation, internal pressure — all plausible, none proven individually, some combination almost certain.

These are not failures. They are the frontier. Every one of these questions is actively being investigated. Every one of them could yield a breakthrough tomorrow, or remain open for another generation. The mystery is alive.


# Key Names and Terms

**Atzompa** (aht-SOHM-pah) — A hilltop site one kilometer northwest of Monte Albán, serving as a satellite community. A major stucco frieze with Zapotec glyphs was discovered here in 2018.

**Building J** — The uniquely arrow-shaped structure near the center of Monte Albán's Grand Plaza, oriented differently from all other buildings. Contains over forty carved conquest slabs.

**Caso, Alfonso** (1896–1970) — Mexican archaeologist who began systematic excavation of Monte Albán in 1931, discovered Tomb 7 in 1932, and laid the foundation for Zapotec and Mixtec epigraphy.

**Codex** (plural: codices) — Painted manuscripts on deerskin or bark paper, folded accordion-style. Several Mixtec codices survive; no Zapotec codices from the Classic period are known to exist.

**Conquest slabs** — Carved stone panels on Building J depicting place glyphs with inverted human heads, interpreted as records of military conquests.

**Danzantes** (dahn-SAHN-tes) — "The Dancers." Over three hundred carved stone figures at Monte Albán, now understood to depict sacrificed war captives. Among the oldest carvings at the site.

**Danibaan** / **Danipaguache** (dah-nee-pah-GWAH-cheh) — Zapotec names for Monte Albán, meaning roughly "Sacred Hill" and "Sacred Mountain." These are later traditional names; the original is unknown.

**Flannery, Kent** — American archaeologist, longtime collaborator of Joyce Marcus, co-author of *Zapotec Civilization* (1996).

**INAH** (ee-NAH) — Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia. Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History, responsible for archaeological research and site protection.

**Logo-syllabic** — A writing system that combines logograms (symbols for whole words) with syllabograms (symbols for sounds or syllables). The model proposed by Javier Urcid for the Zapotec script.

**Marcus, Joyce** — American archaeologist at the University of Michigan, pioneer of Zapotec epigraphy. Proved the script encoded the Zapotec language through internal linguistic evidence.

**Mesoamerica** — The cultural region encompassing central and southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, and El Salvador, where complex civilizations developed independently before European contact.

**Mitla** (MEET-lah) — Zapotec: *Lyobaa*, "Place of Rest." An important Zapotec religious center forty kilometers east of Monte Albán, famous for its geometric stone mosaics.

**Mixtec** (MEESH-tek) — A highland people from the Mixteca region west of the Valley of Oaxaca, who expanded into the valley after Monte Albán's abandonment and produced brilliant painted codices and metalwork.

**Monte Albán** (MON-teh al-BAHN) — The mountaintop capital of the Zapotec civilization in the Valley of Oaxaca, occupied from approximately 500 BCE to 900 CE.

**Oaxaca** (wah-HAH-kah) — A state in southern Mexico; also the name of its capital city. The Valley of Oaxaca, a Y-shaped highland valley, was the heartland of Zapotec civilization.

**Period classifications** — Monte Albán's history is divided by archaeologists into phases: Period I (500–100 BCE), Period II (100 BCE–200 CE), Period IIIA (200–500 CE), Period IIIB (500–800 CE), Period IV (800–1300 CE), Period V (1300–1521 CE).

**Piye** (PEE-yeh) — The Zapotec name for the 260-day ritual calendar used across Mesoamerica, combining twenty day names with thirteen numbers.

**San José Mogote** (san ho-ZAY mo-GO-teh) — The dominant settlement in the Valley of Oaxaca for roughly a thousand years before Monte Albán's founding. Monument 3, found here, contains one of the earliest known examples of Mesoamerican writing.

**Spencer, Charles & Redmond, Elsa** — Archaeologists at the American Museum of Natural History whose research on Tilcajete revealed resistance to Monte Albán's consolidation of the valley.

**Synoikism** — From Greek *synoikismos*, "a bringing together of households." The process by which dispersed communities consolidate into a single settlement. Used by Marcus and Flannery to describe Monte Albán's founding.

**Teotihuacan** (teh-oh-tee-wah-KAHN) — A massive city in the Basin of Mexico, near present-day Mexico City, that was the dominant power in Mesoamerica from roughly 100 BCE to 550 CE.

**Tomb 7** — A burial chamber on the north slope of Monte Albán, originally Zapotec, reopened and reused by Mixtec elites centuries later. Discovered by Alfonso Caso in 1932, containing spectacular gold, turquoise, and crystal grave goods.

**Urcid, Javier** — Epigrapher who cataloged over 200 Zapotec inscriptions and proposed the logo-syllabic model of the Zapotec script. Author of *Zapotec Hieroglyphic Writing* (2001).

**Zapotec** (ZAH-po-tek) — The indigenous people of the Valley of Oaxaca and surrounding highlands. The Zapotec language is still spoken by over 500,000 people in more than fifty regional variants.


# Further Reading

These are the best starting points for a reader who wants to go deeper. They are listed roughly in order of accessibility, from most general to most specialized.

**For the general reader:**

- Marcus, Joyce, and Kent V. Flannery. *Zapotec Civilization: How Urban Society Evolved in Mexico's Oaxaca Valley*. Thames and Hudson, 1996. — The most comprehensive and readable single account of Zapotec history and archaeology.

- Flannery, Kent V., and Joyce Marcus. *The Creation of Inequality: How Our Prehistoric Ancestors Set the Stage for Monarchy, Slavery, and Empire*. Harvard University Press, 2012. — Not specifically about Monte Albán, but uses it as a key case study in the development of complex societies.

- Coe, Michael D., and Rex Koontz. *Mexico: From the Olmecs to the Aztecs*. Thames and Hudson, 7th edition, 2013. — Excellent overview of all Mesoamerican civilizations, with strong coverage of Oaxaca.

**For the script specifically:**

- Urcid, Javier. *Zapotec Hieroglyphic Writing*. Studies in Pre-Columbian Art and Archaeology, No. 34. Dumbarton Oaks, 2001. — The foundational technical reference. Dense but essential.

- Marcus, Joyce. *Mesoamerican Writing Systems: Propaganda, Myth, and History in Four Ancient Civilizations*. Princeton University Press, 1992. — Comparative study placing the Zapotec script alongside Maya, Mixtec, and Aztec writing.

**For Tomb 7 and the Mixtec connection:**

- Caso, Alfonso. *El Tesoro de Monte Albán* (The Treasure of Monte Albán). INAH, 1969. — Caso's own account of the Tomb 7 discovery and its contents. In Spanish.

**For the broader Mesoamerican context:**

- Houston, Stephen D., ed. *The First Writing: Script Invention as History and Process*. Cambridge University Press, 2004. — Comparative study of the origins of writing worldwide, including Mesoamerica.

- Christenson, Allen J., trans. *Popol Vuh: The Sacred Book of the Maya*. University of Oklahoma Press, 2007. — The K'iche' Maya creation narrative, including the ball game mythology.
