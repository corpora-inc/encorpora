# A Note to the Reader

On August 19, 1953, a thirty-seven-year-old CIA officer sat in a basement in Tehran and listened to the radio. The mobs he had helped organize were in the streets. The tanks he had helped arrange were moving toward the prime minister's residence. By nightfall, the democratically elected government of Iran would be gone.

The officer's name was Kermit Roosevelt Jr. He was the grandson of President Theodore Roosevelt. Four days earlier, his first attempt to overthrow Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh had failed spectacularly -- the plot was exposed, the Shah fled the country, and CIA headquarters in Washington cabled Roosevelt to abort the operation and leave Iran. He ignored the order. He stayed in Tehran, improvised a second plan, and on August 19 he watched it succeed.

This is the story of Operation Ajax -- the CIA's first overthrow of a foreign government. The British intelligence service conceived the idea. The Americans executed it. An Iranian prime minister who had nationalized his country's oil industry was removed from power and replaced by a monarch who would rule with increasing autocracy for the next twenty-six years, until a revolution swept him away in 1979.

The operational details come from a remarkable document: the CIA's own internal history of the coup, written by Donald Wilber, one of the operation's planners. Portions of this history were leaked in 2000, more were declassified in 2013, and further sections became available in 2017. The Wilber document reveals an operation that was equal parts planning and improvisation, confidence and panic. The legend of a smoothly executed intelligence coup dissolves under the weight of the evidence into something messier, more contingent, and more troubling -- a story where the outcome hinged on decisions made under pressure by a handful of people who were uncertain whether their plan would work.

Where the documentary record is clear, we follow it. Where it remains classified or contested -- the British files from Operation Boot are still largely sealed -- we say so. The consequences of what happened in Tehran in August 1953 are still unfolding.
