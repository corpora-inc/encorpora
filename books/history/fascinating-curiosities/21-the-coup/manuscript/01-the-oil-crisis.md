# Part One: The Oil Crisis

## Chapter 1 -- The Nationalization

The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company had operated in Iran for over four decades by 1950, and in all that time, the fundamental terms of the relationship had barely changed. The company extracted Iranian oil, refined it at the world's largest refinery in Abadan, shipped it around the globe, and paid Iran a fraction of the profits. The fraction was calculated by the company. Iran had no access to the books.

![Portrait of Mohammad Mosaddegh](../images/placeholders/02-mosaddegh-portrait.png)

The numbers told the story. In 1950, the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company reported profits of 200 million pounds. Iran received 16 million. The British government collected more in taxes from the company than Iran received in royalties from its own oil. The company employed tens of thousands of Iranians in its Khuzestan operations, but they lived in segregated housing, were barred from senior positions, and earned wages far below those of their British counterparts. The refinery city of Abadan had two worlds: the manicured British residential quarter with swimming pools and tennis courts, and the Iranian workers' district with open sewers.

![The Abadan refinery complex, prize of empires](../images/placeholders/04-abadan-refinery-prize.png)

Iranian resentment of the oil arrangement had been building for decades. The Majles -- the Iranian parliament -- had rejected a supplemental agreement proposed by the company in 1949 that would have modestly increased Iran's share. A parliamentary commission chaired by Mohammad Mosaddegh recommended a more radical solution: nationalization. On March 15, 1951, the Majles voted unanimously to nationalize the oil industry. On April 28, Mosaddegh became prime minister.

Mosaddegh was sixty-nine years old. He came from one of Iran's wealthiest and most aristocratic families. He had studied law in Switzerland and France, served in the Majles since the 1920s, and opposed both the Pahlavi monarchy and foreign control of Iranian resources for his entire political career. He wept easily and publicly, fainted during speeches, and governed from his bed in pajamas -- eccentricities that led Western observers to dismiss him as unstable. The dismissal was a mistake. Mosaddegh was a shrewd parliamentarian, a committed constitutionalist, and a figure of enormous popular support.

Nationalization meant that the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company's assets in Iran -- the wells, the pipelines, the Abadan refinery -- now belonged to the Iranian government. The company's British employees were expelled. A new National Iranian Oil Company would operate the industry. Mosaddegh argued, correctly under international precedent, that a sovereign nation had the right to nationalize industries within its borders, provided it offered compensation. He offered compensation.

Britain refused to accept it. The British government, which held a controlling stake in the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, treated nationalization as theft. Foreign Secretary Herbert Morrison wanted military intervention. The chiefs of staff drew up plans for Operation Buccaneer, a military seizure of Abadan. Prime Minister Clement Attlee, warned by the Americans that an invasion would destabilize the Middle East and play into Soviet hands, held back. Instead, Britain chose economic warfare.

The British embargo was devastating. The Royal Navy patrolled the Persian Gulf, intercepting tankers that attempted to load Iranian oil. British diplomats warned every potential buyer that purchasing nationalized oil would result in legal action. The major oil companies -- the "Seven Sisters" -- closed ranks. Iran's oil exports dropped from 241 million barrels in 1950 to virtually zero. Government revenues collapsed. Mosaddegh's political position, built on the promise that nationalization would bring prosperity, began to erode.

![Map of the British embargo of Iranian oil exports](../images/placeholders/09-british-embargo-map.png)

But the embargo alone could not remove Mosaddegh. He retained majority support in the Majles. His popular base, though fraying under economic pressure, remained formidable. The National Front coalition he led -- a loose alliance of nationalists, liberal democrats, bazaar merchants, and some clerical figures -- held together through 1951 and 1952. Mosaddegh survived a brief political crisis in July 1952 when the Shah attempted to replace him; street protests forced the Shah to back down and reappoint Mosaddegh with expanded powers. The prime minister emerged stronger than before.

Britain needed another approach.


## Chapter 2 -- Operation Boot

The British Secret Intelligence Service -- MI6 -- had maintained a network in Iran since long before the oil crisis. The most important assets were the Rashidian brothers: Assadollah, Qodratollah, and Seyfollah. Wealthy, well-connected, and deeply opposed to Mosaddegh, the Rashidians ran a network of contacts that reached into the military, the clergy, the bazaar, the press, and the parliament. MI6 had been paying them for years.

![The Rashidian brothers network](../images/placeholders/05-rashidian-network.png)

By late 1951, MI6 had begun planning Mosaddegh's overthrow. The operation was codenamed Boot. The plan was straightforward in concept: use the Rashidian network to bribe parliamentarians, subsidize newspapers, organize street demonstrations, and cultivate military officers willing to arrest the prime minister. The Shah's constitutional authority to dismiss the prime minister would provide a legal fig leaf. The Rashidians would arrange the political conditions; a cooperative general would execute the arrest.

There was a problem. In October 1952, Mosaddegh severed diplomatic relations with Britain. The British embassy in Tehran closed. MI6 lost its operational base inside Iran. The Rashidian network remained, but running it from outside the country was difficult. MI6 needed a partner with an embassy in Tehran, officers on the ground, and the resources to mount a major covert operation.

They turned to the Americans.

The CIA had been reluctant. Under President Truman, the American position was that Mosaddegh, whatever his faults, was a legitimate democratic leader, and that British intransigence over the oil dispute was the root cause of the crisis. The State Department attempted to mediate between London and Tehran. The CIA station in Tehran reported that Mosaddegh enjoyed genuine popular support and that the Iranian communist party, the Tudeh, while growing, did not control the government.

The election of Dwight Eisenhower in November 1952 changed everything. Eisenhower and his Secretary of State, John Foster Dulles, viewed the world through the lens of Cold War confrontation. The Dulles brothers -- John Foster at State, Allen at the CIA -- were receptive to the British argument that Mosaddegh's weakness invited Soviet penetration. The Tudeh Party's growing street presence, Soviet diplomatic pressure on Iran, and the economic chaos caused by the British embargo all supported the case. Whether Mosaddegh was a communist was beside the point; what mattered was that his continued rule, in the Dulles calculus, created conditions favorable to communism.

In February 1953, the CIA and MI6 agreed to proceed jointly. The British contributed the Rashidian network, the intelligence picture, and the original operational concept. The Americans would provide fresh resources, operational direction from inside Tehran, and -- critically -- the political will that London could no longer project into Iran on its own. MI6's Operation Boot became the CIA's Operation TPAJAX. The plan was assigned to the CIA's Near East and Africa Division. Allen Dulles approved the budget: roughly one million dollars in covert funds for the operation, a sum worth approximately eleven million in today's money.

![Operation Boot: the British origins of the coup plan](../images/placeholders/12-operation-boot.png)

The operational plan was drafted by Donald Wilber, a CIA officer who was also a scholar of Persian architecture. Wilber's plan, later declassified, laid out the sequence in meticulous detail: a propaganda campaign to undermine Mosaddegh's public support, the bribery of key military and political figures, the use of agents provocateurs to create an atmosphere of chaos, the Shah's issuance of royal decrees dismissing Mosaddegh and appointing a replacement, and the arrest of the prime minister by cooperative military units.

The plan required one element that the CIA could not manufacture: the Shah's cooperation. Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi was constitutionally authorized to dismiss the prime minister, but he was terrified. He feared Mosaddegh's popularity. He feared the Tudeh Party. He feared, above all, failure -- the consequences of a botched coup attempt for a monarch whose hold on his throne was already precarious. Convincing the Shah to sign the decrees would prove to be one of the operation's most difficult challenges.

The man chosen to direct the operation from inside Tehran was Kermit Roosevelt Jr.
