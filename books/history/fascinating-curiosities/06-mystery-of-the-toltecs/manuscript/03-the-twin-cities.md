# Part Three: The Twin Cities

## Chapter 6 — Tula and Chichen Itza

Fifteen hundred kilometers separate Tula, in the highlands of central Mexico, from Chichen Itza, in the limestone lowlands of the Yucatan (yoo-kah-TAHN) Peninsula. Between them lies some of the most diverse and difficult terrain in Mesoamerica: the volcanic highlands, the tropical forests of the Gulf Coast lowlands, the swamps of Tabasco, and the dry karst landscape of northern Yucatan. The two sites occupy different ecosystems, different climate zones, and different cultural worlds. Tula is a highland city in the Nahua cultural tradition. Chichen Itza is a lowland city in the Maya cultural tradition.

And yet the resemblances between them are extraordinary.

Both cities have Atlantean warrior columns -- large-scale stone figures of warriors supporting temple roofs. Tula's Atlantean columns are the most famous examples, but Chichen Itza has its own version: the "Temple of the Warriors" features rows of carved columns depicting armed figures in regalia strikingly similar to Tula's warriors. Both cities have chacmool sculptures -- the reclining figures with offering plates on their stomachs. Both have prominent feathered serpent imagery -- at Chichen Itza, the Pyramid of Kukulkan (koo-kool-KAHN), the Maya name for the Feathered Serpent, is the site's most famous structure, its balustrades carved with serpent heads, its shadow casting a serpentine pattern on the stairway at the spring equinox. Both have colonnaded halls -- large covered spaces supported by columns, used for public gatherings. Both have tzompantli skull racks. Both have ball courts of unusual size -- Chichen Itza's Great Ball Court is the largest in Mesoamerica, over 150 meters long, its walls carved with scenes of decapitation.

The similarities are specific enough to rule out coincidence. These are not general Mesoamerican traits shared by many cities. They are a particular set of architectural and artistic features that appear at Tula and at Chichen Itza, and at very few other sites. Even the details correspond: at both sites, the warrior figures wear nearly identical regalia -- butterfly breastplates, back mirrors, atlatl dart throwers, and sandals with heel guards. The artistic vocabulary is shared down to the level of individual costume elements. The question is: why?

![Comparison of architectural features at Tula and Chichen Itza](../images/placeholders/05-tula-chichen-comparison.png)

Three hypotheses have competed for over a century.

The first, and oldest, is the Toltec invasion hypothesis. In this reading, Toltec warriors from central Mexico -- led, in some versions, by the exiled Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl or his followers -- traveled to the Yucatan and conquered Chichen Itza, imposing their architectural and artistic styles on the Maya city. The Maya sources support this interpretation, at least partially: the *Books of Chilam Balam* (chee-LAHM bah-LAHM), colonial-era Maya texts that record pre-conquest history, describe the arrival of foreigners called the Itza (eet-SAH) at Chichen, and some scholars have identified these Itza with Toltec or central Mexican groups. The Toltec invasion hypothesis dominated scholarship for most of the twentieth century. It provided a neat narrative: the Toltecs conquered the Maya, brought their architecture with them, and left their mark on Yucatan.

The second hypothesis reverses the direction of influence. In this reading, Chichen Itza influenced Tula, not the other way around. Chichen Itza was a larger and wealthier city than Tula during the period of overlap. Its art and architecture are more sophisticated and varied than Tula's. If influence flowed in one direction, it may have flowed from the larger and richer city to the smaller one. Maya merchants, diplomats, or artists may have carried ideas north to central Mexico, where they were adopted and adapted at Tula. The resemblances between the two cities, in this view, reflect Maya influence on the Toltecs, not Toltec influence on the Maya.

The third hypothesis, and the one currently favored by most scholars, is that both cities drew on a shared cultural tradition -- a common set of ideas about warfare, religion, and political authority that circulated across Mesoamerica during the Epiclassic and Early Postclassic periods (roughly 700-1100 CE). This shared tradition was not the invention of either city. It emerged from the political and cultural upheaval that followed Teotihuacan's fall -- a period when new ideas about military power, merchant networks, and the cult of the Feathered Serpent spread across the Mesoamerican world. Tula and Chichen Itza both participated in this wider tradition, and their resemblances reflect their shared participation rather than direct contact or conquest.

The shared-tradition hypothesis does not mean that there was no contact between the two cities. There almost certainly was. The archaeological evidence shows that goods, ideas, and possibly people moved between central Mexico and the Yucatan during this period. Fine Orange pottery -- a distinctive ceramic ware produced on the Gulf Coast -- appears at both Tula and Chichen Itza, suggesting trade connections that linked the two regions. Turquoise, which originates in the American Southwest, has been found at Chichen Itza, indicating long-distance trade networks that stretched from what is now New Mexico through central Mexico to the Yucatan. The connections were real, but they may have been commercial and cultural rather than military and political.

The term "Itza" in Chichen Itza's name adds another layer. The *Books of Chilam Balam* describe a group called the Itza who arrived at the site and established themselves as rulers. Scholars have debated the identity of the Itza for over a century. Were they Toltecs from central Mexico? Chontal Maya merchants from the Gulf Coast who had adopted central Mexican cultural elements? A local Maya faction that used central Mexican symbolism to legitimize their rule? The name "Chichen Itza" itself means "At the Mouth of the Well of the Itza" -- the well being the Sacred Cenote. The Itza were the people who controlled the cenote and its pilgrimage trade, and their identity -- Maya, Mexican, or something in between -- is central to the puzzle.

The chronology complicates the picture further. For decades, scholars assumed that Tula's peak preceded Chichen Itza's -- that the Toltecs built their city first, then conquered the Maya. But revised dating of Chichen Itza's monuments, based on radiocarbon analysis and reevaluation of ceramic sequences, suggests that Chichen Itza's "Toltec-style" architecture may be contemporary with or even earlier than Tula's. If Chichen Itza's warrior columns and feathered serpent imagery date to the ninth century, while Tula's peak is in the tenth and eleventh centuries, then the old invasion hypothesis -- Toltecs imposing their style on the Maya -- becomes untenable. You cannot be influenced by a city that has not yet reached its peak.

The Maya epigrapher David Stuart has contributed an important dimension to the debate. Stuart's work on Maya inscriptions at Chichen Itza shows that the city's rulers used Maya titles and Maya calendar dates, and that their inscriptions are written in Yucatec Maya, not in any central Mexican language. If Toltec conquerors had taken over the city, one would expect some evidence of their language or their political titles. The inscriptions show Maya rulers participating in a wider Mesoamerican cultural tradition, not foreign conquerors imposing an alien culture.

The Pyramid of Kukulkan at Chichen Itza -- also known as El Castillo (ehl kahs-TEE-yoh), "The Castle" -- is the most visually dramatic expression of the Feathered Serpent cult at either site. The pyramid has nine tiers, four stairways with 91 steps each (plus the platform at the top, for a total of 365 -- the number of days in the solar year), and serpent heads at the base of the northern stairway. At the spring and autumn equinoxes, the sun's angle creates a pattern of triangular shadows on the northern stairway that resembles a serpent descending the pyramid -- a phenomenon that draws tens of thousands of visitors each year.

The pyramid's interior contains earlier structures, nested one inside the other like Russian dolls -- a common Mesoamerican construction practice in which new temples were built over older ones. Inside El Castillo, archaeologists found an earlier pyramid with a jaguar throne -- a red-painted stone sculpture of a reclining jaguar, its eyes made of jade, its spots represented by jade discs -- and a chacmool sculpture. The inner temple's style is consistent with the "Toltec" phase at Chichen Itza, suggesting that the Feathered Serpent cult was established at the site before the current pyramid was built.

Whether the equinox shadow effect was intentional is debated. The alignment is precise enough that many scholars believe it was deliberate -- an architectural expression of the Feathered Serpent's connection to the calendar and the seasons. Others point out that similar shadow effects occur at many pyramids, and that the specific serpentine interpretation may be a modern projection. The debate mirrors broader questions about how much intentionality to attribute to ancient builders -- a question that has no simple answer.

The cenote (seh-NOH-teh) at Chichen Itza adds another dimension. The Sacred Cenote -- a natural sinkhole, roughly sixty meters in diameter -- was a site of pilgrimage and offering for centuries. Dredging operations in the early twentieth century recovered thousands of objects from its depths: jade, gold, copper, pottery, obsidian, rubber, textiles, and human bones. The offerings came from across Mesoamerica -- some objects were clearly of central Mexican origin, others of Maya manufacture, others from the Gulf Coast or the Pacific slope. The Sacred Cenote was a point of convergence for the entire Mesoamerican world, and its deposits provide material evidence for the wide-ranging connections that linked Chichen Itza to distant regions, including central Mexico.

The site of Chichen Itza itself is divided by scholars into two zones: the southern zone, with architecture in the pure Puuc (POOK) Maya style -- vaulted stone buildings with elaborate stone mosaic facades -- and the northern zone, where the "Toltec-style" buildings stand. The juxtaposition is dramatic: walk south and you are in the Maya world; walk north and you are in something that looks like central Mexico. Whether this reflects two distinct periods of construction, two coexisting cultural communities, or a single community that blended traditions is debated. The physical layout of the site embodies the puzzle.

The resemblance between Tula and Chichen Itza remains one of the great puzzles of Mesoamerican archaeology. The two cities clearly share something -- a vocabulary of symbols, architectural forms, and religious ideas that connects them across fifteen hundred kilometers of difficult terrain. The nature of that connection -- conquest, trade, shared participation in a broader cultural movement, or some combination -- is still debated. The puzzle is unlikely to be resolved until new evidence emerges: an inscription naming a central Mexican ruler at Chichen Itza, or a Maya artifact at Tula, or a set of radiocarbon dates that establishes the chronological relationship between the two cities' "Toltec-style" monuments with precision.


## Chapter 7 — The Burning (Again)

Around 1150 CE -- the date is approximate, based on radiocarbon dates and ceramic evidence -- the ceremonial center of Tula was burned and systematically destroyed.

The destruction was thorough. Pyramid B, with its Atlantean warrior columns, was set on fire. The columns themselves were toppled, falling from the pyramid's summit to the ground below, where they were found by archaeologists in the twentieth century. The carved panels depicting jaguars and eagles were smashed. The colonnaded hall was burned. Sculptures were broken and scattered. The skull rack platform was damaged. The destruction was focused on the ceremonial center -- the temples, the public buildings, the symbols of political and religious authority. The residential areas surrounding the center show less evidence of destruction, though the city as a whole was abandoned in the decades that followed.

The Atlantean columns, which had stood for roughly two centuries atop Pyramid B, were found by archaeologists lying face-down at the pyramid's base. They had fallen -- or been pushed -- in a single event, landing in the rubble of the pyramid's collapsed upper structure. The archaeologist Jorge Acosta (HOR-heh ah-KOHS-tah), who excavated Tula's ceremonial center in the 1940s and 1950s, reconstructed the columns and returned them to their original positions atop the pyramid -- the restored figures that visitors see today. The reconstruction is impressive but somewhat misleading: the columns now stand proudly atop a neatly restored pyramid, giving an impression of order and permanence that the original destruction deliberately erased.

The pattern is by now familiar. At San Lorenzo, around 900 BCE, the Olmec ceremonial center was dismantled and its monuments defaced -- apparently by its own inhabitants. At Monte Alban, around 700 CE, the ceremonial center was abandoned and its monuments ceased to be maintained. At Teotihuacan, around 550 CE, the ceremonial center was selectively burned while residential neighborhoods were largely spared. At Tula, around 1150 CE, the same pattern recurred: the destruction of the ceremonial core, the targeting of temples and symbols of power, the relative sparing of residential areas.

The pattern is distinctive enough that scholars have given it a name: ritual termination. The deliberate destruction of ceremonial centers at the end of their political or cosmic cycle is a Mesoamerican practice that spans at least two thousand years and appears at sites across the region. The destruction is not random vandalism. It is targeted, methodical, and meaningful -- an act that simultaneously destroys the physical infrastructure of the old power and performs a cosmological function, ending one era so that a new one can begin.

At Tula, the evidence for deliberate destruction is clear. The Atlantean columns were not simply knocked over by an earthquake or pulled down by neglect. They were toppled from the top of a pyramid that was simultaneously set on fire -- an act that required planning, effort, and access to the pyramid's summit. The carved panels on the pyramid's facade were systematically smashed, their fragments left in place -- not looted, not carted away, but destroyed on site. The destruction was personal: it targeted specific symbols of Toltec authority, leaving them broken at the base of the monuments they had adorned.

![The ruins of Tula's ceremonial center after the burning](../images/placeholders/06-tula-burning-ruins.png)

Who did the burning? The same three hypotheses that apply to Teotihuacan's burning apply here: internal revolution, foreign invasion, or ritual destruction. At Tula, the most likely explanation -- as at Teotihuacan -- is internal revolution, possibly with ritual elements. The selectivity of the destruction (targeting the ceremonial center while leaving residential areas relatively intact) suggests that the destroyers were the city's own inhabitants, burning the institutions that governed them. An external army would have had little reason to spare the residential neighborhoods.

The specific trigger for the destruction is debated. Tula's position was always geographically vulnerable. The city sat at the northern edge of the Mesoamerican agricultural zone, in a region where rainfall is marginal for maize agriculture. The semi-arid climate of the Tula region made the city dependent on irrigation, and irrigation systems are vulnerable to disruption -- by drought, by conflict over water rights, by the accumulation of salts in irrigated soil. Any sustained period of drought would have stressed the city's agricultural base and, with it, the legitimacy of its rulers.

The late twelfth century appears to have been a period of environmental stress in central Mexico. Paleoclimatic evidence -- tree-ring data, lake sediment cores, speleothem records from caves -- suggests a period of reduced rainfall in the region around 1100-1200 CE. The timing is suggestive: environmental stress undermining agricultural production, leading to social unrest, leading to the destruction of the political institutions blamed for the crisis. The same sequence that may explain Teotihuacan's burning -- environmental stress, loss of legitimacy, political revolution -- may explain Tula's burning six centuries later.

The Aztec sources describe Tula's fall in mythological terms -- the conflict between Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca, the departure of the priest-king, the city's subsequent decline. The archaeological record describes the same event in material terms -- fire, destruction, abandonment. The two accounts may describe the same process from different perspectives: the mythology preserving the memory of a political-religious conflict (the Quetzalcoatl faction versus the Tezcatlipoca faction), the archaeology preserving the material traces of that conflict's violent conclusion.

After the burning, Tula's population dispersed. Some groups migrated south into the Valley of Mexico, where they established or joined other communities. Others moved in different directions. The *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca* describes the migration of Toltec-Chichimec groups after Tula's fall, their founding of new communities, and their struggles to establish themselves in a world where the old center of power had been destroyed. These migration narratives, filtered through centuries of retelling, preserve memories of a diaspora -- a population scattered by the destruction of its capital, carrying its culture, its skills, and its claims of legitimacy to new locations.

The Aztec treatment of Tula's ruins is itself revealing. When the Aztecs visited Tula -- and they did visit, just as they visited Teotihuacan -- they looted it selectively. Excavations at the Templo Mayor in Tenochtitlan, led by Leonardo Lopez Lujan, have recovered objects of Toltec manufacture carefully placed in ritual deposits within the Aztec temple. The Aztecs were not merely remembering the Toltecs; they were collecting their artifacts and incorporating them into their own sacred architecture, just as they collected Teotihuacan objects. The Toltec past was a physical resource as well as a narrative one -- real objects from real ruins, deployed in the service of a mythologized history.

At Tula itself, some of the Atlantean columns' decorative elements were found to be missing in ways that suggest deliberate removal rather than accidental breakage. The Aztecs may have taken pieces of the Toltec monuments back to Tenochtitlan as relics -- fragments of the glorious past, physically embedded in the empire that claimed to inherit it.

The Aztecs themselves may have been part of this post-Tula world. The Mexica migration story -- the journey from the legendary homeland of Aztlan (AHS-tlahn) to the Valley of Mexico -- describes a group moving through a landscape populated by communities that claimed Toltec heritage. The Mexica were latecomers, arriving in the Valley after the established communities had already divided the territory among themselves. Their eventual rise to power involved, among other strategies, marrying into lineages that claimed Toltec descent -- acquiring by marriage the legitimacy that their own origins did not provide.

The destruction of Tula created the power vacuum that the Aztecs would eventually fill. Between Tula's fall around 1150 CE and the founding of Tenochtitlan around 1325 CE, central Mexico experienced a period of political fragmentation -- multiple small city-states competing for territory, resources, and prestige, with no single power dominant. The Epiclassic period that followed Teotihuacan's fall repeated itself in a minor key: the collapse of the center, the fragmentation of the periphery, the gradual emergence of a new dominant power. The cycle was a feature of Mesoamerican political life, recurring with almost mathematical regularity.

The destruction of Tula echoes through the landscape. The site today is a quiet archaeological park, its restored pyramids and columns standing in a grassy precinct visited by tourists and school groups. The Atlantean warriors gaze southward across the valley, their expressions unreadable, their butterfly breastplates and feathered headdresses intact -- or rather, reassembled by Acosta's restoration team from fragments found in the rubble. The modern visitor sees a tidy, well-maintained ruin. The twelfth-century visitor saw a smoking wreck, a broken city, the end of an era. The gap between the two experiences -- the calm of archaeology and the violence of history -- is one that the ruins themselves cannot bridge.

The Mesoamerican pattern of urban rise, peak, burning, and abandonment invites comparison with other civilizations' cyclical theories of history -- the Chinese dynastic cycle, the Greek concept of political decline from monarchy to tyranny, the Islamic historian Ibn Khaldun's theory of the rise and fall of civilizations driven by *asabiyyah* (group solidarity). The comparison is suggestive but imprecise: the Mesoamerican pattern has a material, archaeological reality that distinguishes it from purely theoretical models. The burning of ceremonial centers is a physical event, not a metaphor. The ash layers are real. The pattern may reflect not a theory about history but a structural feature of Mesoamerican political systems -- a built-in mechanism for political renewal that operated through destruction rather than reform.
