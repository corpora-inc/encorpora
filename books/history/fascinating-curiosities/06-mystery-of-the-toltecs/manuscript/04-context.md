# Part Four: Context

## Chapter 8 — Between Two Giants

The Toltecs occupy a peculiar position in Mesoamerican history: wedged between two of the most famous civilizations in the ancient Americas. Teotihuacan, discussed in Volume 5, dominated the Mesoamerican world from roughly 100 to 550 CE. The Aztecs, who need no introduction, dominated it from roughly 1430 to 1521 CE. Between them -- in the seven centuries that separate Teotihuacan's burning from the Aztec empire's zenith -- the Toltecs were the most important political and cultural force in central Mexico.

Understanding the Toltecs means understanding the transition between these two giants. The Epiclassic period (roughly 550-900 CE) that followed Teotihuacan's collapse was, as discussed in Volume 5, a time of political fragmentation and cultural experimentation. Multiple regional powers -- Xochicalco (soh-chee-KAHL-koh), Cacaxtla (kah-KAHSH-tlah), Cholula (choh-LOO-lah), Cantona (kahn-TOH-nah) -- competed for influence in central Mexico. None achieved Teotihuacan's scale or dominance.

Tula emerged from this fragmentation as the first city to reassemble something approaching centralized political authority in the region. Its rise, in the ninth and tenth centuries, marked the end of the Epiclassic period and the beginning of the Early Postclassic -- a new era characterized by increased militarism, long-distance trade networks, and the spread of the Feathered Serpent cult as a pan-Mesoamerican religious and political symbol.

The Toltec achievement, stripped of Aztec mythologization, was substantial. The city of Tula controlled a territory that extended from the Valley of Mexico into the Bajio region to the northwest -- an area that included agricultural lands, obsidian sources, and trade routes linking the central highlands to the Gulf Coast and beyond. The city's craft production, particularly in obsidian, was significant. Its architectural innovations -- the colonnaded hall, the monumental warrior columns -- influenced Mesoamerican architecture for centuries. Its religious system, centered on the cults of Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca, shaped the religious landscape of Postclassic Mesoamerica.

The military emphasis in Toltec art and architecture is striking. Where Teotihuacan's public art emphasized collective ritual, processions, and deity imagery -- with individual rulers rendered invisible -- Tula's art celebrates the warrior. The Atlantean columns are warriors. The carved panels on Pyramid B depict eagles and jaguars devouring human hearts -- imagery of military orders, of warrior sodalities organized around predatory animal symbolism. The chacmool sculptures receive offerings in a context of military power. The tzompantli displays the skulls of the sacrificed. The visual program at Tula announces a city whose identity was built around warfare and sacrifice.

This militaristic emphasis may reflect a genuine shift in Mesoamerican political culture. Teotihuacan, for all the mass sacrifice at the Feathered Serpent Pyramid, projected an image of collective, impersonal, cosmologically ordered authority. Tula projected an image of military power, individual warrior achievement, and sacrificial violence as public spectacle. The Aztecs would take both tendencies to their extreme -- combining massive public sacrifice with an elaborate system of warrior honors and military ranks. The Toltecs may represent a middle point in this trajectory: more militaristic than Teotihuacan, less so than the Aztecs, but moving in the direction that the Aztecs would later follow.

The connections between Tula and the other sites discussed in this series deserve explicit attention. At Monte Alban, discussed in Volume 1, the ceremonial center was abandoned around 700 CE -- two centuries before Tula's rise. The Zapotec capital's decline was part of the same post-Teotihuacan fragmentation from which Tula eventually emerged. At Cahokia, discussed in Volume 4, the Mississippian tradition developed independently but arrived at parallel solutions: platform mounds, plaza-centered ceremonial cores, chunkey games, and long-distance trade networks based on prestige goods. Cahokia and Tula were exact contemporaries -- both peaking around 1050-1100 CE -- and both declined in the twelfth century, though they had no contact across the thousands of kilometers that separated them. The Olmec, discussed in Volume 3, developed the feathered serpent imagery that would eventually become the centerpiece of Toltec religion, over a thousand years before Tula existed. At Teotihuacan, discussed in Volume 5, the anonymous metropolis pioneered the urban model and the religious vocabulary that the Toltecs inherited and transformed.

The Toltec trade network extended in every direction. To the north, Tula had connections with communities in the Bajio and possibly with the cultures of the American Southwest -- turquoise, a prized material in Mesoamerica, was mined in what is now New Mexico and Arizona and traded south through networks that may have linked the Pueblo cultures to the Mesoamerican world. To the south and east, Tula's connections reached the Gulf Coast, the Maya lowlands, and the Pacific coast of Guatemala. The obsidian trade was central to these networks: Tula controlled obsidian sources in the central highlands and distributed the material through its commercial connections.

The relationship between Tula and the cities of the Gulf Coast was particularly important. The Gulf lowlands produced cacao, rubber, vanilla, tropical feathers, and other luxury goods that highland cities coveted. The Fine Orange pottery that appears at both Tula and Chichen Itza was produced in the Gulf Coast region, suggesting that Gulf Coast merchant networks linked the two cities. The Gulf Coast may have been the conduit through which ideas, goods, and people traveled between central Mexico and the Yucatan -- explaining the architectural similarities between Tula and Chichen Itza without requiring direct military contact.

The Toltec legacy, as filtered through Aztec memory, shaped the political landscape of Postclassic Mesoamerica. Virtually every major ruling dynasty in central Mexico claimed Toltec descent. The city-states of the Valley of Mexico -- Texcoco (tesh-KOH-koh), Azcapotzalco (ahs-kah-poht-SAHL-koh), Culhuacan (kool-wah-KAHN) -- all traced their royal lineages to Toltec ancestors. The Aztecs, who arrived in the Valley without a Toltec pedigree, acquired one through strategic marriages with the ruling families of Culhuacan, which claimed direct descent from the Toltec royal line. The first Aztec *tlatoani* (tlah-toh-AH-nee), Acamapichtli (ah-kah-mah-PEECH-tlee), was chosen partly because he had Culhua (and therefore Toltec) ancestry. The Aztec imperial project was, in part, a project of Toltec legitimization -- an empire that justified itself by claiming to be the rightful heir of a civilization it had never known.

The Toltec religious innovations shaped the Postclassic world in ways that outlasted the Toltecs themselves. The emphasis on Quetzalcoatl as a priestly, learning-oriented deity -- associated with wind, the morning star, and the arts of civilization -- and Tezcatlipoca as a warrior deity associated with night, sorcery, and fate, created a religious framework that structured Postclassic Mesoamerican politics. The dual opposition between these two divine principles -- creation and destruction, learning and war, the morning star and the obsidian mirror -- provided the theological vocabulary for political conflict. When the Aztecs described their own history, they used this vocabulary: periods of priestly wisdom alternating with periods of military expansion, the eternal tension between the temple and the battlefield.

The Toltec institution of the warrior orders -- the Eagle Warriors and the Jaguar Warriors, organizations of elite military personnel distinguished by their predatory animal insignia -- was another innovation that the Aztecs adopted wholesale. The carved panels at Tula depicting eagles and jaguars devouring human hearts are the earliest known representations of these warrior orders, which would become central to Aztec military organization. The orders provided a path to social mobility: a commoner who distinguished himself in battle could rise to the rank of Eagle Warrior or Jaguar Warrior, gaining privileges, prestige, and exemptions from tribute. The system channeled martial ambition into institutional structures, rewarding individual achievement while reinforcing state authority.

The irony is that the Toltecs themselves probably claimed similar legitimacy from Teotihuacan. The archaeological evidence at Tula includes Teotihuacan-style objects and imagery that suggest the Toltecs, too, looked backward to a greater predecessor for legitimation. The chain of inherited legitimacy stretched from Teotihuacan to Tula to Tenochtitlan -- each civilization claiming to be the heir of the one before it, each constructing a version of the past that served its present political needs. The Mesoamerican past was not a fixed record but a living political resource, continuously reshaped by each generation's requirements.


## Chapter 9 — What Was Happening Elsewhere

Tula's peak -- roughly 900 to 1150 CE -- coincided with one of the most dynamic periods in world history. The city's rise and fall occurred against a global backdrop of transformation, expansion, and upheaval.

The period was also the age of the Crusades. In 1095, Pope Urban II called for the First Crusade, launching two centuries of intermittent warfare between Christian European armies and Muslim states for control of the Holy Land. The First Crusade captured Jerusalem in 1099 -- roughly fifty years before Tula burned. The Crusader states in the Levant were, like Tula, outposts of one cultural tradition planted in the territory of another, sustained by trade and military force, and ultimately destroyed by the forces they had displaced. The parallel is imprecise but suggestive: the twelfth century was, across the world, a time of military expansion, cultural collision, and political fragility.

In northern Europe, the Viking Age was reshaping the continent. Norse seafarers, traders, and settlers from Scandinavia expanded across the North Atlantic, establishing settlements in Iceland (874), Greenland (985), and briefly in North America (Vinland, around 1000 CE) -- making the Norse the first people of the Old World to reach the Americas, roughly five centuries before Columbus. The Viking expansion was driven by the same forces that drove expansion everywhere: population growth, political competition, and the search for new resources and trade routes. At the same time the Toltecs were building their warrior columns in central Mexico, Norse longships were raiding the coasts of England, France, and Ireland, and Norse merchants were trading along river routes from Scandinavia to Constantinople and Baghdad.

In East Asia, the Song Dynasty (960-1279 CE) presided over a golden age of Chinese civilization. Song China was the most technologically advanced society on Earth: it developed movable-type printing, gunpowder weapons, the magnetic compass, and a sophisticated banking system that included paper currency. The Song capital of Kaifeng (KY-fung) had a population of over one million in the eleventh century -- more than twenty times larger than Tula. The comparison highlights the enormous variation in urban scale across the medieval world: Tula was significant in its region, but it was a provincial city by global standards.

The Islamic world was in its golden age. The Abbasid (ah-BAH-sid) Caliphate, centered on Baghdad, fostered advances in mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and philosophy that built on Greek, Persian, and Indian foundations. The House of Wisdom in Baghdad -- a library, translation center, and academy -- preserved and transmitted the knowledge of antiquity during the centuries when Europe had largely lost access to it. Al-Khwarizmi (ahl-KWAH-riz-mee) had already developed algebra. Ibn Sina (IB-uhn SEE-nah) had written the *Canon of Medicine*, which would remain the standard medical reference in Europe for centuries. The breadth of Islamic intellectual achievement during the period of Tula's peak was extraordinary -- and entirely unknown to the Toltecs, separated from the Islamic world by two oceans and a continent.

In sub-Saharan Africa, the Kingdom of Ghana (not the modern country of the same name, but a medieval empire centered in what is now Mauritania and Mali) controlled the trans-Saharan gold trade, linking the gold mines of West Africa to the markets of North Africa and the Mediterranean. The Ghana Empire's wealth -- derived from gold, salt, and the control of trade routes -- made it one of the most powerful states in the medieval world. It would be succeeded by the Mali Empire, whose ruler Mansa Musa would become the wealthiest person in recorded history. The West African gold trade and the Mesoamerican obsidian trade operated on different scales and with different materials, but they shared a common structure: a valuable commodity, controlled by a political authority, distributed through long-distance networks, generating the wealth that sustained urban centers and political power.

In the Americas, Tula's peak coincided with the rise of Cahokia, discussed in Volume 4. Cahokia's "Big Bang" -- the sudden transformation of a modest village into North America's largest city -- occurred around 1050 CE, during the period of Tula's greatest power. The two cities were contemporary, and both represented the apex of urban development in their respective regions. There is no evidence that they had any contact with each other -- separated by over three thousand kilometers of forests, plains, and deserts -- but the parallel is striking: two indigenous American civilizations, in two different cultural traditions, reaching their urban peaks simultaneously.

The comparison between Tula and its global contemporaries reveals a consistent pattern: the period from roughly 900 to 1200 CE was one of urban expansion and political consolidation across the world. The Song Dynasty, the Abbasid Caliphate, the Norman conquests, the Crusades, the expansion of the Khmer Empire in Southeast Asia (whose capital, Angkor, was another contemporary urban center of extraordinary ambition), the rise of Cahokia, the peak of the Chaco Canyon system in the American Southwest -- all occurred during the same broad period. The underlying causes varied by region -- the Medieval Warm Period may have contributed to agricultural surpluses that supported urban growth in some areas -- but the global pattern is clear: this was an era of city-building, state-formation, and long-distance connection.

Tula's destruction around 1150 CE also had parallels. The twelfth century saw political upheaval and urban decline in several parts of the world. The Crusader states in the Near East were under pressure from Saladin's unified Muslim response. The Khmer Empire was beginning the slow decline that would lead to Angkor's abandonment. Cahokia was entering its own period of decline, its population shrinking, its palisade walls signaling the internal tensions that would eventually empty the city. The Medieval Warm Period was transitioning into the Little Ice Age -- a global cooling trend that would stress agricultural systems worldwide. Tula's fall, like its rise, was part of a global pattern, even if the specific causes were local.

In India, the Chola (CHOH-lah) dynasty of southern India was building one of the most powerful maritime empires in Asian history. At the same time the Toltecs were carving their warrior columns in central Mexico, the Chola navy was crossing the Bay of Bengal to attack the Srivijaya (sree-vee-JAH-yah) Empire in Southeast Asia -- projecting military power across an ocean in a way that no Mesoamerican state could have imagined. The Chola temple at Thanjavur (TAHN-jah-voor), completed in 1010 CE, is one of the masterpieces of Indian architecture -- its main tower rising over sixty meters, its walls covered with sculpture of extraordinary refinement. The Chola dynasty and the Toltec state were exact contemporaries, occupying opposite sides of the planet, separated by the Pacific Ocean and two continents, building monuments and projecting power in perfect mutual ignorance.

The Toltecs knew nothing of any of this. Their world was Mesoamerica -- the cultural zone stretching from central Mexico to Honduras and El Salvador, where shared traditions of maize agriculture, calendar systems, pyramid construction, and the Mesoamerican ball game linked diverse peoples across thousands of kilometers. Within that world, the Toltecs were important. Beyond it, they were invisible. The Atlantic and Pacific oceans were absolute barriers to communication. The civilizations of Eurasia, Africa, and the Americas developed in complete isolation from each other, arriving at parallel solutions to parallel problems -- urban planning, long-distance trade, monumental architecture, political legitimization through mythology -- without any contact or knowledge of each other's existence.

In Southeast Asia, the Khmer Empire was building Angkor Wat -- the largest religious monument in the world, covering over 160 hectares, its towers rising above the Cambodian forest. Angkor Wat was completed around 1150 CE -- the same decade that Tula burned. The Khmer capital, Angkor, had a population estimated at several hundred thousand, making it one of the largest cities in the medieval world. Like Tula, Angkor was sustained by an elaborate water management system -- reservoirs, canals, and irrigation channels that supported intensive rice agriculture. Like Tula, Angkor would eventually be abandoned when its environmental and political systems could no longer sustain the urban population. The parallel between the two cities -- both dependent on water management, both destroyed by a combination of environmental and political stress, both abandoned in favor of smaller, more dispersed settlements -- is striking, and entirely coincidental.

The independence of these parallel developments is, as noted in every volume of this series, one of the most profound facts about human civilization. When civilizations on opposite sides of the planet both build pyramids, both create long-distance trade networks, and both use mythology to legitimize political power, the conclusion is that these are things human beings do when they build complex societies. The parallel is evidence of shared human capacity. The Toltecs' place in this global pattern -- small in absolute scale, but significant within their world -- is a reminder that every civilization, however large or small, is an experiment in what human beings are capable of building.

The Toltecs occupy a peculiar place in the imagination. They are the civilization that was famous for being famous -- a people whose greatest achievement, in the end, may have been the legend that others told about them. The Aztecs needed the Toltecs to have been magnificent, and so they were magnificent. The Spanish needed the Quetzalcoatl prophecy to explain the conquest, and so the prophecy existed. Modern Mexico needs the Feathered Serpent as a symbol of pre-colonial greatness, and so Quetzalcoatl endures. The real Toltecs -- the people who built Tula, carved the Atlantean warriors, traded obsidian across central Mexico, and were burned out of their city around 1150 CE -- are visible behind the legend, but only just. They are the phantom empire: real enough to leave ruins, powerful enough to shape the civilizations that followed, but obscured by the very fame that kept their memory alive.

The warrior columns still stand. The Feathered Serpent still coils through Mesoamerican art and Mexican national identity. The ruins of Tula still sit on their limestone ridge above the river, modest and real and utterly unlike the paradise that the Aztecs described. The gap between the legend and the reality is the mystery -- and it is a mystery that says as much about the people who told the legend as about the people who lived at the site. The Toltecs are a mirror: every generation that looks into them sees its own reflection.
