# Part One: Legend vs. Archaeology

## Chapter 1 — Tollan: The Paradise That May Not Have Existed

The Aztec sources describe Tollan as a paradise on earth.

The accounts survive in several colonial-era documents, written down in the decades after the Spanish conquest by indigenous scribes and Spanish friars working together to preserve pre-conquest knowledge. The most important is the *Florentine Codex*, compiled by the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagun (sah-ah-GOON) between the 1540s and 1570s, based on interviews with Aztec elders who remembered the old traditions. The *Florentine Codex* is a massive work -- twelve books, covering every aspect of Aztec life, from religion to natural history to daily customs. Its account of the Toltecs is vivid and specific.

In Tollan, the codex says, the Toltecs grew cotton that came out of the ground already colored -- amaranth red, yellow, violet, blue-green, azure, orange, tawny, brown, and coyote-colored. These colors were natural, not dyed. The Toltecs did not need to apply pigments because the cotton itself grew in every shade. Corn was so abundant and the ears so enormous that short ears were used as fuel for the fires -- they were burned as firewood, too plentiful to bother eating. Squashes were so large that a man could barely put his arms around one. Amaranth plants grew as tall as trees, and people climbed them like trees to harvest the grain.

The Toltec artisans, the codex continues, were masters of every craft. They invented the art of featherwork -- the creation of mosaics and garments from brilliantly colored tropical bird feathers, an art form that the Aztecs considered the highest form of artistic expression. They were the first jade carvers, the first metalworkers, the first painters, the first astronomers. They knew the movements of the stars and the calendar. They built houses of jade and turquoise and quetzal feathers. Everything beautiful in the world had been made first by the Toltecs, and everyone who came after them was merely imitating their achievements.

This is propaganda. It is magnificent propaganda, vivid and memorable and politically effective, but it is propaganda nonetheless.

The Aztecs -- more properly called the Mexica (meh-SHEE-kah) -- arrived in the Valley of Mexico in the thirteenth century as a small, marginal group with no particular claim to authority. They settled on an island in Lake Texcoco (tesh-KOH-koh) and built their capital, Tenochtitlan (teh-nohch-TEE-tlahn), around 1325 CE. Within two centuries they had conquered an empire that stretched from the Gulf of Mexico to the Pacific, from central Mexico to the borders of present-day Guatemala. It was one of the most rapid imperial expansions in the history of the Americas.

But rapid expansion creates a legitimacy problem. A ruling dynasty needs a pedigree. It needs to demonstrate that its right to rule is ancient, not improvised. Throughout Mesoamerican history, the solution to this problem was the same: claim descent from the Toltecs. The Aztec royal families traced their genealogies back to Toltec rulers. The city-states of the Valley of Mexico competed to establish the most convincing Toltec pedigree. To marry a woman of Toltec descent was to acquire legitimacy. To claim a Toltec ancestor was to claim the mantle of civilization itself.

The result is that virtually everything we know about the Toltecs comes from sources produced by people who had a political stake in how the Toltecs were remembered. The Aztec accounts are not disinterested history. They are origin stories told by an empire that needed the Toltecs to have been glorious, because the Aztecs' own legitimacy depended on inheriting that glory.

The *Annals of Cuauhtitlan* (kwow-TEE-tlahn), another colonial-era source compiled from pre-conquest oral traditions, provides a somewhat different account of Toltec history -- more focused on political events, less on the paradise motif, but equally shaped by the political concerns of the communities that preserved the traditions. The *Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca*, a mid-sixteenth-century document from Cuauhtinchan (kwow-TEEN-chahn) in Puebla, tells the story of the Toltec-Chichimec migration and the founding of new communities after Tula's fall. Each of these sources has its own biases, its own agenda, and its own relationship to the Toltec past. None is a neutral record.

The historian Nigel Davies, who spent decades working through these contradictory sources, described the problem in his 1977 book *The Toltecs Until the Fall of Tula*: the sources do not agree with each other, they contain chronological impossibilities, they mix historical figures with gods, and they were compiled centuries after the events they describe. Separating historical fact from political mythology in the Toltec sources is, Davies concluded, one of the most difficult problems in Mesoamerican history.

The word "Toltec" itself carries the weight of this mythology. In Nahuatl, *toltecatl* means both "person from Tollan" and "artisan" or "master craftsman." The dual meaning is not an accident. The Aztecs so thoroughly identified the Toltecs with artistic excellence that the name of the people became the name of the skill. A skilled featherworker was a *toltecatl*. A master sculptor was a *toltecatl*. The word embedded the assumption that all good craft originated with the Toltecs -- that to be skilled was to be, in some essential way, Toltec.

This linguistic fusion of identity and excellence made the Toltecs impossible to evaluate objectively. If "Toltec" means "master artisan," then by definition the Toltecs were master artisans. The mythology is built into the vocabulary. Every time a Nahuatl speaker used the word *toltecatl* to describe a skilled craftsman, they were reinforcing the idea that the Toltecs had been the original masters -- whether or not the historical Toltecs were actually more skilled than their contemporaries.

The concept of "Tollan" adds another layer of complexity. Alfredo Lopez Austin and Leonardo Lopez Lujan (leh-oh-NAHR-doh LOH-pehs loo-HAHN) have argued that "Tollan" was not simply the name of one city -- it was a title, a concept, a category. In Mesoamerican political thought, a *tollan* was any great city, any place of civilization and artistry. Teotihuacan was a Tollan. Cholula was a Tollan. Chichen Itza was a Tollan. The word meant something like "Place of Reeds" -- reeds being a metaphor for densely packed people, a great gathering, a city. When the Aztec sources describe Tollan as the origin of civilization, they may be describing not a specific city but a concept -- the idea of the great city itself, the archetypal metropolis from which all civilized life flows. The archaeological site of Tula may be one incarnation of this concept, perhaps the most recent before the Aztecs, but not necessarily the original or the greatest.

If Lopez Austin is right, the gap between legend and archaeology becomes more understandable. The Aztec sources are not describing Tula; they are describing an ideal -- the Platonic form of the great city, projected backward onto a real but imperfect place. The Toltecs are not a people so much as a concept: the idea that somewhere in the past there existed a civilization so perfect that all subsequent civilizations are mere shadows of it. The concept served the Aztecs politically -- it gave them a standard to aspire to and an ancestry to claim -- but it was never meant to be a factual description of one particular archaeological site.


## Chapter 2 — Tula: What's Actually There

The identification of Tula as Tollan was not always accepted. The first scholar to make the identification definitively was the Mexican historian Wigberto Jimenez Moreno (hee-MEH-nehs moh-REH-noh), who presented his argument at a scholarly conference in 1941. Before Jimenez Moreno, many scholars -- influenced by the grandeur of the Aztec descriptions -- had assumed that Tollan must be Teotihuacan, the great city discussed in Volume 5. Teotihuacan was larger, more impressive, and more consistent with the Aztec descriptions of a paradise city. The identification of relatively modest Tula as the legendary Tollan was, for some scholars, a disappointment -- a discovery that the reality behind the legend was less magnificent than the legend itself. The disappointment is itself revealing: scholars, like the Aztecs, wanted Tollan to be grand.

The archaeological site identified as Tollan is located near the modern town of Tula de Allende (TOO-lah deh ah-YEHN-deh) in the state of Hidalgo, roughly eighty kilometers north of Mexico City. The site sits on a limestone ridge above the Tula River, in a semi-arid landscape of scrubby hills and irrigated farmland. It is not an especially dramatic setting -- the valley is pleasant but not grand, the river modest, the surrounding terrain unremarkable. The site itself, however, is substantial.

Tula's urban core covers approximately thirteen square kilometers, with a ceremonial center containing several major structures. The population at the city's peak, around 1000-1100 CE, is estimated at 30,000 to 60,000 people -- a significant city by any standard, though far smaller than Teotihuacan's 125,000-200,000. The city was a genuine urban center, with residential neighborhoods, workshops, markets, and monumental public architecture. It was the largest city in central Mexico during its time, the dominant power in a region that stretched from the Valley of Mexico into the Bajio (bah-HEE-oh) region to the northwest.

The most famous structures at Tula are the Atlantean warrior columns -- four colossal basalt figures, each standing 4.6 meters tall, that once supported the roof of the Temple of Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli (tlah-wees-kahl-pahn-teh-KOOT-lee), the "Temple of the Morning Star." The figures represent warriors in full military regalia: butterfly-shaped breastplates, feathered headdresses, atlatl (AHT-lahtl) dart throwers held at their sides, and short, decorated aprons. Their faces are stern, their posture rigid, their gaze fixed forward. They are among the most iconic images in Mesoamerican art -- reproduced in countless textbooks, tourist brochures, and museum exhibitions.

The warriors once stood atop Pyramid B, the principal temple at Tula's ceremonial center. The pyramid is a five-tiered structure, modest in scale compared to the great pyramids of Teotihuacan but impressive in its decoration. Its facades were covered with carved panels depicting jaguars, coyotes, eagles devouring human hearts, and images of a figure that scholars identify as Quetzalcoatl (keht-sahl-KOH-ahtl) -- the Feathered Serpent deity whose cult was central to Toltec religion. The carved panels are arranged in horizontal bands that wrap around the pyramid's exterior, creating a continuous narrative of military power and divine authority.

Pyramid B is not the only major structure at the ceremonial center. Pyramid C, located across the central plaza, was actually the larger of the two main pyramids, though it has received less archaeological attention and is less well preserved. The Palacio Quemado (pah-LAH-syoh keh-MAH-doh) -- the "Burned Palace" -- is a complex of colonnaded halls adjacent to Pyramid B that was destroyed by fire during the city's abandonment. Its charred remains gave the structure its modern name. The Burned Palace contained carved bench reliefs showing processions of warriors, their bodies in profile, their faces turned toward the viewer, marching in orderly lines -- an image of military discipline and collective power.

Adjacent to Pyramid B also stands a large colonnaded hall -- a roofed structure supported by rows of square columns, covering a floor area of roughly 1,100 square meters. The hall could have accommodated hundreds of people simultaneously. Its function is debated: it may have been a meeting hall for warriors, a space for public ceremonies, a market, or some combination. Colonnaded halls of this type are a distinctive Toltec architectural feature, different from anything at earlier Mesoamerican sites. They suggest a new kind of public architecture -- a covered space for large gatherings, rather than the open plazas and pyramid-top temples that characterized earlier Mesoamerican cities.

Chacmool (CHAHK-mohl) sculptures are another distinctive Toltec form. These are reclining human figures, lying on their backs with knees drawn up and heads turned to one side, holding a shallow dish or plate on their stomachs. The dish was probably a receptacle for offerings -- food, incense, rubber balls, or human hearts. The chacmool form was not invented at Tula -- examples exist at earlier sites -- but Tula made it a standard feature of ceremonial architecture. Chacmool sculptures were placed at the entrances to temples, where they served as intermediaries between the human and divine realms -- the plate on the stomach receiving the offering that was then transmitted to the gods.

The *tzompantli* (tsohm-PAHNT-lee), or skull rack, is another feature of Tula's ceremonial center. The tzompantli was a wooden framework on which the skulls of sacrificed individuals were displayed. At Tula, the skull rack platform is a low stone structure carved with rows of human skulls -- permanent stone replicas of the wooden racks that would have held actual skulls. The iconography is explicit: skulls in profile, arranged in neat rows, staring outward. The tzompantli was a public display of sacrificial violence, a monument to the dead that was simultaneously a warning to the living. The Aztecs later adopted the skull rack as a standard feature of their own ceremonial centers, placing a massive tzompantli at the heart of Tenochtitlan.

A ball court -- an I-shaped playing field flanked by sloping walls -- stands near the ceremonial center. The Mesoamerican ball game, played across the region for over three thousand years, had deep cosmological significance: in the *Popol Vuh* (poh-POHL vooh), the Maya creation epic, the Hero Twins play the ball game against the lords of the underworld, and their victory enables the creation of humanity. Ball courts at Tula are larger than those at most earlier central Mexican sites, suggesting an increased emphasis on the ball game as a political and religious institution during the Toltec period.

The residential areas of Tula have received less attention than the ceremonial center, but excavations by Dan Healan and others have revealed a city with substantial craft production. Obsidian workshops -- areas where volcanic glass was worked into tools, weapons, and ornaments -- were widespread. Tula controlled access to obsidian sources in the region, and obsidian production was apparently a major economic activity. The residential architecture consists of multi-room compounds organized around courtyards, similar in concept (though smaller in scale) to Teotihuacan's apartment compounds. The compounds housed extended families or lineage groups, and some show evidence of specialized craft production -- suggesting that certain families or neighborhoods specialized in particular trades. Healan's excavations in the Canal Locality, a residential area north of the ceremonial center, revealed dense concentrations of obsidian debitage -- the waste flakes produced during tool manufacturing -- indicating that obsidian workshops operated within ordinary residential compounds, not in segregated industrial zones. The craft was embedded in daily life, not separated from it.

Mazapa (mah-SAH-pah) pottery -- a distinctive red-on-buff ceramic ware -- is the diagnostic artifact of the Toltec period at Tula and across the region. Its presence at sites far from Tula marks the extent of Toltec commercial and cultural influence. Plumbate (PLOOM-bayt) pottery, a glazed ware produced on the Pacific coast of Guatemala and Chiapas (chee-AH-pahs), appears at Tula in quantities that indicate long-distance trade connections -- the only true glazed pottery produced in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, its shiny surface a technological achievement unique in the Americas.

Richard Diehl's excavations in the 1970s and 1980s produced the definitive archaeological study of the site. His 1983 monograph, *Tula: The Toltec Capital of Ancient Mexico*, established the chronological framework that scholars still use. Diehl demonstrated that Tula was occupied from at least the seventh century CE, grew rapidly in the ninth and tenth centuries, reached its maximum extent around 1000-1100 CE, and was destroyed and abandoned around 1150-1200 CE. The city's lifespan -- roughly four centuries of significant occupation -- was shorter than Teotihuacan's, and its peak was less dramatic. Tula was a major regional power, not a continental one.

The site also includes a large open plaza -- the Plaza Mayor -- surrounded by the main ceremonial structures. The plaza could have accommodated thousands of people for public ceremonies, markets, or political gatherings. Its scale is typical of Mesoamerican ceremonial centers: a flat, open space defined by the surrounding architecture, creating a public arena where the power of the state was displayed and reinforced. The placement of the tzompantli at the plaza's edge ensured that anyone entering the ceremonial heart of the city passed the displayed skulls -- a constant reminder of the sacrificial obligations that sustained the cosmic order.

Coyotlatelco (koh-YOH-tlah-TEHL-koh) pottery -- a red-on-buff ware named for the site where it was first identified -- marks the earliest phases of occupation at Tula, in the seventh and eighth centuries CE, before the city rose to prominence. The transition from Coyotlatelco to Mazapa pottery, around the ninth century, corresponds to the period of rapid growth that transformed Tula from a modest settlement into a major urban center. The ceramic sequence provides the chronological backbone for understanding the city's development -- a timeline written in broken pottery, each style marking a phase in the city's life.

The gap between the Aztec legend and the archaeological reality is striking. The Aztec sources describe a paradise of supernatural abundance and unmatched artistic achievement. The archaeology reveals a substantial and impressive city -- but not a unique one. Tula's architecture, while monumental, is not larger or more sophisticated than what came before at Teotihuacan or what existed contemporaneously at Chichen Itza (chee-CHEHN eet-SAH). Its craft production, while skilled, does not obviously surpass that of other Mesoamerican centers. The city was real, important, and powerful. It was not the paradise the legends describe.


## Chapter 3 — The Credibility Problem

The Toltec credibility problem goes deeper than the gap between legend and archaeology. It touches the foundations of how we know anything about the Mesoamerican past.

The core difficulty is this: virtually all our narrative sources for Toltec history were produced by people who lived centuries after the Toltecs, and who had powerful political reasons to shape the story in particular ways. The Aztec accounts of the Toltecs are not eyewitness reports. They are memories of memories, stories told and retold across generations, filtered through the political needs of the societies that preserved them. By the time they were written down in the sixteenth century, they had been shaped by centuries of Aztec political culture -- a culture that used Toltec heritage as the basis for political legitimacy.

The problem is not unique to the Toltecs. Every ancient civilization's history is partially constructed from later sources -- the Hebrew Bible describes events centuries before it was compiled, the Homeric epics describe a Bronze Age world from the perspective of the Iron Age, the Roman foundation myths blend history and legend. But the Toltec case is extreme, because the political motivation to reshape the past was so explicit. The Aztec ruling class did not merely remember the Toltecs -- they *needed* the Toltecs to have been magnificent, because their own right to rule depended on Toltec ancestry.

The result is a puzzle that Nigel Davies compared to a hall of mirrors: every source reflects every other source, and the original image is impossible to recover. When the *Florentine Codex* says that the Toltecs invented featherwork, does that reflect a genuine historical tradition -- a memory of a time when featherworking techniques were first developed at Tula -- or does it reflect the Aztec assumption that everything good must have a Toltec origin? When the *Annals of Cuauhtitlan* describes a sequence of Toltec rulers with specific reign lengths, does that reflect an actual king list preserved through oral tradition, or a post-hoc construction designed to give the Toltec past the appearance of historical precision?

The anthropologist Alfredo Lopez Austin (LOH-pehs ow-STEEN) has argued that many elements of the Toltec tradition are not historical at all -- they are mythological narratives, stories about gods and cosmic cycles, that later generations reinterpreted as history. The story of Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, which will be examined in Part Two, is the clearest example: a narrative that has elements of genuine historical memory (a ruler, a city, a conflict) entangled with elements of cosmological myth (a god, a cosmic struggle, a departure to the east). Separating the two is not always possible, and any attempt to do so involves scholarly judgment calls that other scholars will dispute.

The archaeologist Michael Smith, in his comprehensive study *The Aztecs* (2012), has proposed a useful framework for thinking about the credibility problem. Smith distinguishes between what he calls the "ethnohistorical Toltecs" -- the Toltecs as they appear in the written sources -- and the "archaeological Toltecs" -- the people who actually built and lived at Tula. The two are related but not identical. The ethnohistorical Toltecs are a composite: part historical memory, part political mythology, part cosmological narrative. The archaeological Toltecs are the people whose buildings, pottery, tools, and art survive in the ground at Tula. The challenge is to use the archaeology to test the written sources -- to determine which elements of the Aztec tradition correspond to archaeological reality and which are mythological additions.

Some elements of the tradition pass the archaeological test. The Aztec sources say that the Toltecs were skilled craftsmen -- and Tula's workshops confirm substantial craft production, particularly in obsidian working. The sources say that Tula was a major city -- and the archaeology confirms a significant urban center. The sources say that Tula was destroyed by fire -- and the archaeology confirms a burning event.

Other elements fail the test. The Aztec sources describe Tula as a city of unprecedented wealth and beauty -- but Tula's material culture, while impressive, does not support this characterization. The sources describe a sequence of specific rulers with detailed biographical narratives -- but the archaeology provides no confirmation of any individual ruler. The sources describe Tula as the origin of all Mesoamerican civilization -- but the archaeology clearly shows that Teotihuacan, Monte Alban, the Olmec cities, and the Maya kingdoms all preceded the Toltecs by centuries or millennia.

The credibility problem extends to chronology. The Aztec sources assign specific dates to Toltec events using the Mesoamerican calendar system, but these dates are internally inconsistent -- different sources give different dates for the same events, and some of the implied time spans are implausibly long. The archaeological dates, based on radiocarbon dating and ceramic sequences, provide a general chronological framework (Tula's peak at roughly 900-1150 CE), but they cannot confirm or deny the specific dates given in the written sources.

The problem has a parallel in European history. The story of King Arthur -- the once and future king, the ruler of a golden age, the founder of Camelot -- is one of the most enduring legends in Western culture. Like the Toltec tradition, the Arthurian legend contains a possible historical kernel (a Romano-British leader who resisted the Anglo-Saxon invasions) encrusted with centuries of literary elaboration, political mythology, and wishful thinking. Like the Toltec tradition, the Arthurian legend was used by later rulers to legitimize their authority: English kings claimed Arthurian descent, just as Aztec rulers claimed Toltec descent. And like the Toltec tradition, the Arthurian legend is nearly impossible to separate into its historical and mythological components. The parallel is not exact -- the sources, the cultures, and the contexts are different -- but it illustrates a universal human tendency: the construction of golden ages in the past, used to legitimize power in the present.

What remains, after the mythology is stripped away, is a city and a civilization that were genuinely important -- not as the inventors of Mesoamerican civilization, but as the dominant power in central Mexico during a specific historical period, roughly 900-1150 CE, between the fall of Teotihuacan and the rise of the Aztecs. The Toltecs' real achievement was not the invention of culture but the reorganization of central Mexico after the political fragmentation that followed Teotihuacan's collapse. That achievement -- substantial, complex, and historically significant -- is worth understanding on its own terms, without the golden glow of Aztec mythology.
