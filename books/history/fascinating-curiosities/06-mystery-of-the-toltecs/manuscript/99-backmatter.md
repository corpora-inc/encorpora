# What We Still Don't Know

**Were the Toltecs real?** The question sounds strange, but it is genuine. The "Toltecs" described in Aztec sources -- inventors of all civilization, builders of a paradise city -- are largely mythological. The people who built and lived at Tula are archaeologically real. The gap between the myth and the archaeology remains one of the central problems in Mesoamerican studies. How much of the Aztec tradition reflects genuine historical memory, and how much is political propaganda?

**Who was Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl?** A historical ruler of Tula? A mythological figure based on the deity Quetzalcoatl? A composite of several people across several centuries? The Aztec sources describe a priestly king who opposed human sacrifice and was driven into exile by the followers of Tezcatlipoca. The archaeological record at Tula provides no confirmation of any individual ruler. The narrative blends history and cosmology so thoroughly that separating them may be impossible.

**What is the relationship between Tula and Chichen Itza?** Two cities, 1,500 km apart, share a strikingly specific set of architectural and artistic features: warrior columns, chacmools, feathered serpent imagery, colonnaded halls, skull racks. Did one city conquer the other? Did both draw on a shared cultural tradition? Was there a third source that influenced both? The chronological relationship remains unclear, and the debate is unresolved.

**Did the Quetzalcoatl return prophecy exist before the Spanish conquest?** The story that Moctezuma believed Cortes was the returning Quetzalcoatl is one of the most famous narratives in the history of the Americas. Modern scholars are deeply skeptical: the story may be a post-conquest invention that served both Spanish and Aztec interests. But the relationship between Quetzalcoatl, the east, and the concept of cyclical return is embedded in Mesoamerican cosmology, and some version of the tradition may have existed before 1519.

**What triggered Tula's destruction?** The burning of Tula's ceremonial center around 1150 CE follows the pattern of Teotihuacan (~550 CE) and San Lorenzo (~900 BCE) -- targeted destruction of political-religious infrastructure, apparently by internal revolutionaries. Environmental stress (drought in the semi-arid Tula region) is a likely contributing factor. The specific sequence of events is unknown.

**What language did the Toltecs speak?** Nahuatl is the most common assumption, but Otomi, Huastec, and other languages are possible. The Tula region in the Postclassic period was linguistically diverse. Without written texts, the language of Tula's builders cannot be determined with certainty.

**Is there a Mesoamerican cycle of urban destruction?** San Lorenzo (~900 BCE), Monte Alban (~700 CE), Teotihuacan (~550 CE), Tula (~1150 CE) -- all experienced the targeted destruction or abandonment of their ceremonial centers. Is this pattern a structural feature of Mesoamerican political systems, or a coincidence of unrelated events?

These are open questions. The ruins still hold secrets.


# Key Names and Terms

**Abbasid Caliphate** (ah-BAH-sid) -- The Islamic empire centered on Baghdad (750-1258 CE), during whose golden age advances in mathematics, astronomy, and medicine flourished.

**Acamapichtli** (ah-kah-mah-PEECH-tlee) -- First *tlatoani* of Tenochtitlan; chosen partly for his Culhua (Toltec) ancestry.

**Annals of Cuauhtitlan** (kwow-TEE-tlahn) -- A colonial-era Nahuatl text recording pre-conquest history, including accounts of Toltec rulers and events.

**Atlantean columns** -- Colossal stone warrior figures at Tula, 4.6 meters tall, that once supported the roof of the Temple of the Morning Star.

**Atlatl** (AHT-lahtl) -- A spear-throwing device that extends the length of the arm, increasing the speed and distance of a thrown dart.

**Aztlan** (AHS-tlahn) -- The legendary homeland of the Mexica (Aztecs), from which they migrated to the Valley of Mexico.

**Bajio** (bah-HEE-oh) -- A fertile lowland region in north-central Mexico, northwest of the Valley of Mexico.

**Books of Chilam Balam** (chee-LAHM bah-LAHM) -- Colonial-era Maya texts from the Yucatan that record pre-conquest history and prophecy.

**Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl** (seh ah-KAHTL toh-PEEL-tseen keht-sahl-KOH-ahtl) -- The legendary priest-king of Tula who was driven into exile; a figure blending historical and mythological elements.

**Chacmool** (CHAHK-mohl) -- A reclining stone figure holding an offering dish on its stomach, placed at temple entrances across Postclassic Mesoamerica.

**Chichen Itza** (chee-CHEHN eet-SAH) -- A major Maya city in the Yucatan that shares striking architectural similarities with Tula.

**Cobean, Robert** -- American archaeologist; specialist in Tula-region ceramics.

**Cortes, Hernan** (ehr-NAHN kor-TEHS) -- Spanish conquistador who arrived in Mexico in 1519 and led the conquest of the Aztec empire.

**Culhuacan** (kool-wah-KAHN) -- A city-state in the Valley of Mexico that claimed direct Toltec descent; source of the Aztec royal lineage.

**Davies, Nigel** -- British historian; author of *The Toltecs Until the Fall of Tula* (1977) and *The Toltec Heritage* (1980).

**Diehl, Richard** -- American archaeologist; author of *Tula: The Toltec Capital of Ancient Mexico* (1983), the definitive archaeological monograph.

**El Castillo** (ehl kahs-TEE-yoh) -- "The Castle"; the Pyramid of Kukulkan at Chichen Itza, famous for its equinox serpent shadow.

**Epiclassic** -- The transitional period (~550-900 CE) following Teotihuacan's decline, characterized by political fragmentation and the rise of regional powers.

**Fine Orange pottery** -- A distinctive ceramic ware produced on the Gulf Coast and traded across Mesoamerica during the Postclassic period.

**Florentine Codex** -- A twelve-book encyclopedia of Aztec life compiled by Bernardino de Sahagun (1540s-1570s), the most important source for Aztec and Toltec traditions.

**Healan, Dan** -- American archaeologist; excavator of Tula's residential and workshop areas.

**Historia Tolteca-Chichimeca** -- A mid-sixteenth-century document describing the Toltec-Chichimec migration after Tula's fall.

**Kukulkan** (koo-kool-KAHN) -- The Maya name for the Feathered Serpent deity, equivalent to the Nahuatl Quetzalcoatl.

**Lopez Austin, Alfredo** (LOH-pehs ow-STEEN) -- Mexican scholar of Mesoamerican cosmology; analyst of the Quetzalcoatl tradition as "mythic history."

**Lopez Lujan, Leonardo** -- Mexican archaeologist; Templo Mayor excavations and Aztec-Toltec connections.

**Malintzin** (mah-LEEN-tseen) -- Indigenous woman who served as translator and advisor to Cortes during the Spanish conquest.

**Mexica** (meh-SHEE-kah) -- The proper name for the people commonly called the Aztecs; founders of Tenochtitlan.

**Moctezuma II** (mohk-teh-SOO-mah) -- Aztec emperor at the time of the Spanish arrival (r. 1502-1520).

**Popocatepetl** (poh-poh-kah-TEH-pehtl) -- Active volcano southeast of the Valley of Mexico; through whose pass Ce Acatl Topiltzin traveled into exile.

**Pulque** (POOL-keh) -- Fermented sap of the maguey plant; the intoxicating drink used in the story of Ce Acatl Topiltzin's downfall.

**Pyramid B** -- The principal temple at Tula's ceremonial center, crowned by the Atlantean warrior columns.

**Quetzalcoatl** (keht-sahl-KOH-ahtl) -- "Feathered Serpent"; one of the most important Mesoamerican deities, associated with wind, learning, and the morning star (Venus).

**Ritual termination** -- The Mesoamerican practice of deliberately destroying ceremonial centers at the end of their political or cosmic cycle.

**Sahagun, Bernardino de** (sah-ah-GOON) -- Franciscan friar who compiled the *Florentine Codex* from Aztec oral traditions (1540s-1570s).

**Smith, Michael** -- American archaeologist; author of *The Aztecs* (2012), essential context for understanding the Aztec use of Toltec heritage.

**Stuart, David** -- American Maya epigrapher; work on Maya inscriptions at Chichen Itza showing Maya (not central Mexican) rulers.

**Tezcatlipoca** (tehs-kaht-lee-POH-kah) -- "Smoking Mirror"; Mesoamerican deity associated with night, sorcery, and fate; rival of Quetzalcoatl.

**Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli** (tlah-wees-kahl-pahn-teh-KOOT-lee) -- "Lord of the Dawn"; the Morning Star deity; temple at Tula crowned by the Atlantean columns.

**Tlatoani** (tlah-toh-AH-nee) -- "Speaker"; the Nahuatl title for a supreme ruler.

**Toltecatl** (tohl-TEH-kahtl) -- Nahuatl word meaning both "person from Tollan" and "master artisan."

**Tollan** (TOHL-lahn) -- The legendary Toltec capital, identified by archaeologists with the site of Tula in Hidalgo.

**Townsend, Camilla** -- American historian; author of *Malintzin's Choices* (2006); leading critic of the Quetzalcoatl-return narrative.

**Tula** (TOO-lah) -- The archaeological site in Hidalgo identified as the Toltec capital; peak population 30,000-60,000.

**Tzompantli** (tsohm-PAHNT-lee) -- A wooden rack or stone platform for the display of human skulls, a feature of Toltec and Aztec ceremonial centers.


# Further Reading

**For the general reader:**

- Davies, Nigel. *The Toltecs Until the Fall of Tula*. University of Oklahoma Press, 1977. -- The most comprehensive historical study of the Toltecs, working through the contradictory sources with care and skepticism.

- Smith, Michael E. *The Aztecs*. 3rd ed. Wiley-Blackwell, 2012. -- Essential for understanding how the Aztecs used Toltec heritage; the best single-volume introduction to the Aztec world.

- Townsend, Camilla. *Fifth Sun: A New History of the Aztecs*. Oxford University Press, 2019. -- A groundbreaking retelling of Aztec history from indigenous sources, with important reassessment of the Quetzalcoatl tradition.

- Mann, Charles C. *1491: New Revelations of the Americas Before Columbus*. Vintage, 2006. -- Excellent survey with strong sections on Mesoamerican civilization.

**For the archaeology:**

- Diehl, Richard A. *Tula: The Toltec Capital of Ancient Mexico*. Thames & Hudson, 1983. -- The definitive archaeological study of the site.

- Healan, Dan M., ed. *Tula of the Toltecs: Excavations and Survey*. University of Iowa Press, 1989. -- Technical reports from excavations of Tula's residential and workshop areas.

**For the Quetzalcoatl tradition:**

- Lopez Austin, Alfredo. *The Myths of the Opossum: Pathways of Mesoamerican Mythology*. University of New Mexico Press, 1993. -- Essential for understanding Mesoamerican "mythic history."

- Townsend, Camilla. "Burying the White Gods: New Perspectives on the Conquest of Mexico." *American Historical Review* 108, no. 3 (2003): 659-687. -- The most influential modern critique of the Quetzalcoatl-return narrative.
