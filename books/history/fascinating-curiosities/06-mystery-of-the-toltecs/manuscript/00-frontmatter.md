# A Note to the Reader

The Aztecs told a story about a golden age.

Before the Aztecs rose to power, they said, there had been a civilization so brilliant that the very word for "artisan" was named after its people. These were the Toltecs (TOHL-tehks), the master craftsmen, the builders of the legendary city of Tollan (TOHL-lahn). In Tollan, cotton grew already dyed in brilliant colors -- red, yellow, green, blue -- so that weavers never needed to color it. Corn grew so large that a single ear could barely be carried. The Toltec artisans made everything beautiful: featherwork, jade carving, metalwork, pottery, sculpture. They were the inventors of civilization itself. Every good thing in the Aztec world was said to have come from them.

The Aztec rulers claimed Toltec descent. Toltec blood was the ultimate legitimizer -- the proof that a dynasty had the right to rule. The word *toltecatl* (tohl-TEH-kahtl) -- literally "Toltec person" -- became the Nahuatl word for any skilled artisan. To be a Toltec was to be a master of craft. To claim Toltec ancestry was to claim mastery over civilization itself.

In the twentieth century, archaeologists went looking for Tollan. They found a site called Tula (TOO-lah), in the state of Hidalgo, roughly eighty kilometers north of Mexico City. Tula is an impressive archaeological site. It has monumental sculpture, pyramids, colonnaded halls, and evidence of a substantial population. It is a real place with real ruins.

It is not paradise. It is not the place where cotton grew pre-dyed and corn grew the size of a child. The gap between the Aztec legend and the archaeological reality is one of the central puzzles of Mesoamerican history -- and it opens onto questions about mythology, political propaganda, and the uses of the past that resonate far beyond ancient Mexico.

This is the story of the phantom empire -- the civilization that may have been real, may have been imaginary, and was almost certainly both.
