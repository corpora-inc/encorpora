# Part Two: Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl

## Chapter 4 — The Man Who Became a God (or the God Who Became a Man)

At the center of the Toltec tradition stands a figure who is simultaneously a priest-king, a deity, a cultural hero, and a prophecy. His name is Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl (seh ah-KAHTL toh-PEEL-tseen keht-sahl-KOH-ahtl), and his story is one of the most famous and most contested narratives in the history of the Americas.

The name itself is layered. *Ce Acatl* -- "One Reed" -- is a date in the 260-day Mesoamerican ritual calendar. Mesoamerican people were often named for the day on which they were born, so "Ce Acatl" is a birth name, a personal identifier. *Topiltzin* -- "Our Revered Prince" -- is a title of respect and authority. *Quetzalcoatl* -- "Feathered Serpent" or "Precious Twin" -- is the name of one of the most important deities in the Mesoamerican religious tradition, a god whose worship spans thousands of years and the entire Mesoamerican world. The full name merges the personal, the political, and the divine into a single identity: a prince, born on the day One Reed, who bore the name of a god.

The story, as told in the Aztec sources, runs roughly as follows.

Ce Acatl Topiltzin was the son of a Toltec lord named Mixcoatl (meesh-KOH-ahtl), "Cloud Serpent," a warrior chief who conquered territory in central Mexico and was murdered by a rival named Ihuitimal before his son was born. The young prince was raised in exile by his maternal grandparents, far from the court that had killed his father. When he came of age, he learned the truth of his father's death, found Mixcoatl's remains, and gave them proper burial on a hilltop that became a place of veneration. He then confronted Ihuitimal and killed him, avenging his father and claiming the throne of Tula. The narrative follows the archetype of the hero's return -- exile, discovery, vengeance, and restoration -- a story pattern found in cultures across the world, from Hamlet to the tale of Horus and Set in Egyptian mythology. As ruler, he was a priest-king devoted to the cult of Quetzalcoatl. He promoted a religion of penance and prayer. He was said to have opposed human sacrifice, offering only snakes, birds, and butterflies to the gods instead of human hearts. He was an ascetic, a holy man, a ruler whose authority rested on spiritual rather than military power.

His reign at Tula was prosperous. Under his leadership, the city flourished. The Toltec arts -- featherwork, jade carving, astronomy, architecture -- reached their height. The golden age of Tollan and the rule of Ce Acatl Topiltzin are the same story, the same era.

Then came the fall.

The god Tezcatlipoca (tehs-kaht-lee-POH-kah) -- "Smoking Mirror" -- was Quetzalcoatl's great rival in the Mesoamerican pantheon. Where Quetzalcoatl represented wind, learning, priestly wisdom, and the morning star, Tezcatlipoca represented night, sorcery, fate, and the obsidian mirror that revealed hidden truths. In the Aztec narrative, Tezcatlipoca came to Tula and tricked Ce Acatl Topiltzin into breaking his priestly vows.

The most famous version of the trick involves pulque (POOL-keh), the fermented sap of the maguey plant. Tezcatlipoca, disguised as a merchant or healer, offered the priestly king a drink, telling him it was medicine. Ce Acatl Topiltzin drank, became intoxicated, and committed acts that violated his vows of purity -- in some versions sleeping with his own sister, in others simply losing his composure and dignity in public drunkenness. When he awoke and realized what he had done, he was overcome with shame.

In other versions, Tezcatlipoca showed Ce Acatl Topiltzin his own reflection in an obsidian mirror -- and the prince, who had never seen his own face, was horrified by his appearance. The mirror reveals the truth, and the truth destroys the illusion of holiness. In still other versions, Tezcatlipoca introduced human sacrifice to Tula, corrupting the city's religious practices and undermining Ce Acatl Topiltzin's authority among factions that favored the new rites.

The details vary, but the outcome is consistent across the sources: Ce Acatl Topiltzin was shamed, defeated, or driven out. He left Tula, traveling east with a retinue of followers. He passed through the Valley of Mexico, through the mountains between the great volcanoes Popocatepetl (poh-poh-kah-TEH-pehtl) and Iztaccihuatl (ees-tahk-SEE-wahtl), and continued east toward the Gulf Coast. At the shore, according to one version, he built a raft of serpents and sailed east across the sea. According to another, he immolated himself on a funeral pyre and rose into the sky as the morning star -- the planet Venus. According to a third, he simply departed across the water, promising to return.

The story is magnificent. It is also, in the form we have it, almost certainly a blend of history and myth that cannot be cleanly separated into its component parts.

The historical kernel may be real. Tula did exist. It was a major city with a priestly class. It experienced political conflicts -- the archaeological record shows evidence of burning and destruction. It is plausible that a ruler associated with the Quetzalcoatl cult held power at Tula and was overthrown in a conflict with a rival faction -- perhaps one associated with Tezcatlipoca's cult, which emphasized warfare and sacrifice. Political conflicts between religious factions are well documented in Mesoamerican history. The outline of the story -- a priestly ruler overthrown by a warrior faction -- is historically plausible.

But the narrative as we have it is saturated with mythological elements that cannot be taken as historical fact. The contest between Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca is a cosmic struggle, not merely a political one. It appears in Mesoamerican creation myths that predate the Toltecs by centuries -- the Feathered Serpent and the Smoking Mirror are primordial forces, locked in an eternal alternation of creation and destruction that structures the Mesoamerican cosmos. The departure to the east and the transformation into the morning star are celestial mythology -- the planet Venus disappears below the western horizon and reappears in the east as the morning star, a cycle that Mesoamerican astronomers tracked with extraordinary precision. Ce Acatl Topiltzin's story mirrors the Venus cycle so closely that many scholars believe the celestial mythology was mapped onto a historical figure (or vice versa) to create a narrative in which human history and cosmic order are one and the same.

The Feathered Serpent deity himself has a much longer history than the Toltecs. As discussed in Volume 3, feathered serpent imagery appears in Olmec art as early as 900 BCE -- more than a thousand years before Tula existed. The great Feathered Serpent Pyramid at Teotihuacan, discussed in Volume 5, was built around 200 CE, seven centuries before Tula's peak. The deity was ancient, widespread, and deeply embedded in Mesoamerican religious thought long before any Toltec ruler took his name. Ce Acatl Topiltzin did not invent the Feathered Serpent; he (or the tradition he represents) adopted the name of a god whose worship was already ancient.

The Venus cycle deserves special attention. Mesoamerican astronomers tracked the planet Venus with extraordinary precision. The Dresden Codex, a Maya manuscript, contains Venus tables that predict the planet's appearances and disappearances with an accuracy of less than one day over centuries. Venus has two phases visible from Earth: as the morning star (rising before the sun in the east) and as the evening star (setting after the sun in the west). Between these phases, Venus disappears for periods of invisibility -- roughly eight days between its evening and morning appearances, and roughly fifty days between its morning and evening appearances. The disappearance and reappearance of Venus were laden with cosmological meaning: they represented death and rebirth, the journey through the underworld and the return to the sky. Ce Acatl Topiltzin's departure to the east and his transformation into the morning star map directly onto the Venus cycle -- a ruler who disappears and will return, just as Venus disappears and returns. The astronomy and the mythology are one.

Alfredo Lopez Austin has argued that the Ce Acatl Topiltzin narrative belongs to a category he calls "mythic history" -- stories that use historical events as the framework for cosmological narratives, or cosmological narratives as the framework for historical events. The two registers -- the human-political and the divine-cosmological -- are not separate in Mesoamerican thought. A ruler who takes the name of a god is, in some meaningful sense, that god. A political overthrow that mirrors a cosmic struggle is, in some meaningful sense, that cosmic struggle. The question "Was Ce Acatl Topiltzin a real person?" may be the wrong question -- or at least a question that Mesoamerican thinkers would not have understood, because the categories it depends on (real versus mythological, human versus divine) were not categories they recognized.

There is another possibility: Ce Acatl Topiltzin may be a composite figure, a narrative constructed from the lives and legends of multiple people across multiple centuries. The name Quetzalcoatl was likely borne by more than one Toltec ruler -- it was a title as well as a name, a priestly office as well as a personal identity. Different communities may have had different Quetzalcoatls, and the stories told about them may have merged over time into a single narrative. The *Annals of Cuauhtitlan* appears to describe events spanning decades or centuries but attributes them all to a single life, which is possible only if the narrative has telescoped multiple generations into one. Nigel Davies devoted considerable attention to this problem, and concluded that the sources contain traces of at least two or three distinct figures whose stories have been combined -- a process that makes the historical kernel even harder to extract, because it is not a single kernel but a mixture.

The story of the conflict between Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca may also encode a real historical tension in Mesoamerican religious politics. The Feathered Serpent cult emphasized priestly learning, astronomical observation, and relatively restrained forms of sacrifice. The Tezcatlipoca cult emphasized warfare, human sacrifice on a large scale, and military achievement. These two religious-political orientations coexisted and competed throughout Mesoamerican history, and the Ce Acatl Topiltzin narrative -- the priestly leader overthrown by the warrior faction -- may be a mythologized version of a conflict that recurred, in various forms, at many Mesoamerican cities over many centuries. The story is "true" not because it describes one specific event at one specific time, but because it describes a recurring pattern in Mesoamerican political life.


## Chapter 5 — The Return That Changed Everything

Ce Acatl Topiltzin's departure included a promise -- or what later generations interpreted as a promise. He would return. He would come back from the east, reclaim his throne, and restore the golden age. The prophecy was tied to the calendar: his return would occur in a year bearing the day-sign One Reed, his birth name, which recurred every fifty-two years in the Mesoamerican calendar cycle.

The year 1519, when Hernan Cortes (ehr-NAHN kor-TEHS) landed on the Gulf Coast of Mexico, was a One Reed year.

According to the traditional account -- repeated in countless history books, dramatized in films and novels, taught in schools around the world -- the Aztec emperor Moctezuma II (mohk-teh-SOO-mah) believed that Cortes was the returning Quetzalcoatl. The bearded strangers arriving from the east, in a One Reed year, fulfilling the ancient prophecy -- Moctezuma was paralyzed by religious awe, unable to resist the invaders because he believed them to be gods or agents of a returning god. His hesitation gave the Spanish the opening they needed to establish themselves, gather indigenous allies, and ultimately conquer the Aztec empire.

It is one of the most famous stories in the history of the Americas. It is also, almost certainly, wrong -- or at minimum, far more complicated than the traditional telling suggests.

The story of Moctezuma's paralysis before the returning god comes primarily from Spanish and post-conquest indigenous sources, all written after the conquest was complete. The most influential account is Bernardino de Sahagun's *Florentine Codex*, which describes Moctezuma's reaction to the Spanish arrival as one of terror, despair, and resignation. Sahagun's informants -- Aztec elders speaking decades after the conquest -- described a ruler who wept, who tried to flee, who sent sorcerers to stop the Spanish with magic, and who ultimately surrendered to what he believed was divine fate.

But the *Florentine Codex* was compiled in the 1550s-1570s, decades after the events it describes. Sahagun's informants were speaking in a world already transformed by the conquest -- a world in which the Spanish had won, in which indigenous people were subject to colonial rule, and in which explaining why the mighty Aztec empire had fallen so quickly was both a political necessity and a psychological need. The story of the returning god provided an explanation that preserved Aztec dignity: Moctezuma did not lose because he was weak or foolish, but because he was pious -- he submitted to divine authority, not to human conquerors.

The historian Camilla Townsend, in her 2003 article "Burying the White Gods" and her 2006 book *Malintzin's Choices*, mounted the most influential modern challenge to the traditional narrative. Townsend argued that the story of Moctezuma believing Cortes was Quetzalcoatl was largely a post-conquest invention -- a narrative that served the interests of both the Spanish (who wanted to portray their conquest as divinely ordained) and the defeated Aztecs (who wanted to explain their loss as the result of cosmic forces rather than military failure).

Townsend pointed out several problems with the traditional account. First, the earliest Spanish sources -- the letters of Cortes himself, the account of Bernal Diaz del Castillo (deh-AHS dehl kahs-TEE-yoh) -- do not describe Moctezuma as believing the Spanish were gods. Cortes describes Moctezuma as a sophisticated political actor who negotiated, offered gifts, and tried to manage the Spanish presence through diplomacy. The "returning god" narrative appears only in later sources, after the conquest was complete and the story had been shaped by decades of colonial interpretation.

Second, Moctezuma's actual behavior -- as described even in the sources that promote the Quetzalcoatl narrative -- does not look like the behavior of a man who believes he is facing a god. He sent spies to gather intelligence on the Spanish. He attempted to bribe them to go away. He tried to prevent them from entering Tenochtitlan. He received them with elaborate diplomatic ceremony. These are the actions of a ruler confronting a political threat, not a priest confronting a divine visitation.

Third, the prophecy itself -- the return of Quetzalcoatl in a One Reed year -- may not have existed in the form the post-conquest sources describe. The One Reed calendar date was associated with Quetzalcoatl, and the concept of cyclical return is embedded in Mesoamerican cosmology. But a specific, widely known prophecy predicting the return of a bearded figure from the east, arriving precisely in 1519 -- this may be a retrospective construction, a prophecy created after the event it supposedly predicted, shaped to explain a catastrophe that seemed otherwise inexplicable.

The debate continues. Some scholars argue that while the specific details of the traditional narrative are post-conquest elaborations, there was a genuine cultural tradition connecting Quetzalcoatl to the east and to the possibility of return, and that this tradition influenced how the Aztecs interpreted the arrival of the Spanish -- not as a simple equation of Cortes with a god, but as an ominous event requiring interpretation within a cosmological framework. Others argue that the entire Quetzalcoatl-return narrative is a colonial-era invention, produced by the collision of Spanish Christian ideas about prophecy and providence with Mesoamerican ideas about cosmic cycles.

What is clear is that the story, whether or not it reflects pre-conquest beliefs, had enormous consequences. The narrative of Moctezuma submitting to Cortes because of the Quetzalcoatl prophecy became one of the foundational stories of European colonialism -- a story in which the conquest of the Americas was divinely ordained, foretold by the victims' own mythology. It was used to justify colonization. It was used to portray indigenous people as passive, superstitious, and incapable of rational political action. It was used to obscure the actual reasons for the Aztec defeat -- disease, indigenous alliances against the Aztec empire, superior weaponry, and the strategic genius of figures like Malintzin (mah-LEEN-tseen), the indigenous woman who served as translator and advisor to Cortes.

The actual reasons for the Aztec defeat were military and epidemiological, not mythological. Smallpox, introduced by a member of the Spanish expedition, devastated Tenochtitlan during the siege of 1520-1521, killing an estimated 40 percent of the city's population -- including Moctezuma's successor, Cuitlahuac (kweet-LAH-wahk), who ruled for only eighty days before dying of the disease. The Spanish allied with indigenous groups -- particularly the Tlaxcalteca (tlahsh-kahl-TEH-kah), long-standing enemies of the Aztec empire -- who provided tens of thousands of warriors for the final assault on Tenochtitlan. The conquest was achieved not by a handful of Spaniards overawing a superstitious emperor, but by a devastating combination of epidemic disease, indigenous alliance politics, and European military technology. The Quetzalcoatl narrative obscured all of this, replacing a complex military and political history with a simple story of prophecy fulfilled.

Malintzin -- also known as Dona Marina (DOH-nyah mah-REE-nah) or La Malinche (lah mah-LEEN-cheh) -- played a role in the conquest as consequential as any military figure's. A Nahua woman who had been sold into slavery among the Maya, she spoke both Nahuatl and Chontal Maya, and quickly learned Spanish. She served as Cortes's translator, advisor, and intermediary with indigenous groups. Without her linguistic skills and cultural knowledge, the Spanish would have been unable to negotiate the complex indigenous alliances that made the conquest possible. Her name became, in Mexican culture, a byword for betrayal -- *malinchismo* -- though recent scholarship has emphasized that her choices were made within constraints that left her few alternatives.

A myth about history became history itself. Whether or not Moctezuma believed Cortes was Quetzalcoatl, the story that he did shaped how Europeans understood the conquest for five hundred years. The Toltecs -- the phantom civilization whose glorification by the Aztecs created the Quetzalcoatl tradition in the form we know it -- are thus implicated in one of the most consequential misunderstandings in world history. A political mythology invented to legitimize Aztec rule was reinterpreted by European colonizers as evidence of divine providence, and used to justify the destruction of the very civilization that created it.

The irony is complete. The Aztecs invented the Toltec golden age to justify their own power. The Spanish used the Aztec invention to justify the destruction of Aztec power. The Toltecs, who may or may not have resembled their legend, became instruments in two successive projects of political legitimization -- first Aztec, then Spanish -- neither of which served the Toltecs themselves.

The Quetzalcoatl tradition also shaped how post-independence Mexico understood its own history. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Mexican intellectuals and artists appropriated Quetzalcoatl as a symbol of indigenous civilization -- a figure of wisdom and creativity, opposed to the violence and superstition that Europeans attributed to Mesoamerican cultures. The muralist Diego Rivera (dee-EH-goh ree-VEH-rah) depicted Quetzalcoatl in his monumental paintings at the National Palace in Mexico City as a figure of light and progress, and the Feathered Serpent became a recurring motif in Mexican national art. The political uses of the Toltec past did not end with the conquest; they continued, in new forms, into the modern era. Every generation remakes the Toltecs in its own image.
