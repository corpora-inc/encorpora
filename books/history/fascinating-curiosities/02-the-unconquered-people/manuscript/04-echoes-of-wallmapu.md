# Part Four: Echoes of Wallmapu

## Chapter 14 — The Mapuche Today

The Occupation of Araucanía ended the military contest. It did not end the Mapuche.

After 1883, the Chilean state confined the Mapuche population to *reducciones* — small reservations granted through *títulos de merced* (land titles). Between 1884 and 1929, approximately 3,000 of these titles were issued, covering roughly 475,000 hectares — a fraction of the territory the Mapuche had held. The reducciones were deliberately small, designed to transform the Mapuche from autonomous farmers and pastoralists into a dependent rural workforce. Communities that had managed vast territories were compressed into parcels too small for traditional agriculture, surrounded by Chilean and European settler farms that now occupied their ancestral land.

The twentieth century brought wave after wave of upheaval. In the 1920s and 1930s, Mapuche political organizations emerged — among the most important was the Corporación Araucana, led by Venancio Coñoepán, which sought to advance Mapuche rights through engagement with the Chilean political system. Mapuche leaders were elected to the Chilean Congress for the first time.

The presidency of Salvador Allende (1970-1973) brought the most significant land reform since the Occupation. Under Allende's agrarian reform laws, approximately 100,000 hectares of land were returned to Mapuche communities. For the first time in nearly a century, the trajectory of dispossession was partially reversed.

This brief period of restitution coincided with a broader awakening of Mapuche political consciousness. Mapuche organizations participated actively in Allende's coalition, and for the first time in the modern era, the Mapuche land question was treated as a matter of national justice rather than a local problem to be managed.

The military coup of September 11, 1973, and the seventeen-year dictatorship of Augusto Pinochet reversed everything. Pinochet's government implemented Decree Law 2568 (1979), which divided communal Mapuche land into individual private parcels — a policy designed to eliminate collective indigenous land ownership entirely. Communal titles were dissolved. Individual Mapuche families received small private plots. The legal category of "indigenous community" was effectively abolished. The land that had been returned under Allende was confiscated and redistributed to forestry companies and large agricultural enterprises.

The effects of this policy were profound and intentional. By dissolving communal land ownership, the Pinochet regime severed the legal connection between the Mapuche and their territory — the connection that had defined Mapuche identity for centuries. The word *Mapuche* means "people of the land." Take the land, and you attack the identity.

The forestry industry moved in. Pine and eucalyptus plantations — monocultures grown for export — replaced native forests across vast stretches of former Mapuche territory. These plantations drain water tables, acidify soil, and support almost no wildlife. They are the ecological opposite of the araucaria forests that had sustained the Pewenche for millennia. Where ancient, diverse forests had stood, identical rows of fast-growing foreign trees now marched to the horizon.

The return to democracy in 1990 opened new possibilities. The Indigenous Law of 1993 (Law 19,253) recognized indigenous peoples in Chile for the first time and created CONADI (Corporación Nacional de Desarrollo Indígena), a state agency for indigenous affairs. Chile ratified ILO Convention 169 on indigenous rights in 2008. Land purchase programs began transferring some properties back to Mapuche communities, though the pace has been slow and the demand far exceeds the supply.

The modern Mapuche movement is multifaceted. Some organizations pursue land claims and cultural rights through legal and political channels. Others have engaged in direct action — land occupations (*tomas*), blockades of logging roads, and confrontations with forestry companies and police in the Araucanía region. The Chilean state has responded with heavy policing, surveillance, and the application of anti-terrorism laws originally enacted under Pinochet. The conflict in the Araucanía — sometimes called the "new Arauco War" by Chilean media — is ongoing and politically divisive.

In 2019, a massive social uprising swept Chile. Millions took to the streets demanding a new constitution, economic reform, and social justice. The Mapuche flag — the *Wenufoye* (weh-noo-FOH-yeh) — was everywhere. Created in 1992 by the Consejo de Todas las Tierras, a Mapuche organization, during counter-celebrations of the 500th anniversary of Columbus's arrival, the flag features the *kultrun* (ceremonial drum) at its center against bands of blue, green, and red. Its name means "canelo of the sky" — the *foye* (cinnamon tree, *Drimys winteri*) being the most sacred tree in Mapuche culture.

The 2019 uprising — called the *estallido social*, the "social explosion" — was triggered by a subway fare increase in Santiago but quickly grew into a nationwide reckoning with inequality, privatized education and healthcare, and the legacy of the Pinochet-era constitution. Millions marched. In the central plaza of Santiago — renamed "Plaza de la Dignidad" by the protesters — the Wenufoye flew alongside Chilean flags, feminist banners, and hand-painted signs. It became the most recognizable symbol of a movement that extended far beyond indigenous rights.

The Wenufoye's adoption as a symbol of Chile's broader social movements is remarkable. A flag created by an indigenous people to assert their distinct identity was embraced by millions of non-indigenous Chileans as a symbol of resistance to injustice. The Mapuche, who had fought Spain and Chile for centuries, found their banner carried by the very society that had dispossessed them.

The constitutional process failed at the ballot box. But the conversation it opened — about what kind of country Chile is, about who belongs, about whether a single national identity can encompass the diversity of peoples within its borders — has not closed. The question of Mapuche self-determination within the Chilean state remains the most unresolved political issue in modern Chilean society.

Today, approximately 1.74 million people in Chile identify as Mapuche — about 10 percent of the national population. In Argentina, roughly 200,000 more identify as Mapuche. They are the largest indigenous group in Chile by a wide margin, and their political and cultural influence is greater now than at any point since the Occupation.

The questions that animate the Mapuche movement today are the same questions that have animated it for five centuries: Who owns the land? Who decides how it is used? What does sovereignty mean for a people who never had a state and never wanted one? And can a way of life rooted in a specific relationship between people and territory survive when 95 percent of that territory has been taken?

The constitutional convention of 2021-2022 — the first such process in Chilean history to include reserved seats for indigenous peoples — offered another possibility. Mapuche delegates participated directly in drafting a new constitution that would have recognized Chile as a plurinational state and guaranteed indigenous rights to territory, language, and self-governance. The proposed constitution was rejected by Chilean voters in a September 2022 referendum, but the process itself — indigenous delegates sitting as equals in a body charged with defining the fundamental law of the state — marked a break from three centuries of exclusion.

These are open questions. The Mapuche are still answering them.


## Chapter 15 — What Was Happening Elsewhere

The Mapuche story unfolds across five centuries, and it helps to see it alongside everything else that was happening in the world during that time.

The Mapuche story unfolds alongside everything else that was happening in the world. Seeing the two timelines together reveals just how long this resistance lasted and how central it was to the history of the Americas.

When the Inca reached the Maule River and stopped, sometime in the 1470s or 1480s, Leonardo da Vinci was painting in Florence. The Gutenberg press had been operating in Mainz for barely thirty years. The Ottoman Empire had just conquered Constantinople. Columbus had yet to sail.

When Pedro de Valdivia founded Santiago in 1541, Henry VIII was king of England, Michelangelo was painting the Last Judgment in the Sistine Chapel, and Copernicus was completing his manuscript arguing that the Earth orbits the Sun. In Japan, the first Portuguese traders had arrived just two years earlier, introducing firearms. In the Ottoman Empire, Suleiman the Magnificent was at the height of his power. The world was entering the age of global connection — and in southern Chile, a people who would resist that connection for three centuries were about to meet it face to face.

When Lautaro defeated Valdivia at the Battle of Tucapel in 1553, Elizabeth I was twenty years old and five years from becoming queen. The Council of Trent was redefining Catholic doctrine. Ivan the Terrible was consolidating power in Russia.

When the Mapuche destroyed the Seven Cities in 1598-1604, Shakespeare was writing *Hamlet* and *Othello*. The Dutch East India Company was being founded. Galileo was building his first telescope. In China, the Ming dynasty was entering its final decline. Across the world, the early modern period was reshaping every continent — and in southern Chile, a decentralized people with no writing system had just inflicted one of the most devastating defeats any European power had ever suffered in the Americas.

When the Treaty of Quilín was signed in 1641, the English Civil War was raging. The Tokugawa shogunate had sealed Japan from most foreign contact. Descartes had just published his *Meditations on First Philosophy*. Louis XIV was three years old. In North America, the English colonies at Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay were barely two decades old.

The parlamento system persisted through the Enlightenment. While Voltaire and Rousseau were writing about the social contract and natural liberty, the Mapuche were practicing a form of consensual governance that embodied those principles more fully than any European state of the period. While the American colonists were declaring independence from Britain in 1776, citing the right of peoples to govern themselves, the Mapuche were negotiating their forty-third parlamento with the Spanish governor — having exercised that right continuously for over two centuries.

When Chile declared independence from Spain in 1818, the Mapuche initially benefited. The new republic inherited the treaty system, and for decades the Bío-Bío frontier remained intact. The Mapuche traded with the new Chilean state, maintained diplomatic relations, and continued to govern themselves as they had for centuries. Some Mapuche leaders allied with Chilean independence forces, seeing an opportunity to maintain their autonomy under a weaker successor state. Others remained wary, correctly suspecting that Chilean independence would eventually prove more dangerous to Mapuche sovereignty than Spanish colonialism had been.

When the Occupation of Araucanía began in 1861, the American Civil War was underway, and for remarkably similar reasons — a modernizing nation-state asserting control over territory it claimed but did not fully govern, using industrial technology to overwhelm indigenous resistance. Darwin's *Origin of Species* had just been published. The Suez Canal was under construction. And when the Occupation ended in 1883, the Scramble for Africa was beginning, the Brooklyn Bridge had just opened, and the telephone was seven years old. The world was being wired together, and the spaces where autonomous peoples could exist outside the reach of nation-states were closing everywhere, simultaneously, on every continent.

The Mapuche resistance lasted from the 1550s to the 1880s — roughly 330 years. That is longer than the Roman Republic. It lasted longer than the Ottoman Empire's European expansion. It outlasted the entire period from the founding of Jamestown to the end of the American Civil War.

This is a story that runs parallel to the central narratives of world history. It is contemporary with the Renaissance, the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, the Enlightenment, the Industrial Revolution, and the age of European imperialism. It happened in real time, alongside everything else, and it challenges the assumption that the spread of European power across the globe was inevitable or irresistible. For three hundred years, in one corner of South America, it was resisted — successfully — by a people who organized their world on principles so different from European norms that the Europeans could barely comprehend what they were fighting.

Consider the span of it. When the resistance began, the printing press was new. When the resistance ended, the telephone existed. The Mapuche fought on horseback against an empire that arrived in sailing ships, and they were still fighting when that empire's successor deployed telegraph lines and railroads. They adapted to every innovation of the early modern period — gunpowder, cavalry, European diplomacy — and held their ground until the industrial age made their kind of warfare impossible.

Few stories in human history demonstrate so clearly that the shape of a society is a choice, that hierarchy and centralization are strategies rather than inevitabilities, and that a people's strength can reside in the very things an empire perceives as weakness. The Mapuche had no king to surrender because they chose to govern themselves differently. They had no capital to capture because they chose to distribute their power across the land rather than concentrate it in a single place. They had no written records because they chose to carry their knowledge in living voices rather than in documents that could be burned.

These were choices. And for three hundred years, they worked.

The Mapuche story is part of world history. It deserves to be told as such.
