# Part Three: The Invisible State

## Chapter 10 — How to Win a War Without an Army

Lautaro's death in 1557 was supposed to end the Mapuche threat. The Spanish assumed that the rebellion, like those they had crushed in Mexico, Peru, and the Caribbean, would collapse once its charismatic leader was eliminated. They assumed that because they had always operated in societies where power concentrated in individuals — remove the individual, remove the power.

The Mapuche kept fighting.

In the four decades after Lautaro's death, the Arauco War settled into a grinding frontier conflict. The Spanish rebuilt their southern cities. They garrisoned forts along the Bío-Bío River. They launched periodic expeditions south into Mapuche territory, burning crops, taking captives, and attempting to establish permanent control. The Mapuche raided north, attacking settlements, capturing horses and weapons, freeing enslaved workers from the encomiendas. The frontier became a zone of perpetual, low-intensity warfare punctuated by moments of catastrophic violence.

Then, in 1598, the Mapuche ended the debate about who controlled the south.

The toqui Pelantaru (peh-lahn-TAH-roo) organized a coordinated uprising across the entire Mapuche territory. The planning was meticulous and secret. Multiple ayllarewe synchronized their attacks — a feat of coordination that the Spanish believed impossible for a people with no written communication, no centralized command, and no professional officer corps.

On December 23, 1598, Mapuche forces ambushed and destroyed a Spanish column at the Battle of Curalaba (koo-rah-LAH-bah), near the modern city of Lumaco. The Spanish governor Martín García Óñez de Loyola — a nephew by marriage of the Jesuit founder Ignatius of Loyola — was killed in the fighting, along with nearly his entire force.

Curalaba was the signal. Over the following months, the Mapuche systematically attacked and destroyed every Spanish settlement south of the Bío-Bío. La Imperial. Villarrica. Valdivia. Angol. Osorno. Santa Cruz. Arauco. Seven cities, razed to the ground. Their populations — those who were not killed — fled north to Concepción and Santiago, or were taken captive by the Mapuche. The entire Spanish colonial infrastructure south of the Bío-Bío was obliterated in a single coordinated campaign.

This event — known as the Destruction of the Seven Cities — remains one of the most devastating military defeats suffered by any European colonial power anywhere in the Americas. Spain's losses in soldiers, settlers, and resources during the Arauco War exceeded its losses in the entire conquest of the Aztec and Inca empires. A professional colonial army, backed by the wealth and logistics of the largest empire on Earth, was driven from a territory larger than Portugal by a decentralized collection of farming communities with no formal military organization.

![The burning of a Spanish colonial settlement south of the Bío-Bío](../images/placeholders/07-destruction-seven-cities.png)

The aftermath was devastating for Spanish Chile. Thousands of settlers — men, women, and children — were killed or taken captive. Those taken captive by the Mapuche often lived for years or decades among their captors, and some chose to stay permanently. Spanish women captured in the destruction married Mapuche men and raised Mapuche children. Spanish soldiers taken prisoner taught their captors metalworking, tactics, and horsemanship. The frontier became a zone of involuntary cultural exchange that permanently altered both societies.

The financial cost was staggering. The Spanish Crown was forced to establish a permanent military subsidy — the *real situado* — to maintain an army on the Bío-Bío frontier. This annual payment, drawn from the silver mines of Peru, made the Arauco War one of the most expensive military commitments in the entire Spanish Empire. Chile, which had been expected to generate wealth like Peru and Mexico, instead became a drain on imperial resources — a colony that cost more to defend than it produced.

![A battle scene at Tucapel with relay attack tactics](../images/placeholders/06-battle-of-tucapel.png)

How? The answer lies in the structure of Mapuche society itself.

A centralized state can be defeated by capturing its center. An empire can be dismantled by seizing its capital, killing its ruler, and redirecting its bureaucracy. The Spanish had perfected this technique. But the Mapuche had no center to capture. Every community was autonomous. Every lonko led by consent. Every toqui served for the duration of a campaign and then returned to being a farmer. There was no permanent military infrastructure to destroy, no chain of command to sever, no single point whose loss would cascade into general collapse.

The Spanish found themselves in the position of trying to extinguish a fire by stamping on sparks. They could defeat a particular Mapuche force in a particular battle, and the overall war effort would barely notice. The warriors they defeated would disperse to their home communities, recover, rearm, and return — or be replaced by others from communities untouched by the recent fighting. The system regenerated faster than the Spanish could damage it.

The Mapuche also developed tactical innovations that specifically exploited Spanish weaknesses. The relay attack — fresh units rotating in as exhausted units withdrew — neutralized the advantage of Spanish heavy cavalry, which was devastating in short engagements but rapidly exhausted horses and riders. Mapuche warriors learned to fight in terrain that negated cavalry advantage: forests, ravines, riverbanks, and hillsides where horses could not charge in formation. They built temporary fortifications — palisades and earthworks — at sites chosen for their tactical advantage, fought from them until the defenses were breached, then withdrew to the next prepared position.

The intelligence systems were sophisticated. The Mapuche maintained networks of scouts, informants, and messengers across their territory. Information about Spanish troop movements traveled through the kinship network faster than a Spanish courier could ride. Captured Spanish soldiers and civilian settlers — some held for years, some permanently absorbed into Mapuche communities — provided detailed intelligence about Spanish capabilities, internal politics, and strategic intentions. By the seventeenth century, Mapuche leaders understood the internal workings of the Spanish colonial system better than most Spanish officials understood Mapuche society.

They also adopted Spanish technology with extraordinary speed. By the early seventeenth century, many Mapuche warriors fought on horseback, using lances, and wearing captured Spanish armor. Some Mapuche communities manufactured their own metal weapons using techniques learned from Spanish prisoners and deserters. The Mapuche became, in effect, a mirror of the Spanish military — cavalry-based, lance-armed, and tactically sophisticated — while retaining the distributed organizational structure that made them impossible to permanently defeat.


## Chapter 11 — The Parliament System

After the Destruction of the Seven Cities, the Spanish colonial administration in Chile faced a choice: continue an unwinnable war at ruinous expense, or negotiate.

They negotiated.

The result was one of the most extraordinary diplomatic institutions in colonial American history: the *parlamento* system.

The parlamento grew from a Mapuche institution called the *koyagtun* (koh-yag-TOON) — a deliberative assembly where leaders gathered to discuss matters of collective concern. The koyagtun was not a parliament in the European sense. There was no voting, no majority rule, no formal legislation. It was a process of extended deliberation — speeches, proposals, responses, counter-proposals — that continued until consensus emerged or failed to emerge. The goal was agreement, and agreement meant that every participating leader was willing to support the outcome. If a lonko disagreed, he simply did not participate, and his community was not bound by the result.

When the Spanish governors began seeking peace treaties after the disaster of 1598, they entered into this indigenous diplomatic framework. The resulting parlamentos combined Mapuche and Spanish diplomatic forms into a hybrid institution conducted according to Mapuche protocols, on Mapuche territory, in the Mapuche language.

The Parlamento de Quilín in January 1641 was the most significant. Over two thousand Mapuche leaders — lonkos and toquis from across the territory — gathered at Quilín, deep in Mapuche-controlled land south of the Bío-Bío. The Spanish Governor, the Marqués de Baides, traveled south to meet them, bringing gifts, a military escort, and interpreters.

![A large outdoor parlamento assembly with Spanish officials approaching](../images/placeholders/08-parlamento-assembly.png)

The protocols were elaborate and deeply symbolic. The Spanish delegation approached bearing branches of the *foye* (FOH-yeh), the cinnamon tree (*Drimys winteri*) that was sacred in Mapuche culture — a gesture of peace that the Mapuche required as a precondition for talks. Speeches were delivered in Mapudungun and translated into Spanish. Llamas were sacrificed. Rituals of mutual recognition were performed. The entire event lasted several days.

The Treaty of Quilín, which emerged from this parlamento, was extraordinary in its terms. Spain formally recognized the Bío-Bío River as the boundary between Spanish and Mapuche territory. South of the Bío-Bío was Mapuche land, and the Mapuche were acknowledged as its sovereign inhabitants. The treaty established terms for trade, the return of prisoners, and the treatment of missionaries (who were allowed to cross the border under Mapuche supervision). In exchange, the Mapuche agreed to peaceful coexistence with the Spanish north of the river.

This was the only time in the history of the Spanish Empire in the Americas that Spain signed a treaty recognizing an indigenous people as sovereign equals.

The significance of this requires emphasis. The Spanish Empire, at its height, controlled territory from California to Patagonia, from the Philippines to the Netherlands. It was the first truly global empire in human history. Its armies had conquered civilizations with populations in the tens of millions. Its missionaries had baptized entire continents. Its legal system claimed sovereignty over every inch of land its explorers had sighted. And at Quilín, the representative of this empire sat in a field in southern Chile, under the rules of people who could neither read nor write, and acknowledged in a formal treaty that there was a territory he could not claim and a people he could not govern. The Aztecs had been conquered. The Incas had been conquered. The Maya, the Muisca, the Guaraní, the Taíno, the countless other peoples of the Americas — all had been subjected to Spanish rule by force. The Mapuche alone compelled Spain to sit down, cross into their territory, follow their protocols, speak their language, and sign a document acknowledging that they owned their land and governed themselves.

The parlamento system persisted for nearly two centuries after Quilín. Dozens of major parlamentos were held between the 1640s and the 1820s — at Negrete, Tapihue, Lonquilmo, and other sites along and south of the frontier. Each followed the same basic pattern: Mapuche leaders gathered in large numbers, Spanish (and later Chilean) officials traveled to meet them, gifts were exchanged, speeches were delivered, and agreements were reached on matters of trade, territorial boundaries, prisoner exchange, and mutual obligations.

The anthropologist Tom Dillehay and the historian José Manuel Zavala, in their collaborative research published in *The Hispanic-Mapuche Parlamentos* (2020), argue that the parlamento system represented a genuine form of interethnic geopolitics — a sustained diplomatic relationship between two polities that recognized each other's existence and negotiated their coexistence over generations. It was messy, imperfect, and frequently violated by both sides. But it persisted, and its persistence tells us something important about the nature of Mapuche political organization.

A people who could gather two thousand leaders for a diplomatic assembly, maintain a consistent negotiating position across decades, and enforce the terms of treaties through coordinated military response were practicing statecraft. They were simply practicing it through institutions that Europeans could not see — kinship networks rather than bureaucracies, oral consensus rather than written law, temporary authority rather than permanent offices.

The parlamento system also created something that both sides valued: predictability. Between parlamentos, the frontier was governed by agreed-upon norms — trade could flow at regulated points, missionaries could operate under Mapuche supervision, prisoners could be exchanged, and disputes could be resolved through negotiation rather than warfare. This was imperfect. Both sides violated the agreements regularly. Spanish settlers encroached on Mapuche land. Mapuche warriors raided Spanish settlements. But the parlamento system provided a mechanism for managing these violations without escalation to total war — a diplomatic safety valve that benefited both sides.


## Chapter 12 — A Nation Without a Capital

The question that Western political theory struggles with is this: how do you coordinate collective action at scale without hierarchy?

Empires, kingdoms, and modern nation-states all solve this problem the same way — through centralized authority. A king issues commands. A bureaucracy implements them. A military enforces them. A written legal code defines the rules. This model is so dominant that Western political theory often treats it as the only possibility, as if hierarchy were a law of nature rather than a design choice.

The Mapuche offer a different answer.

The *lonko* (LONG-koh) — the leader of a lof, the basic kinship group — was a chief whose authority derived from prestige. A lonko was typically an older man known for his skill in oratory, his generosity, his judgment, and his knowledge of *ad mapu* (ahd MAH-poo) — the customary law that governed Mapuche social life. A lonko led because people listened. His power resided in his ability to persuade, to mediate disputes, to distribute resources fairly, and to articulate the consensus of his community. If he failed at these things, people stopped listening, and his authority evaporated.

The *toqui* (TOH-kee) — the war chief — was elected for a specific campaign. His authority was real but temporary. During wartime, a toqui could command warriors from multiple communities, coordinate strategy, and make tactical decisions. When the campaign ended, so did his command. He returned to his fields. There was no peacetime military establishment, no officer corps, no barracks, no military budget. The entire war-making apparatus materialized when needed and dissolved when the need passed.

The *machi* (mah-CHEE) — the spiritual specialist — held a form of authority that existed outside the political domain entirely. A machi, almost always a woman, was called to her role through dreams, illness, and spiritual experience. Her authority rested on her relationship with the spirit world, her ability to heal, and her knowledge of the ceremonial practices that maintained cosmic balance. A machi's judgment on matters of spiritual significance carried binding weight — she could declare a proposed action spiritually dangerous, and even a powerful lonko would defer to her assessment. In a system with no written laws and no courts, the machi served as a living connection to the normative order of the cosmos.

![A lonko standing and speaking to a seated community assembly](../images/placeholders/11-lonko-speaking.png)

The *ad mapu* — customary law — governed everything from marriage and inheritance to conflict resolution and resource management. It was oral, transmitted through practice and instruction, and it varied in detail from community to community while sharing common principles across the Mapuche world. Disputes were resolved through mediation rather than adjudication — a lonko or a council of elders would hear both sides and seek a resolution that restored balance, rather than declaring a winner and a loser. Serious offenses — murder, theft, violations of reciprocity — were handled through a system of compensation and restitution that aimed to repair the social fabric rather than punish the offender.

The interplay between these three sources of authority — the lonko's political prestige, the toqui's military command, and the machi's spiritual judgment — created a system of checks and balances that lacked any formal constitutional design. Authority was distributed across domains. A lonko could not override a machi's spiritual assessment. A toqui could not sustain a war that lonkos refused to support. A machi could not direct political affairs. Each form of authority operated in its own sphere, and the boundaries between spheres were maintained by custom, by social expectation, and by the practical reality that no single person could accumulate enough power to override the others.

What made this system work at scale was the layered structure of alliance. The lof was self-governing. The rewe — a group of allied lof — coordinated on ceremonial and defensive matters. The ayllarewe — a confederation of nine rewe — could mobilize for larger efforts. And in times of crisis, ayllarewe from across the territory could come together under a toqui for coordinated military action. Each layer was voluntary. Each layer preserved the autonomy of the units within it. The system could expand to mobilize tens of thousands of warriors for a campaign like the Destruction of the Seven Cities, and then contract back to the level of individual farming families living their daily lives.

Comparisons are instructive. The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy in northeastern North America achieved something similar — a political alliance of autonomous nations that coordinated collective action through consensus rather than coercion. The Swiss Confederation began as a voluntary alliance of independent cantons that gradually developed federal institutions while preserving cantonal autonomy. Athenian democracy, in its original form, distributed political authority among all adult male citizens rather than concentrating it in a ruler or ruling class.

Each of these examples demonstrates that large-scale political coordination is possible without centralized authority. What the Mapuche case adds is a demonstration that such a system can function under extreme military pressure — under sustained assault from the most powerful military forces in the world — for over three centuries. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy lasted roughly three centuries before being overwhelmed by European colonization. The Mapuche alliance lasted as long, under arguably greater pressure, and against a more powerful adversary.

The lesson the Mapuche offer political theory is this: the absence of a state is not the absence of governance. The absence of a king is not the absence of leadership. The absence of a written constitution is not the absence of law. There are other ways to organize collective human life, and some of them are remarkably durable.


## Chapter 13 — The Fall

The Spanish Empire could not conquer the Mapuche. The Chilean Republic did.

Chile declared independence from Spain in 1818. For the first four decades of the republic's existence, the Bío-Bío frontier held. The new state inherited the treaty system, the parlamento tradition, and a pragmatic recognition that the territory south of the river was Mapuche land. Chilean settlers, merchants, and missionaries operated on the frontier, and small-scale conflicts continued, but the basic arrangement — Mapuche sovereignty south of the Bío-Bío, Chilean sovereignty north of it — remained intact.

What changed in the 1860s was a combination of ideology, economics, and technology.

The ideology was "civilization versus barbarism" — the dominant framework of nineteenth-century Latin American nation-building, which held that the mixed-race, Spanish-speaking, Catholic populations of the new republics were "civilized" and that indigenous peoples were obstacles to progress. This framework was imported directly from European colonial thought and reinforced by contemporary developments in North America, where the United States was waging its own campaigns of westward expansion and indigenous dispossession.

The economics were straightforward. Southern Chile — the Mapuche territory — contained some of the richest agricultural land on the continent. European immigrants, particularly German settlers, were already establishing farms in the frontier zones. Chilean elites wanted that land, and the Mapuche were on it.

The technology was decisive. The telegraph allowed the Chilean military to coordinate operations across hundreds of kilometers in real time. The railroad could move troops and supplies faster than Mapuche warriors could respond. The repeating rifle gave Chilean soldiers a rate of fire that overwhelmed the tactics the Mapuche had developed against single-shot muskets and cavalry charges. These were the same technologies that were simultaneously transforming warfare everywhere — in the American West, in southern Africa, in the European colonial empires — and in every case, they tipped the balance against indigenous resistance.

![A Chilean military fort under construction on a hilltop](../images/placeholders/12-occupation-araucania-fort.png)

In 1861, General Cornelio Saavedra proposed a plan for the "Pacification of Araucanía" — a euphemism for military conquest. The plan was straightforward: build a line of forts across the frontier, advance the line southward year by year, and occupy the territory systematically. It was modeled explicitly on the United States Army's strategy of westward expansion — Chilean military officers studied American frontier doctrine and adapted it to their own geography.

The Occupation of Araucanía lasted from 1861 to 1883. It was a war, though the Chilean state preferred to call it a "pacification." The Mapuche resisted — they fought engagements at Temuco, Villarrica, and dozens of other sites — but the structural advantages that had served them for three centuries were neutralized. The telegraph eliminated the element of surprise. The railroad eliminated the logistical advantage of fighting on home territory. The repeating rifle eliminated the tactical viability of cavalry charges and massed infantry assaults.

And for the first time in three centuries, the Mapuche could be encircled. On the Chilean side, Saavedra's forts advanced southward. On the Argentine side, the simultaneous "Conquest of the Desert" (1878-1885) — Argentina's own campaign of indigenous dispossession — closed the eastern frontier. The Mapuche communities that had for centuries used the Andes as a corridor, trading and migrating between Chile and Argentina, found themselves caught between two advancing armies with no escape route.

The Mapuche resisted. They fought at Temuco, at Villarrica, at sites across the territory. Individual lonkos organized defenses. Toquis mobilized ayllarewe for counterattacks. Some communities fled east across the Andes, seeking refuge with kinship groups in Argentina — only to find that Argentina's own "Conquest of the Desert" under General Julio Argentino Roca was closing that escape route at the same time. For the first time in three centuries, the Mapuche faced coordinated military pressure from two directions simultaneously, with industrial technology that neutralized every advantage their decentralized system had provided.

The telegraph was perhaps the most devastating innovation. For three hundred years, Mapuche military success had depended on superior information — their kinship networks transmitted intelligence faster than Spanish couriers could ride. The telegraph reversed this advantage overnight. Chilean military commands could now coordinate operations across hundreds of kilometers in minutes. Troop movements could be synchronized. Ambushes could be anticipated. The information warfare that had been the Mapuche's greatest weapon was neutralized by copper wire and electrical signals.

By 1883, the military conquest was complete. The Mapuche population — roughly 150,000 people at the time — was confined to *reducciones*, reservations totaling approximately 475,000 hectares of land. Before the Occupation, the Mapuche had controlled an estimated 10 million hectares. They lost approximately 95 percent of their territory.

![A small reducción adjacent to vast parceled farmland](../images/placeholders/13-reduccion-landscape.png)

The land they lost was distributed to Chilean settlers, European immigrants, and agricultural companies. The communities that had sustained themselves for millennia on farming, gathering, and pastoralism were compressed into parcels too small for subsistence agriculture. Poverty, malnutrition, and social disruption followed immediately and have persisted, in varying degrees, ever since.

The Mapuche were defeated by the industrial age. For three centuries, they had adapted to every military innovation the Spanish could bring — horses, steel, gunpowder, cavalry tactics. They had absorbed European technology and turned it to their own advantage. But the telegraph, the railroad, and the repeating rifle arrived together, as parts of an interconnected industrial system, and they arrived simultaneously from two directions. The window of adaptation closed before the Mapuche could open it.

The reducciones were deliberately placed on the poorest land. The best agricultural land — the fertile valley bottoms, the irrigated fields, the forest margins — went to Chilean settlers and European immigrants, particularly Germans, Swiss, and French who arrived in the late nineteenth century under government colonization programs. The immigrants received generous land grants and financial support. The Mapuche, who had lived on this land for millennia, received the rocky hillsides, the eroded slopes, and the waterlogged lowlands that the settlers did not want.

It is worth stating plainly what was lost. A people who had governed themselves for centuries were placed under foreign rule. A territory the size of a European country was confiscated. A way of life — distributed, autonomous, rooted in a particular relationship between people and land — was dismantled. And the conditions that had sustained Mapudungun — territorial sovereignty, diplomatic prestige, practical necessity — were eliminated in a single generation.

The three-hundred-year resistance was over. What followed would require a different kind of endurance.
