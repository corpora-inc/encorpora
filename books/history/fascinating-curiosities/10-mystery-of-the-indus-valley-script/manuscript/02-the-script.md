# Part Two: The Script

## Chapter 4 — 4,000 Signs and Counting

The Indus Valley civilization left behind over four thousand inscribed objects. They are found at every major site, from Harappa in the north to Dholavira in the south, from the coastal town of Lothal (LOH-tahl) in Gujarat to the far-flung outpost of Shortugai in Afghanistan. The inscriptions appear on stone seals, on clay tablets, on pottery, on copper tools, on ivory rods, and on small objects of uncertain purpose. The inscriptions are everywhere. And no one can read them.

The most common inscribed objects are the stamp seals -- small, square or rectangular stones, typically made of steatite (STEE-ah-tyte, a soft stone also called soapstone), carved with an image and a short inscription. The seals are usually between two and five centimeters on a side -- small enough to hold in one hand. The back of each seal has a perforated boss (a raised knob with a hole through it), designed for threading a cord so the seal could be worn around the neck or attached to a belt. The front bears a carved image -- most commonly the so-called "unicorn" (a bull shown in profile, so that only one horn is visible), but also elephants, water buffalo, rhinoceroses, tigers, and composite creatures that combine features of different animals. Above or beside the animal image, a row of signs is carved in intaglio -- recessed into the stone, so that when the seal is pressed into soft clay, the signs appear in relief.

![Close-up of an Indus stamp seal with unicorn motif and script signs in steatite](../images/placeholders/04-indus-seal-closeup.png)

The seals were used for marking -- that much is clear. Impressions of Indus seals have been found on clay lumps that were used to seal bundles, containers, and doors. The system resembles the sealing practices of contemporary Mesopotamia, where cylinder seals were rolled across clay to mark ownership or authorize transactions. The Indus seals were likely used for similar purposes: identifying the owner of goods, authorizing trade transactions, or marking property. Some seals may have served as identity markers -- personal or institutional emblems that identified the bearer or the organization the bearer represented.

The number of distinct signs in the Indus script is a matter of ongoing debate. Different scholars, using different criteria for what counts as a separate sign versus a variant of the same sign, have arrived at different totals. The most widely cited concordance, compiled by the Indian epigrapher Iravatham Mahadevan (ee-RAH-vah-tahm mah-HAH-deh-vahn) in 1977, identified 417 distinct signs. Subsequent studies have proposed totals ranging from roughly 400 to over 600. The exact number matters because it provides a clue to the nature of the script.

Writing systems fall into several broad categories based on how many signs they use. Alphabetic scripts (like the Latin alphabet used for English) typically have twenty to forty signs, each representing a single consonant or vowel sound. Syllabic scripts (like Japanese kana) use fifty to one hundred signs, each representing a syllable. Logographic scripts (like Chinese) use thousands of signs, each representing a word or a meaningful unit of language. And logo-syllabic scripts (like Sumerian cuneiform or Egyptian hieroglyphics) combine logographic and syllabic elements, using several hundred to over a thousand signs.

The Indus script, with its four hundred to six hundred signs, fits most comfortably in the logo-syllabic range -- a script that combines signs representing words with signs representing sounds. This is the same category as the writing systems of the two contemporary civilizations, Egypt and Mesopotamia. The number of signs is too large for a pure alphabet or syllabary, and too small for a pure logographic system. If the sign count is accurate, the Indus script likely represents a writing system of the same general type as cuneiform and hieroglyphics -- a system that encodes a spoken language using a combination of word-signs and sound-signs.

But there is a complication. Some scholars have argued that the sign count is inflated -- that many signs counted as separate are actually variants of the same sign (rotated, simplified, or combined with diacritical marks). If the true number of distinct signs is closer to one hundred or two hundred, the script might be a syllabary rather than a logo-syllabic system. And if the number is lower still -- if many apparent "signs" are actually combinations of a smaller set of basic elements -- the script might be something else entirely.

The inscriptions themselves are short. This is the central, defining problem of Indus script studies. The average inscription contains four to five signs. The longest known inscription, found on a surface at Dholavira, contains approximately twenty-six signs -- but this is exceptionally long. The vast majority of inscriptions contain fewer than ten signs. Many contain only one, two, or three.

To appreciate the difficulty this creates, consider the Rosetta Stone, which allowed Jean-François Champollion (zhahn-frahn-SWAH shahm-pol-YON) to decipher Egyptian hieroglyphics in the 1820s. The Rosetta Stone contains roughly 1,400 hieroglyphic signs arranged in fourteen lines of text. Champollion could identify repeated words, track the frequency of individual signs, find patterns of beginning and ending, and compare the hieroglyphic text to the accompanying Greek translation. He had enough data to work with.

The Indus script offers nothing comparable. A four-sign inscription is a fragment -- like finding a torn scrap of paper with the letters "K-I-N-G" and trying to determine not only what it says but what language it is written in, what the letters represent, and whether it is a word, an abbreviation, a name, or a symbol. The brevity of the inscriptions means that the standard tools of decipherment -- frequency analysis, pattern recognition, contextual inference -- are severely limited. There is simply not enough text to establish reliable patterns.

The distribution of signs is also informative. Certain signs appear far more frequently than others. A small number of signs -- perhaps thirty to fifty -- account for the majority of all occurrences. Many signs appear only once or twice in the entire known corpus. This kind of distribution is consistent with a writing system (natural languages show similar frequency patterns, with common words appearing far more often than rare ones), but it is also consistent with a non-linguistic symbol system in which certain emblems or markers are widely used while others are specialized or local.

The signs themselves are pictographic in origin -- many appear to represent recognizable objects. There are signs that look like fish, jars, humans, plants, animals, and various tools or implements. There are also abstract signs -- lines, curves, strokes, and combinations of geometric elements that do not resemble any identifiable object. The pictographic signs were probably originally iconic (the fish sign represented a fish, the jar sign represented a jar), but in a developed writing system, pictographic signs typically evolve to represent sounds or abstract concepts rather than the objects they depict. The Egyptian sign for "mouth" came to represent the sound "r." The Sumerian sign for "foot" came to represent the concept of "going." Whether the Indus fish sign represents a fish, the word for fish, the sound of the word for fish, or something else entirely is unknown.

The direction of writing has been established with reasonable confidence. In most inscriptions, the signs were written from right to left. The evidence comes from several observations: the cramping of signs at the left end of inscriptions (as if the writer was running out of space, which happens at the end of a line), the greater spacing at the right end, and the occasional continuation of inscriptions onto a second line starting from the right. Right-to-left writing is shared by several ancient scripts, including early Semitic scripts (the ancestors of Hebrew and Arabic), suggesting a broad pattern in the development of early writing systems -- though the connection is chronological coincidence rather than evidence of direct relationship.

Iravatham Mahadevan spent decades cataloging and analyzing the signs. His 1977 concordance -- *The Indus Script: Texts, Concordance and Tables* -- remains the foundational reference work for the field. Mahadevan photographed, drew, and classified every known inscription, creating a systematic record that allowed other scholars to work with the corpus. His work was meticulous, exhaustive, and invaluable. He also proposed his own readings of the script, arguing that it encoded an early Dravidian language -- a hypothesis that we will examine in the next chapter.

The corpus of inscriptions continues to grow. New seals and inscribed objects are found in nearly every excavation season at Indus sites. Each new inscription adds to the database, potentially providing the pattern or the context that might unlock the script. But after a century of discovery, the fundamental obstacle remains: the inscriptions are too short. There is not enough text to work with. The silence of Harappa is measured not in the absence of inscriptions but in their brevity.


## Chapter 5 — The Decipherment Wars

The history of attempts to decipher the Indus script is a history of brilliant hypotheses, fierce disputes, and persistent failure. Over one hundred scholars have proposed readings of the script since the 1920s. None has achieved the scholarly consensus that would constitute a successful decipherment.

The first attempts came almost immediately after the discovery of the civilization. In the 1920s and 1930s, scholars proposed connections to various known scripts: Egyptian hieroglyphics, Sumerian cuneiform, the Hittite script, the Easter Island rongorongo script, and others. These early proposals were speculative and unsystematic, based on superficial visual resemblances between individual Indus signs and signs from other writing systems. They were quickly discarded.

The first serious, sustained attempt at decipherment came from a group of Finnish and Soviet scholars in the 1960s and 1970s. The Finnish team, led by Asko Parpola (AHS-koh PAR-poh-lah) at the University of Helsinki, and the Soviet team, based at the Institute of Ethnography in Moscow, independently applied computational methods to the script -- one of the earliest uses of computers in linguistic analysis. Both teams fed the known inscriptions into mainframe computers and analyzed the positional patterns of the signs: which signs appeared at the beginning of inscriptions, which at the end, which signs commonly appeared together, and which sequences were repeated.

Both teams reached similar conclusions. They argued that the script was a writing system (not a set of random symbols or non-linguistic marks). They identified what appeared to be grammatical patterns -- certain signs functioning as prefixes or suffixes, suggesting that the script encoded a language with inflectional morphology (a language that modifies words by adding beginnings or endings, as English adds "-ed" for past tense or "-s" for plurals). And both teams proposed that the language encoded by the script was Dravidian -- a member of the language family that includes Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, and Malayalam, spoken today primarily in southern India.

The Dravidian hypothesis became, and remains, the most influential proposed reading of the Indus script. Asko Parpola has devoted his entire scholarly career to this hypothesis -- over fifty years of work, summarized in his 1994 book *Deciphering the Indus Script* and refined in subsequent publications. Parpola's argument is multi-layered and draws on linguistics, archaeology, comparative religion, and the analysis of sign frequencies and patterns.

The core of Parpola's argument rests on the fish sign. The Indus script contains a sign that clearly depicts a fish -- one of the most recognizable pictographic signs in the corpus. In Proto-Dravidian (the reconstructed ancestor of the modern Dravidian languages), the word for "fish" is *meen* -- or a close variant. In several Dravidian languages, the word *meen* (or *min*) also means "star" -- a homophone, where two different meanings share the same sound. Parpola argues that the Indus fish sign represents the word *meen*, and that when the sign appears with modifying marks (such as a numeral or an additional symbol), it refers to specific stars or celestial bodies. A fish sign with the numeral six, for example, might represent the Pleiades star cluster (which is visible as a group of six stars to the naked eye). A fish sign with a roof-like mark might represent the word for "house" combined with "star" -- possibly a reference to a deity or a calendrical notation.

The argument is ingenious. It connects the visual form of the sign (a fish) to a known linguistic feature of Dravidian languages (the fish/star homophone) in a way that produces meaningful readings. Parpola has extended this approach to other signs, proposing Dravidian readings for signs depicting humans, animals, and objects -- each reading grounded in the vocabulary and grammar of reconstructed Proto-Dravidian.

![Diagram showing Parpola's fish-sign hypothesis connecting the Indus fish glyph to the Proto-Dravidian word meen](../images/placeholders/05-fish-sign-hypothesis.png)

The hypothesis has supporters. Many South Indian scholars accept the Dravidian identification as the most plausible reading. The geographical argument is suggestive: Dravidian languages are spoken today in southern India, but there is evidence (including Brahui (brah-HOO-ee), a Dravidian language still spoken in Balochistan, Pakistan, far from the Dravidian heartland) that Dravidian languages were once more widely distributed across the subcontinent. The Indus Valley is a plausible homeland for the Dravidian language family, and the Indus script is a plausible vehicle for an early Dravidian language.

But the hypothesis has not been accepted as a decipherment. The readings are clever but unverifiable. Without a bilingual text or a known descendant script, there is no way to confirm that the fish sign represents *meen*, that the star interpretation is correct, or that the proposed grammatical patterns reflect actual Dravidian morphology rather than patterns imposed by the analyst. Parpola's readings are possible, even plausible -- but they are not proven.

The alternative hypotheses are equally unproven. The Indo-Aryan hypothesis -- that the script encodes an early Indo-European language, perhaps a precursor of Sanskrit -- has been proposed by several Indian scholars, including S.R. Rao (ROW, rhymes with "how") and others. The hypothesis is attractive to some because it would connect the Indus civilization to the later Vedic civilization that produced the earliest Sanskrit texts. But the linguistic arguments are weaker than Parpola's Dravidian case, and the hypothesis is entangled with politically sensitive questions about the origins of Indo-European languages in South Asia -- questions that we will examine in Chapter 8.

A third possibility is that the script encodes a language belonging to neither the Dravidian nor the Indo-European family -- a language isolate, now extinct, with no surviving relatives. The Sumerian language of ancient Mesopotamia is a precedent: Sumerian is a language isolate, unrelated to any known language family, that was deciphered only because bilingual Sumerian-Akkadian texts existed and because the cuneiform writing system was adapted by Akkadian speakers who preserved knowledge of the signs' values. If the Indus language was a similar isolate, decipherment without a bilingual text would be essentially impossible -- there would be no living or known language against which to test proposed readings.

The most provocative challenge to all decipherment attempts came in 2004, when Steve Farmer, Richard Sproat, and Michael Witzel published a paper titled "The Collapse of the Indus-Script Thesis." Farmer, an independent scholar, Sproat, a computational linguist at Johns Hopkins University, and Witzel, a Sanskrit scholar at Harvard, argued that the Indus signs are not writing at all. Their argument was based on several observations.

First, the inscriptions are too short. In every known writing system, texts of significant length exist -- letters, administrative records, literary compositions, religious texts. The Indus corpus contains nothing longer than twenty-six signs. If the Indus signs encode a spoken language, where are the longer texts? The absence of long texts, Farmer, Sproat, and Witzel argued, is evidence that the signs do not encode language. Instead, they proposed that the signs are non-linguistic symbols -- comparable to heraldic emblems, religious icons, or the kind of symbolic markers used in many pre-literate and non-literate societies to indicate identity, affiliation, or status.

Second, they pointed to the lack of textual variation. In a true writing system, you would expect to find texts used for many different purposes: administrative records, personal letters, religious texts, lists, labels. The Indus inscriptions appear almost exclusively on seals and related objects. The narrowness of the contexts in which the signs appear suggests a limited function -- marking ownership or affiliation -- rather than the broad communicative function of a true writing system.

Third, they noted that the sign sequences show little of the syntactic structure expected of written language. In known writing systems, sign order reflects grammatical rules -- subjects precede verbs, modifiers precede or follow nouns, and certain signs appear in predictable positions based on the grammar of the underlying language. The positional patterns in the Indus sign sequences, Farmer and his co-authors argued, are simpler and less structured than what would be expected of a grammatical system.

The "not writing" hypothesis provoked an immediate and fierce response. Parpola, Mahadevan, and many other scholars rejected it categorically. They argued that the brevity of the inscriptions reflects the function of the seals (markers on trade goods do not require lengthy texts), not the nature of the script. They pointed out that longer texts may have existed on perishable materials -- palm leaves, bark, cloth, wood -- that have not survived five thousand years of burial. They noted that the positional patterns identified by computational analysis are consistent with a grammatical system, even if the system cannot yet be identified. And they argued that the sheer number of distinct signs (four hundred or more) is far greater than any known non-linguistic symbol system, which typically uses a few dozen symbols at most.

The debate generated extraordinary heat. Academic papers were published and rebutted. Conference sessions turned contentious. Online exchanges between the rival camps became sharp. The intensity of the disagreement reflects the stakes: if the Indus signs are writing, they represent the voice of a lost civilization, waiting to be heard. If they are not writing, they are silent in a more fundamental way -- not a code to be cracked but a system of symbols that never encoded speech in the first place.

The debate has not been resolved. The Farmer-Sproat-Witzel hypothesis is a minority position, but it is a serious one, backed by legitimate arguments. The majority of scholars continue to treat the Indus signs as a writing system -- but the majority position rests on inference and analogy rather than proof. The question "Is the Indus script writing?" remains open. It is the most fundamental question in the field, and it may be the most important: if the signs are not writing, then the effort to "decipher" them is misconceived from the start.


## Chapter 6 — Why It's So Hard

The Indus script has resisted decipherment for a century. Understanding why requires comparing it to the scripts that have been successfully deciphered -- and understanding what those successes had that the Indus case lacks.

The most famous decipherment in history is Jean-François Champollion's reading of Egyptian hieroglyphics in 1822. Champollion's breakthrough was made possible by three factors. First, the Rosetta Stone: a bilingual (actually trilingual) inscription that presented the same text in hieroglyphics, Demotic Egyptian, and ancient Greek. Because scholars could read the Greek text, they had a key to the meanings encoded in the hieroglyphic signs. Second, Champollion knew Coptic -- the last surviving form of the ancient Egyptian language, still used as a liturgical language by the Coptic Christian Church. Coptic provided a bridge from the known (a living language) to the unknown (the ancient script). Third, the hieroglyphic texts were long. The Rosetta Stone alone provided enough data for pattern analysis, and the walls of Egyptian temples and tombs were covered with inscriptions containing thousands of signs arranged in extended texts.

The decipherment of Linear B -- the script used by the Mycenaean (my-suh-NEE-uhn) civilization of Bronze Age Greece -- was achieved by Michael Ventris (VEN-triss) in 1952. Ventris had no bilingual text, but he had something almost as valuable: long texts. The Linear B tablets from Knossos (kuh-NOS-sos) and Pylos (PY-loss) contained hundreds of signs arranged in lists that were clearly administrative records -- inventories of livestock, grain, oil, textiles, and personnel. The lists showed patterns -- repeated sequences, consistent formatting, apparent categories -- that allowed Ventris to identify the underlying structure of the script. And when Ventris proposed that the language was Greek (a surprising conclusion, since the tablets predated the Greek alphabet by centuries), the proposed readings produced recognizable Greek words. The decipherment was confirmed: the readings made sense in a known language.

![Comparison showing the Rosetta Stone, a Linear B tablet, and an Indus seal, illustrating the difference in text length](../images/placeholders/06-text-length-comparison.png)

The Maya script was deciphered gradually over the second half of the twentieth century, through the work of Yuri Knorosov (yoo-REE knoh-ROH-soff), Tatiana Proskouriakoff (pross-KOO-ree-ah-koff), David Stuart, Linda Schele (SHEH-lee), and others. The Maya case had several advantages. First, the script was used for long texts -- inscriptions on stone monuments sometimes contained hundreds of glyphs arranged in detailed narrative sequences recording the births, accessions, wars, and deaths of rulers. Second, the Maya languages are still spoken by millions of people in Guatemala and Mexico, providing a living bridge to the ancient script. Third, partial bilingual resources existed: Bishop Diego de Landa's sixteenth-century account of the Maya script, while deeply flawed, provided a starting point for identifying sign values.

The Indus script has none of these advantages.

There is no bilingual text. There is no Rosetta Stone, no inscription that presents the same content in the Indus script and in a known writing system. Indus seals have been found in Mesopotamian contexts (evidence of trade between the two civilizations), and Mesopotamian texts mention a land called "Meluhha" (meh-LOO-hah), which is widely identified with the Indus Valley. But no Mesopotamian text provides a translation of Indus signs. The two civilizations traded goods but apparently did not create bilingual inscriptions.

There is no known descendant script. The Indus script disappeared when the Indus civilization declined, around 1900 BCE. The later writing systems of South Asia -- Brahmi (BRAH-mee), the ancestor of most modern South Asian scripts, first attested in the third century BCE -- show no convincing connection to the Indus script. The gap between the end of the Indus script (approximately 1900 BCE) and the appearance of Brahmi (approximately 300 BCE) is roughly 1,600 years -- a gap too large to bridge through visual comparison of sign forms. The Indus script, unlike Egyptian hieroglyphics (which evolved through Hieratic and Demotic into Coptic), has no known descendants.

The underlying language is unknown. This is perhaps the most fundamental obstacle. When Ventris deciphered Linear B, he was able to test his proposed readings against a known language -- Greek. When the readings produced recognizable Greek words, the decipherment was confirmed. For the Indus script, there is no comparable test. If a scholar proposes that a particular sign sequence reads as a Dravidian word, the proposal cannot be verified independently -- there is no external evidence to confirm or deny the reading. The proposed language is itself a hypothesis, and the proposed readings are circular: the readings are used to argue for the language, and the language is used to justify the readings.

The brevity of the inscriptions compounds every other difficulty. Statistical methods -- frequency analysis, positional analysis, entropy calculations -- work best with large samples of text. The entire known Indus corpus contains roughly two thousand inscriptions with a total of perhaps eight thousand to ten thousand sign occurrences. This is a tiny dataset by the standards of computational linguistics. By comparison, the Egyptian hieroglyphic corpus contains millions of sign occurrences, and the Maya glyphic corpus contains hundreds of thousands. The Indus dataset is simply too small for the standard analytical tools to produce reliable results.

Several scholars have attempted to apply modern computational methods to the problem. Bryan Wells, an American epigrapher, has developed computer databases and search tools for the Indus script corpus. Rajesh Rao (rah-JESH ROW), a computer scientist at the University of Washington, published a study in 2009 arguing that the statistical structure of the Indus sign sequences is consistent with a linguistic system (as opposed to a non-linguistic symbol system). The study measured the "conditional entropy" of the sign sequences -- a mathematical measure of how predictable each sign is given the signs that precede it -- and found that the Indus values fell within the range of known writing systems rather than non-linguistic symbol systems.

The study was challenged by Sproat, who argued that the entropy measurements could not reliably distinguish between linguistic and non-linguistic systems, and that the statistical tests used were insufficient to resolve the question. The debate between Rao and Sproat -- conducted through published papers, conference presentations, and online exchanges -- exemplifies the broader stalemate in Indus script studies: sophisticated analytical tools are applied to a dataset that is too small to yield definitive results, and the conclusions depend as much on the assumptions and methods of the analysts as on the data itself.

The comparison with undeciphered scripts elsewhere in the world provides perspective. The Etruscan script of ancient Italy was "deciphered" in the sense that scholars can read the letters (Etruscan used a variant of the Greek alphabet), but the language remains poorly understood because so few extended texts survive. The Rongorongo script of Easter Island has resisted decipherment for reasons similar to the Indus case: short texts, no bilingual inscription, and an unknown underlying language. The Proto-Elamite script of ancient Iran has been partially deciphered through comparison with later Elamite texts, but significant portions remain unread. The Indus script stands among these as the most consequential unsolved problem in epigraphy -- the study of ancient inscriptions -- because of the size, sophistication, and historical importance of the civilization that produced it.

The prospects for decipherment are uncertain. A bilingual inscription -- a text presenting the same content in the Indus script and in a known writing system -- would transform the field overnight. Such an inscription could exist: Indus merchants traded extensively with Mesopotamia, and a bilingual seal or trade document might lie undiscovered in the ruins of an Iraqi or Bahraini port city. The discovery of longer Indus texts -- on a medium that has survived, perhaps a stone monument or a cache of sealed clay tablets -- would provide the data that statistical methods need to work effectively. New excavations at Indus sites, particularly in India (where several large sites, including Rakhigarhi (rahk-ee-GAR-hee), are under active excavation), might yield the breakthrough.

But these are hopes, not certainties. The Indus script may remain undeciphered. The inscriptions may be too short, too formulaic, and too isolated from any known linguistic tradition to ever be read with confidence. The silence of Harappa may be permanent.
