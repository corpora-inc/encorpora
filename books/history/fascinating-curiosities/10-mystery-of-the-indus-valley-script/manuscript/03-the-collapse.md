# Part Three: The Collapse

## Chapter 7 — The End of the Cities

The Indus Valley civilization did not end in a single catastrophe. It faded. The great cities -- Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira, Ganweriwala (gahn-WEHR-ee-WAH-lah) -- declined over a period of several centuries, from roughly 1900 BCE onward. The population did not vanish. The people dispersed into smaller settlements, carried fragments of their material culture into new contexts, and adapted to changing conditions. But the urban system -- the grid-planned cities, the standardized bricks, the elaborate drainage, the long-distance trade networks, the script itself -- collapsed. The civilization that had maintained uniform building standards and consistent weights across 1.5 million square kilometers disintegrated into regional fragments.

The decline is visible in the archaeological record at Mohenjo-daro. The later levels of the city show deterioration: houses built with reused bricks rather than new standardized ones, streets encroached upon by informal construction, drains no longer maintained, the grid plan disrupted by haphazard building. The population may have grown in the city's later phases (there are signs of overcrowding), even as the quality of construction and infrastructure declined. The picture is one of a city losing its organizational capacity -- the authority that had maintained the grid, the drains, and the brick standards weakening or disappearing.

At Harappa, Jonathan Mark Kenoyer has documented a similar trajectory. The later levels show the same deterioration of planning and standardization. New ceramic traditions appear, suggesting the arrival of new populations or the adoption of new cultural practices. The economy shifted: evidence of long-distance trade (Mesopotamian imports, marine shell from the Arabian Sea coast) diminishes, replaced by more localized exchange networks. The city's neighborhoods, which Kenoyer believes were once occupied by distinct social or ethnic groups cooperating within a shared urban framework, may have become more insular and less integrated.

The causes of the decline have been debated for decades. Several factors have been proposed, and most scholars now believe that multiple causes interacted to produce the collapse.

The most frequently cited factor is environmental change -- specifically, the shifting of the rivers on which the cities depended. The Indus Valley civilization was built along two major river systems: the Indus itself (which still flows through Pakistan) and a second system, now largely dry, that once flowed through the regions of Rajasthan, Haryana, and the Cholistan Desert. This second system is identified by many scholars with the Ghaggar-Hakra (GAH-gar HAK-rah) river channel -- a seasonal watercourse that may have once been a perennial river fed by Himalayan glacial meltwater.

The Ghaggar-Hakra hypothesis is one of the most actively researched questions in Indus studies. Geological evidence suggests that the river system underwent significant changes during the third and second millennia BCE. Tectonic activity in the Himalayas may have diverted the headwaters of the Ghaggar-Hakra's tributaries into the Indus or Yamuna river systems, reducing the Ghaggar-Hakra from a perennial river to a seasonal stream. The diversion would have been gradual -- occurring over centuries rather than in a single event -- but the cumulative effect would have been devastating for communities dependent on the river for agriculture, drinking water, and transportation.

The connection between the Ghaggar-Hakra and the legendary Sarasvati (SAH-rahs-vah-tee) River mentioned in the Rigveda (the oldest of the Hindu sacred texts) has generated enormous scholarly and political interest. The Rigveda describes the Sarasvati as a mighty river -- "surpassing in majesty and might all other rivers" -- that flowed from the mountains to the sea. If the Sarasvati is identified with the Ghaggar-Hakra, and if the Ghaggar-Hakra dried up around 1900 BCE, then the Rigvedic description of the Sarasvati might be a memory of the river in its earlier, fuller state -- and the authors of the Rigveda might have lived in the region during or before the Indus civilization's decline. This identification has been embraced by some scholars and rejected by others, and it intersects with the politically charged "Aryan Question" discussed in the next chapter.

Climate change is a second proposed factor. Paleoclimatic studies suggest that the region experienced a shift toward drier conditions around 2000-1900 BCE, part of a broader aridification event that affected much of western and southern Asia during the late third millennium BCE. The same dry period has been linked to the collapse of the Akkadian Empire in Mesopotamia and to cultural disruptions in Egypt, the Levant, and the Aegean. The Indus Valley, already dependent on monsoon rainfall for agriculture, would have been vulnerable to a sustained reduction in precipitation. Crops would have failed more frequently. Pasture would have shrunk. The agricultural surplus that sustained the cities would have diminished.

A third factor is the disruption of trade networks. The Indus civilization was embedded in a web of long-distance trade that connected it to Mesopotamia, the Persian Gulf, Central Asia, and the mineral-rich regions of the Indian peninsula. Indus seals have been found at Ur, in southern Mesopotamia. Mesopotamian texts from the Ur III period (approximately 2100-2000 BCE) reference a place called "Meluhha" that is widely identified with the Indus Valley, describing it as a source of exotic goods: carnelian beads, lapis lazuli, ivory, and timber. By the early second millennium BCE, these references to Meluhha trade had ceased. The disruption of the Mesopotamian trade networks -- caused by political instability in Mesopotamia, changes in trade routes, or the decline of Indus ports -- would have weakened the economic foundations of the Indus urban system.

The interaction of these factors -- river diversion, climate aridification, trade disruption -- may have created a cascading failure. The river changes reduced the agricultural base. The drier climate intensified the problem. The loss of trade revenue undermined the urban economy. The administrative systems that had maintained the cities' infrastructure weakened. The result was de-urbanization: a shift from large, planned cities to smaller, dispersed settlements.

The post-urban phase of the Indus tradition is sometimes called the "Cemetery H" culture (at Harappa) or the "Late Harappan" or "Post-Urban Harappan" phase. The material culture of this phase shows continuity with the earlier Indus tradition -- certain pottery styles, certain tool types, certain burial practices persist -- but the urban characteristics that defined the mature Indus civilization are gone. There are no more grid-planned cities. The standardized brick ratios disappear. The drainage systems are abandoned. The seals with their inscriptions become rare, and then vanish entirely. The script falls out of use.

The Cemetery H culture at Harappa, dating to roughly 1900-1300 BCE, takes its name from a burial ground at the site where distinctive painted pottery was found in graves. The pottery of Cemetery H is decorated with images of peacocks, stars, and stylized human figures -- motifs that have no parallel in the mature Harappan tradition but that some scholars have connected to later South Asian artistic traditions. The burials themselves are simpler than the earlier Harappan burials: fewer grave goods, less uniformity in burial position and orientation, and a general impression of reduced social investment in mortuary ritual. The transition from the mature Harappan to the Cemetery H phase is not a clean break but a gradual transformation, visible in the stratigraphy of the site as a slow erosion of the systems that had organized urban life.

Dholavira's decline is particularly well documented. The city went through several phases of contraction, with some areas abandoned while others continued to be occupied. The elaborate water reservoir system -- one of the city's most impressive engineering achievements -- shows evidence of reduced maintenance in its later phases, suggesting that the administrative capacity to organize large-scale public works was weakening. The final phase of occupation at Dholavira is sparse: a small population living in the ruins of a once-great city, using the old stone walls as the framework for much simpler structures. The signboard with its ten large signs -- the largest known Indus inscription -- was still standing above its gate, but there may have been no one left who could read it.

The disappearance of the script is significant. A writing system (if the Indus signs are indeed writing) is maintained by a literate community -- a group of people who know how to produce and read the signs, and who transmit that knowledge to the next generation. When the urban system collapsed, the institutional contexts in which the script was used -- trade, administration, property marking -- may have disappeared, and with them, the need for literacy. Without the institutions that sustained it, the knowledge of the script was not transmitted. Within a few generations, it was lost.

The people of the Indus Valley did not disappear. They moved. Archaeological evidence shows a population shift during the late Harappan period: settlements in the Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra valleys declined, while settlements in Gujarat, the Ganga-Yamuna doab (the land between the Ganges and Yamuna rivers), and other regions to the east and south increased. The population was redistributing itself across the landscape, moving toward areas with more reliable water sources and agricultural potential. The genetic and cultural heritage of the Indus civilization was carried into these new regions, where it mixed with other traditions to produce the diverse cultures of the subsequent Iron Age and historical periods.

Mohenjo-daro itself may have been abandoned more suddenly than other cities. The upper levels of the site show evidence of unburied human remains -- skeletons found in streets and buildings, some in positions suggesting violent death. Mortimer Wheeler (1890-1976), who excavated at Mohenjo-daro in the 1940s, famously attributed these remains to a "massacre" by Aryan invaders. Wheeler's dramatic interpretation has been largely rejected by subsequent scholarship: the skeletal remains are from different time periods and levels, they do not represent a single event, and there is no evidence of military assault. The deaths may have resulted from disease, flooding, or other catastrophes during the city's final decline. But Wheeler's vivid narrative of invaders storming a dying city persists in popular accounts, illustrating how dramatic interpretations can outlive the evidence that supports them.

The end of the Indus cities was not unique in the Bronze Age world. The late third and early second millennia BCE saw urban collapses across a wide area. The Akkadian Empire in Mesopotamia fell around 2154 BCE. The Egyptian Old Kingdom entered a period of decline and fragmentation (the First Intermediate Period) around 2181 BCE. The Early Bronze Age civilizations of the Levant and Anatolia experienced disruptions. The causes were different in each case, but the pattern -- urban systems collapsing under the pressure of environmental change, political instability, and economic disruption -- was widespread. The Indus collapse was part of a broader phenomenon: the vulnerability of early urban civilizations to stresses that exceeded their adaptive capacity.


## Chapter 8 — The Aryan Question

No topic in South Asian archaeology generates more controversy than the "Aryan Question": the debate over the origins of the Indo-European-speaking peoples who produced the Vedic civilization of northern India and whose descendants include the majority of the modern population of the Indian subcontinent.

The question is straightforward in outline. The Indo-European language family -- which includes Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Persian, German, English, and many other languages -- is the world's largest language family by number of speakers. Linguists have established that all Indo-European languages descend from a single ancestral language, called Proto-Indo-European, spoken somewhere in western Eurasia thousands of years ago. The question is: where was Proto-Indo-European spoken, and how did its descendant languages spread across such an enormous area?

The mainstream scholarly consensus, supported by linguistic, archaeological, and genetic evidence, holds that Proto-Indo-European was spoken on the Pontic-Caspian steppe -- the grasslands north of the Black Sea and Caspian Sea, in what is now southern Russia and Ukraine -- during the fourth and third millennia BCE. From this homeland, Indo-European-speaking peoples migrated outward in multiple waves: westward into Europe, southward into Anatolia and Iran, and eastward into Central Asia and, eventually, the Indian subcontinent. The migrations were gradual, occurring over centuries, and involved the movement of relatively small groups who spread their language through a combination of migration, intermarriage, and cultural influence.

The arrival of Indo-European (Indo-Aryan) speakers in the Indian subcontinent is placed by most scholars in the period between roughly 2000 and 1500 BCE -- after the decline of the Indus cities. The earliest Sanskrit texts, the hymns of the Rigveda, describe a society of pastoralists and warriors who worshipped the sky god Indra and the fire god Agni, who rode horse-drawn chariots, and who lived in a landscape of rivers and plains that can be identified with the northwestern Indian subcontinent. The society described in the Rigveda is different from the Indus Valley civilization in almost every respect: pastoral rather than urban, warrior-oriented rather than trade-oriented, organized around tribal chieftains rather than the anonymous administrative systems of the Indus cities.

The old interpretation -- proposed by Wheeler and others in the mid-twentieth century -- was that the Indo-Aryan speakers invaded the subcontinent by force, conquered the Indus cities, and destroyed the civilization. Wheeler pointed to the Rigvedic descriptions of the god Indra destroying the fortified cities (called *pur* in Sanskrit) of the *Dasas* or *Dasyus* (terms for the enemies of the Vedic people) as evidence that the Aryan migration was a military conquest. The unburied skeletons at Mohenjo-daro, in Wheeler's dramatic formulation, were the victims of this Aryan assault.

The invasion theory has been largely abandoned. The timeline does not support it: the Indus cities had already declined significantly before the period associated with Indo-Aryan migration. The archaeological evidence shows no destruction layers consistent with military conquest. The skeletal remains at Mohenjo-daro do not represent a single massacre. And the Rigvedic descriptions of city-destroying warfare may refer to conflicts among Indo-Aryan groups or between Indo-Aryan speakers and other populations in the subcontinent, rather than to the conquest of the Indus civilization.

The modern scholarly view replaces "invasion" with "migration." Indo-European speakers moved into the subcontinent gradually, over centuries, bringing their language, their religion, and their pastoral economy with them. They encountered a population that was already dispersed from the former Indus cities into smaller settlements. The interaction between the incoming Indo-Aryan speakers and the existing population -- which may have included speakers of Dravidian languages, Munda (MOON-dah) languages, and other language families -- produced the diverse linguistic and cultural landscape of the subcontinent.

Genetic evidence has added a new dimension to the debate. Ancient DNA studies, particularly a landmark 2019 study led by Vagheesh Narasimhan and David Reich, analyzed genomes from Bronze Age and Iron Age skeletal remains across Central and South Asia. The study found evidence of a major genetic influx into the Indian subcontinent from the Central Asian steppe during the second millennium BCE -- consistent with the Indo-Aryan migration hypothesis. The steppe-related genetic component is found at higher frequencies in populations that speak Indo-European languages (particularly among Brahmin and other traditionally "upper-caste" groups in northern India) and at lower frequencies in populations that speak Dravidian languages (concentrated in southern India). The genetic evidence supports a model in which steppe-derived populations migrated into the subcontinent, mixed with the existing population, and spread the Indo-European languages that eventually became the dominant language family of northern India.

The political sensitivity of the question is intense. In India, the debate over the origins of the Indo-Aryans is entangled with questions of national identity, caste, religion, and colonial history. The British colonial administration used the "Aryan invasion" theory to divide India's population into racial categories -- "Aryan" northerners and "Dravidian" southerners -- and to justify British rule by claiming that Indians were themselves descended from invaders. This colonial exploitation of the migration theory has led some Indian scholars and political movements to reject the migration hypothesis entirely, arguing instead that the Indo-European language family originated in India (the "Out of India" hypothesis) and that the Indus civilization was itself Indo-Aryan.

The "Out of India" hypothesis is rejected by the overwhelming majority of linguists, archaeologists, and geneticists outside India, and by many scholars within India as well. The linguistic evidence for an external origin of the Indo-European languages is strong: the vocabulary and grammar of Proto-Indo-European include terms for temperate-zone flora and fauna (birch trees, bees, wolves, snow) that are consistent with a steppe or northern European homeland rather than a South Asian one. The genetic evidence, while politically unwelcome to some, consistently points to a steppe-derived migration into India. The archaeological evidence shows no material culture in the Indus Valley that resembles the reconstructed culture of the Proto-Indo-European speakers (who used horse-drawn chariots, practiced kurgan burial, and had a pastoral rather than urban economy).

The relationship between the Indus civilization and the later Vedic civilization remains unclear. The two cultures overlap in geography but are separated in time: the Indus civilization's urban phase ended around 1900 BCE, and the Rigvedic hymns are generally dated to roughly 1500-1200 BCE. The Rigveda contains no clear description of the Indus cities, the grid-planned streets, the Great Bath, or the seals with their enigmatic inscriptions. If the Vedic people had encountered the Indus civilization in its urban glory, one would expect some echo in their hymns. The silence suggests that by the time the Vedic texts were composed, the Indus cities were already ruins -- or memories so distant that they had faded from the cultural record.

The Aryan Question intersects with the mystery of the Indus script in a direct way. If the Indus script encodes a Dravidian language (as Parpola argues), then the Indus civilization was linguistically distinct from the later Indo-Aryan Vedic civilization -- the two belonged to different language families, and the transition from Indus to Vedic involved a language shift as well as a cultural one. If the Indus script encodes an Indo-Aryan language (as some Indian scholars argue), then the Indus civilization and the Vedic civilization are part of the same linguistic tradition, and the "Aryan migration" may not have occurred in the way the mainstream model proposes. If the Indus script encodes a language isolate (neither Dravidian nor Indo-Aryan), then the linguistic history of the subcontinent is more complex than either hypothesis suggests.

The decipherment of the Indus script would resolve the Aryan Question -- or at least transform it from a political and ideological debate into a linguistic and historical one. This is one reason the decipherment is so eagerly sought, and so fiercely contested. The stakes are not only scholarly but cultural and political. In the Indus script, the past is not merely unknown. It is contested.
