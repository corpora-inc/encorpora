# What We Still Don't Know

**Is the Indus script writing?** The most fundamental question in the field. The majority of scholars treat the Indus signs as a writing system encoding a spoken language, but the "not writing" hypothesis -- that the signs are non-linguistic symbols comparable to heraldic emblems or religious icons -- has not been refuted. The question cannot be resolved without longer texts, a bilingual inscription, or a decisive advance in analytical methods.

**What language does the script encode?** If the Indus signs are writing, the language they represent is unknown. The Dravidian hypothesis (Parpola) is the most developed and influential proposal, but it remains unproven. The Indo-Aryan hypothesis is a minority position with weaker linguistic support. The possibility that the script encodes a language isolate -- a language with no surviving relatives -- cannot be excluded.

**Who governed the Indus cities?** The cities show clear evidence of centralized planning and administrative control: standardized bricks, uniform weights, grid-planned streets, consistent drainage systems. But there are no palaces, no royal tombs, no monumental art celebrating a ruler. The nature of Indus political authority -- whether it was concentrated in individuals, distributed among institutions, or organized in some other way -- is invisible in the archaeological record.

**Was the Indus civilization peaceful?** The apparent absence of warfare evidence (no fortifications, no weapons caches, no military art) has led some scholars to describe the Indus civilization as uniquely non-violent. Others argue that the absence reflects the archaeological record's limitations rather than a genuine absence of conflict. The question cannot be resolved with current evidence.

**What caused the decline?** The collapse of the Indus urban system around 1900 BCE was probably caused by multiple interacting factors: river diversion, climate aridification, and trade disruption. But the relative importance of these factors, and the mechanisms by which they undermined the urban system, are debated. The role of the Ghaggar-Hakra river system and its possible identification with the Vedic Sarasvati remains particularly controversial.

**What is the relationship between the Indus civilization and the later Vedic civilization?** The Indus cities declined before the Vedic period, and the Rigveda contains no clear description of Indus urban features. The nature of the transition -- cultural continuity, population replacement, gradual absorption -- is unclear. The "Aryan Question" (the origins and migration of Indo-European speakers into the subcontinent) remains contested, despite strong genetic evidence supporting the migration hypothesis.

**What does the Dholavira signboard say?** The largest known Indus inscription -- ten large signs displayed above a city gate -- is potentially the most readable text in the Indus corpus. If the script is ever deciphered, the signboard will likely be among the first texts to yield its meaning. Until then, it remains the most tantalizing fragment of the silence.

**Will the script ever be deciphered?** A bilingual inscription, a cache of long texts, or a decisive computational breakthrough could transform the field overnight. New excavations at sites like Rakhigarhi offer hope. But the fundamental obstacles -- short inscriptions, no known descendant script, no identified language -- may prove insurmountable. The Indus script may be permanently silent.


# Key Names and Terms

**Akkadian Empire** -- The first empire in world history, founded by Sargon of Akkad (~2334 BCE) in Mesopotamia; contemporary with the mature Indus phase.

**Banerji, Rakhal Das** (rah-KAHL DAHS bah-NER-jee) -- Indian archaeologist who began excavating Mohenjo-daro in 1921, one of the co-discoverers of the Indus civilization.

**Brahmi** (BRAH-mee) -- The earliest known writing system of post-Indus South Asia, first attested in the third century BCE; ancestor of most modern South Asian scripts. Shows no convincing connection to the Indus script.

**Brahui** (brah-HOO-ee) -- A Dravidian language spoken in Balochistan, Pakistan; cited as evidence that Dravidian languages were once more widely distributed across the subcontinent.

**Champollion, Jean-François** (zhahn-frahn-SWAH shahm-pol-YON) -- French scholar who deciphered Egyptian hieroglyphics in 1822, using the Rosetta Stone and his knowledge of Coptic.

**Cunningham, Alexander** -- First director of the Archaeological Survey of India; visited Harappa in 1872-1873 but did not recognize the site's antiquity.

**Dholavira** (DOH-lah-vee-rah) -- A major Indus city in Gujarat, India; notable for its water reservoir system and the largest known Indus inscription (a signboard of ten signs above a city gate).

**Dravidian** -- A language family spoken primarily in southern India (Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam); proposed by Parpola and others as the language encoded by the Indus script.

**Farmer, Steve** -- Independent scholar; co-author (with Sproat and Witzel) of the 2004 paper arguing that the Indus signs are not writing.

**Ganweriwala** (gahn-WEHR-ee-WAH-lah) -- A large Indus site in the Cholistan Desert of Pakistan; largely unexcavated.

**Ghaggar-Hakra** (GAH-gar HAK-rah) -- A seasonal river channel in India and Pakistan that may have been a perennial river during the Indus period; its diversion may have contributed to the civilization's decline.

**Great Bath** -- A rectangular brick-lined pool at Mohenjo-daro, roughly 12 by 7 meters; the earliest known public water facility in the world.

**Harappa** (hah-RAH-pah) -- One of the two largest known Indus cities, located in Punjab, Pakistan; the first Indus site identified (1850s) and the site from which the civilization takes its alternative name.

**Kenoyer, Jonathan Mark** (KEN-oy-er) -- American archaeologist; excavator of Harappa since the 1980s; advocate for a multi-ethnic model of Indus urban organization.

**Logo-syllabic** -- A type of writing system that combines logographic signs (representing words) with syllabic signs (representing sounds); the category into which the Indus script most likely falls.

**Lothal** (LOH-tahl) -- An Indus port city in Gujarat, India; features a large rectangular structure interpreted as a dock and evidence of overseas trade.

**Mahadevan, Iravatham** (ee-RAH-vah-tahm mah-HAH-deh-vahn) -- Indian epigrapher; compiled the standard concordance of Indus script signs (1977); proposed Dravidian readings.

**Marshall, John** -- British archaeologist; director-general of the Archaeological Survey of India; announced the discovery of the Indus civilization in 1924.

**Meluhha** (meh-LOO-hah) -- The Sumerian name for a distant trading partner, widely identified with the Indus Valley civilization.

**Mohenjo-daro** (moh-HEN-joh DAH-roh) -- "Mound of the Dead" in Sindhi; one of the two largest known Indus cities, located in Sindh, Pakistan; the best-excavated Indus site.

**Parpola, Asko** (AHS-koh PAR-poh-lah) -- Finnish Indologist; the leading proponent of the Dravidian hypothesis for the Indus script; author of *Deciphering the Indus Script* (1994).

**Possehl, Gregory** (poh-SELL) -- American archaeologist; author of *The Indus Civilization: A Contemporary Perspective* (2002); described the Indus civilization as "without a face."

**Proto-Indo-European** -- The reconstructed ancestor of all Indo-European languages; believed by most linguists to have been spoken on the Pontic-Caspian steppe.

**Rakhigarhi** (rahk-ee-GAR-hee) -- A large Indus site in Haryana, India; under active excavation; one of the largest known Indus settlements.

**Rao, Rajesh** (rah-JESH ROW) -- Computer scientist at the University of Washington; applied computational entropy analysis to the Indus sign sequences.

**Rao, S.R.** (ROW) -- Indian archaeologist; excavator of Lothal; proposed Indo-Aryan readings of the Indus script.

**Ratnagar, Shereen** (rah-tuh-NAH-gar) -- Indian archaeologist; critical voice on Indus social organization; argues against the "peaceful Indus" narrative.

**Sahni, Rai Bahadur Daya Ram** (DYE-ah RAHM SAH-nee) -- Indian archaeologist who began systematic excavations at Harappa in 1921.

**Sarasvati** (SAH-rahs-vah-tee) -- A river described in the Rigveda as mighty and life-giving; sometimes identified with the now-dry Ghaggar-Hakra river channel.

**Shortugai** (SHOR-too-guy) -- A small Indus outpost in northern Afghanistan, near lapis lazuli sources; demonstrates the extent of the Indus trade network.

**Sproat, Richard** -- Computational linguist; co-author of the 2004 "not writing" paper; critic of computational approaches to the Indus script.

**Steatite** (STEE-ah-tyte) -- A soft stone (also called soapstone) used for most Indus seals; easily carved and hardened by firing.

**Ventris, Michael** (VEN-triss) -- British architect and amateur linguist who deciphered Linear B in 1952, identifying the script as encoding Greek.

**Wells, Bryan** -- American epigrapher; developer of computational databases for Indus script analysis.

**Wheeler, Mortimer** -- British archaeologist (1890-1976); excavated at Mohenjo-daro in the 1940s; proposed (now rejected) that the Indus civilization was destroyed by Aryan invaders.

**Witzel, Michael** -- Harvard Sanskrit scholar; co-author of the 2004 "not writing" paper.


# Further Reading

**Gregory Possehl, *The Indus Civilization: A Contemporary Perspective* (2002)** -- The most comprehensive English-language synthesis of Indus archaeology. Accessible, well-organized, and balanced in its treatment of controversial questions. The essential starting point.

**Asko Parpola, *Deciphering the Indus Script* (1994)** -- Parpola's magisterial presentation of the Dravidian hypothesis. Dense and technical, but the most thorough published attempt at decipherment. For readers who want to understand the argument in detail.

**Asko Parpola, *The Roots of Hinduism: The Early Aryans and the Indus Civilization* (2015)** -- A more recent and more accessible treatment of Parpola's views, placing the Indus civilization in the broader context of South Asian cultural history.

**Jonathan Mark Kenoyer, *Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization* (1998)** -- A richly illustrated guide to the Indus cities, written by one of the leading excavators of Harappa. Excellent photographs and clear explanations of the archaeological evidence.

**Steve Farmer, Richard Sproat, and Michael Witzel, "The Collapse of the Indus-Script Thesis: The Myth of a Literate Harappan Civilization" (2004)** -- The most provocative challenge to the mainstream view. Available online. Essential reading for anyone interested in the "is it writing?" debate.

**Iravatham Mahadevan, *The Indus Script: Texts, Concordance and Tables* (1977)** -- The foundational concordance of Indus script signs. Technical and specialized, but indispensable for serious students of the script.

**Andrew Robinson, *The Indus: Lost Civilizations* (2015)** -- A concise and accessible popular introduction to the Indus civilization, covering archaeology, art, trade, and the script controversy.

**Shereen Ratnagar, *Understanding Harappa: Civilization in the Greater Indus Valley* (2001)** -- A critical and thought-provoking analysis of Indus social organization, challenging romanticized views of the civilization.

**Vagheesh Narasimhan et al., "The Formation of Human Populations in South and Central Asia" (*Science*, 2019)** -- The landmark ancient DNA study that provided genetic evidence for steppe-derived migration into the Indian subcontinent.

**Jane McIntosh, *The Ancient Indus Valley: New Perspectives* (2008)** -- A comprehensive archaeological overview, useful for its detailed coverage of individual sites and material culture.


# About This Series

**Fascinating Curiosities of Human History** is a series about the mysteries that still surround the ancient world -- the questions that remain unanswered, the civilizations that left incomplete records, and the puzzles that continue to challenge scholars. Each volume focuses on a single mystery or a connected set of mysteries, tracing the evidence, examining the competing theories, and explaining what we know, what we suspect, and what remains genuinely unknown.

The series is designed for curious readers of all ages -- accessible to younger readers (ages nine and up) while remaining substantive and honest enough to engage adults. We do not simplify by removing complexity. We simplify by explaining it clearly.

**Volume 1: The Mystery of Monte Albán** -- The Zapotec capital in the mountains of Oaxaca, its undeciphered script, and the Building of the Dancers.

**Volume 2: The Mystery of the Mapuche** -- The language isolate of southern Chile, the people who defeated the Inca and fought the Spanish to a standstill for three centuries.

**Volume 3: The Mystery of the Olmec** -- The "mother culture" of Mesoamerica, the colossal stone heads, and the Cascajal Block -- sixty-two glyphs in a writing system used once and never seen again.

**Volume 4: The Mystery of Cahokia** -- America's forgotten metropolis, the largest earthen mound in the Americas, and the mass sacrifice of Mound 72.

**Volume 5: The Mystery of Teotihuacan** -- The city of 200,000 people with no known name, no known language, and no known rulers.

**Volume 6: The Mystery of the Toltecs** -- The phantom empire that the Aztecs claimed as their ancestors -- history, legend, or political invention?

**Volume 7: The Mystery Before the Inca** -- Tiwanaku, Puma Punku, and the Gate of the Sun: the engineers of the altiplano who built in stone at 3,800 meters and vanished before the Inca arrived.

**Volume 8: The Mysteries of the Arctic Peoples** -- The Sivullirmiut, the vanished Dorset, and the ivory death masks of Ipiutak: three mysteries from the top of the world.

**Volume 9: The Mystery of Göbekli Tepe** -- The twelve-thousand-year-old temple that was built before agriculture, before pottery, before cities -- and then deliberately buried.

**Volume 10: The Mystery of the Indus Valley Script** -- The only undeciphered writing system of a major Bronze Age civilization, locked behind inscriptions averaging four to five signs long.
