# Part Four: What We Still Don't Know

## Chapter 7 -- The Closed Archives

Intelligence history is written from the margins of the documentary record. The central archives -- the files that would resolve the open questions across all ten volumes of this series -- remain largely inaccessible.

![The landscape of closed archives](../images/placeholders/10-closed-archives.png)

The SIS archive is the most significant gap. MI6 has never been subject to the kind of authorized archival access that MI5 granted to Christopher Andrew for *The Defence of the Realm* (2009). Keith Jeffery's *MI6* (2010) covered only the period 1909-1949, and Jeffery reported that even his access was constrained: certain files were withheld, certain operations remained too sensitive to discuss, and the post-1949 period was excluded entirely. The SIS files for the 1918 Moscow operations -- which might resolve the contradictions between Lockhart's, Reilly's, Hill's, and Ransome's accounts -- have never been released. Jeffery was able to confirm that SIS records exist for this period but was unable to publish from many of them.

The CIA's records are partially accessible. The CIA Historical Review Program has declassified millions of pages, and the Freedom of Information Act has forced the release of many more. The records of Operation Ajax were officially declassified in 2013, sixty years after the event, and confirmed much of what historians had previously reconstructed from British and Iranian sources. But "partially declassified" means partially redacted. Key operational cables, agent identities, and assessments remain blacked out. Malcolm Byrne, at the National Security Archive at George Washington University, has documented the gaps in the declassified Ajax record: the files confirm the operation happened and broadly how, but significant operational details remain classified.

The Russian archives present a different problem. During the brief archival opening of the early 1990s, Western historians gained access to Soviet-era intelligence files that transformed understanding of the early Cold War. The Mitrokhin Archive -- smuggled out by a KGB archivist -- provided extraordinary detail on Soviet intelligence operations. Jonathan Haslam's *Near and Distant Neighbours* (2015) drew on Russian-language sources that Western scholars had previously been unable to consult. But the opening was temporary. Under Putin, the SVR and FSB archives have been progressively re-restricted. The Cheka files from 1918 -- which would give the Soviet perspective on the Lockhart Plot, Reilly's networks, and the Red Terror -- are effectively inaccessible to Western researchers.

The Iranian archives present yet another problem. The SAVAK files were partially seized during the 1979 revolution and partially destroyed. What survived is held by the Islamic Republic's intelligence services and has been selectively published for political purposes rather than scholarly ones. The pre-1953 files of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, now held by BP, are accessible to researchers but have been incompletely catalogued and studied.

The result is that the history told across Volumes 13-21 of this series is built on a documentary base that is structurally incomplete. The memoirs are unreliable -- written by professional deceivers with reputations to protect. The official histories are constrained by what the archives have released. The scholarly literature is rigorous but limited by the sources available.

This is characteristic of intelligence history as a discipline. The historian of intelligence is always working with a partial deck. The question is whether the missing cards would change the hand.


## Chapter 8 -- The Open Questions

Across the ten volumes of this series, certain questions recur. They are unanswered because the evidence to answer them either does not exist, has been destroyed, or remains classified. Listing them honestly is part of the obligation of this series.

**Who was Sidney Reilly?** Volume 14 explored his multiple identities and contradictory origins. The fundamental question -- his actual birth name, nationality, and early biography -- remains unresolved. The SIS personnel files that might settle the matter are classified. Scholars have proposed at least four different origin stories, each supported by fragmentary evidence. Andrew Cook's biography came closest to a definitive account, but Cook himself acknowledged gaps that only the SIS archive could fill.

**What was Arthur Ransome's precise relationship with SIS?** Volume 17 examined the evidence for Ransome as intelligence source, unwitting asset, and possibly double agent. His MI5 file, released under the thirty-year rule, shows that MI5 suspected him of being a Bolshevik sympathizer. His SIS file, which might show what he actually reported and to whom, has never been released. Roland Chambers' biography reconstructed the relationship from the available evidence but could not answer the central question: did Ransome know he was being used, and did he choose to cooperate?

**What exactly happened during the Lockhart Plot?** The memoirs of the principal participants are mutually contradictory on whether the plot was a genuine British operation or a Cheka provocation from the beginning. The "Trust" theory -- that the Cheka controlled the conspiracy throughout and used it to identify and neutralize Allied intelligence networks -- has supporters among historians of Soviet intelligence. The alternative -- that it was a genuine if amateurish conspiracy that was penetrated -- has its own supporters. The Cheka files that might resolve the question are in the FSB archive.

**How much did Stephenson exaggerate?** Volume 18 addressed the documentary problems with the Stephenson legend. William Stevenson's *A Man Called Intrepid* (1976) made claims that subsequent scholarship has significantly qualified. Gill Bennett's work at the Foreign and Commonwealth Office and Nigel West's investigations have narrowed the gap between legend and documented reality, but the BSC organizational files -- which were reportedly destroyed after the war on Churchill's orders -- cannot be consulted.

**Was the 1921 Iranian coup a British intelligence operation?** The degree of British involvement in Reza Khan's seizure of power remains debated. The available evidence -- military reports, diplomatic cables, memoirs of British officers in Iran -- establishes that British personnel facilitated the coup, but whether there was a coordinated intelligence plan or merely opportunistic support by officers on the ground is unclear. The SIS files for Persia in this period have not been released.

**What did Kermit Roosevelt leave out?** Roosevelt's *Countercoup* (1979) was the first insider account of Operation Ajax, but it was written to present the CIA in a favorable light. The declassified CIA internal history, written by Donald Wilber in 1954 and released in 2000, contradicts Roosevelt's account on several points. The full operational files -- agent reports, financial records, communication intercepts -- remain partially classified.

These questions share a common feature: they could be answered, at least partially, by documents that exist but are not accessible. The archives are not empty -- they are closed. Future declassifications, voluntary or compelled, may resolve some of them. The passage of time may render others permanently unanswerable as documents deteriorate and the people who created them die.

The honest conclusion is that the history of twentieth-century intelligence, as it can be written today, is a first draft. The sources that would make it a final draft are behind locked doors. The Great Game, in this sense, continues.
