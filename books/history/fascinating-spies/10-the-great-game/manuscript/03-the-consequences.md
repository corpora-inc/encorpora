# Part Three: The Consequences

## Chapter 5 -- The Iran Thread

No single thread running through the ten volumes is as continuous, as consequential, or as well-documented as the Iran thread. From William Knox D'Arcy's oil concession in 1901 to Operation Ajax in 1953, British and American intelligence maintained an unbroken sixty-year involvement in Iranian affairs. Each intervention created the conditions for the next.

![Timeline of the Iran thread from 1901 to 1953](../images/placeholders/05-iran-thread-timeline.png)

D'Arcy obtained his oil concession from the Qajar Shah in 1901. The terms were extraordinarily favorable to D'Arcy and later to the Anglo-Persian Oil Company (APOC, later Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, later BP): Iran received sixteen percent of net profits, with "net" defined by British accountants. By 1914, when the Royal Navy converted from coal to oil on Winston Churchill's initiative, Iranian oil had become a strategic British interest of the first order. The British government purchased a controlling stake in APOC. From that moment, British policy toward Iran was inseparable from British oil interests.

![Map showing oil fields, pipelines, and strategic positions in Iran](../images/placeholders/08-oil-map.png)

The 1921 coup brought Reza Khan to power with British encouragement, though the precise degree of British involvement remains debated. Scholars including Stephanie Cronin and Cyrus Ghani have examined the available evidence; what is clear is that British military and intelligence personnel in Iran facilitated Reza Khan's march on Tehran, and that the resulting government was more amenable to British interests than the weakening Qajar dynasty. The pattern was established: when Iranian politics threatened British oil interests, British intelligence acted to reshape Iranian politics.

The Second World War brought a joint Anglo-Soviet occupation of Iran in 1941, covered in the context of Volume 19. The Allies deposed Reza Shah, who had tilted toward Germany, and installed his young son Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. Iran became the corridor through which Allied supplies reached the Soviet Union -- the "Persian Corridor" that kept the Eastern Front supplied. British and Soviet intelligence services operated throughout the country, building networks and relationships that would persist long after the war.

The Tehran Conference of 1943, covered in Volume 20, brought Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin to the Iranian capital. The security operations required for the summit -- protecting three heads of state in a city riddled with Axis agents -- drew on decades of British and Soviet intelligence infrastructure in Iran. The conference decided the shape of the postwar world. Iran was the table on which the map was spread, and the Iranians were not invited to sit at it.

The final act, for the purposes of this series, is Operation Ajax in 1953, covered in Volume 21. When Mohammad Mossadegh nationalized the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, the British government's response drew on fifty years of intelligence infrastructure. MI6 planned the initial coup. When the Truman administration refused to participate, MI6 waited. When Eisenhower took office and the Dulles brothers -- John Foster at State, Allen at CIA -- proved more amenable, the operation went forward as a joint MI6-CIA venture. Kermit Roosevelt arrived in Tehran with a plan that rested on relationships, assets, and knowledge accumulated over decades of British intelligence presence.

The thread is continuous. Each intervention built on the previous one's infrastructure, used the previous one's networks, and created the conditions that made the next one necessary. The freelance operatives of 1918 built the networks. The institutions of the interwar period maintained them. The wartime occupation expanded them. Ajax exploited them.


## Chapter 6 -- Blowback

The consequences of covert operations tend to arrive on a delay. The term "blowback" -- originally CIA jargon for the unintended domestic consequences of foreign operations -- applies to the entire arc of the spy series.

![Chain of consequences from covert operations](../images/placeholders/11-blowback-chain.png)

Operation Ajax succeeded in its immediate objective: Mossadegh was removed, the Shah was restored, and a new oil consortium was established that gave American companies a forty percent share alongside the British. In the short term, it was the model covert operation -- cheap, quick, and decisive.

The medium-term consequences were severe. The Shah's government, restored by foreign intelligence services and dependent on their continued support, grew increasingly authoritarian. SAVAK, the Shah's secret police, was trained and advised by the CIA and Mossad. The political opposition that Mossadegh had represented -- secular, nationalist, democratic -- was driven underground and eventually destroyed. The only institution with the organizational capacity and popular legitimacy to oppose the Shah was the mosque. The 1979 Islamic Revolution that overthrew the Shah and produced the Islamic Republic of Iran was, in a meaningful sense, a consequence of the 1953 coup.

Chalmers Johnson, in *Blowback: The Costs and Consequences of American Empire* (2000), traced the concept across multiple theaters. Iran is the clearest single-country example. The template is straightforward: covert action removes a government that threatens Western interests; the replacement government, lacking democratic legitimacy, maintains power through repression; repression generates opposition that is more radical than the original threat; the radical opposition eventually prevails. The cycle took twenty-six years in Iran.

The Ajax template was applied elsewhere with similar results. The CIA's 1954 coup in Guatemala, which overthrew Jacobo Arbenz, followed the Ajax playbook closely -- Kermit Roosevelt's methods, transplanted to Central America. The consequences played out over decades of civil war and political violence. The pattern repeated in multiple forms across the Cold War: Congo, Chile, Indonesia, and elsewhere. Each operation was judged a success by its immediate objectives. Each produced long-term consequences that the planners had not anticipated and the institutions were slow to acknowledge.

The institutional failure was specific. Intelligence agencies are structured to execute operations, not to predict their second- and third-order consequences. The CIA's analytical division -- which might have warned of blowback -- was organizationally separate from the operations division and carried less institutional weight. Tim Weiner documents case after case in which the CIA's own analysts warned of consequences that the operations officers dismissed.

The freelancers of 1918 operated without institutional accountability. The professional agencies that replaced them acquired institutional power without acquiring institutional wisdom. The ability to overthrow governments grew faster than the ability to predict what would replace them.

![Diagram of the legacy -- consequences still unfolding](../images/placeholders/12-legacy-diagram.png)

This is the central lesson of the spy series: intelligence operations are easier to begin than to end. The consequences of covert action extend beyond the planning horizon of the agencies that conduct it. The sixty-year arc from D'Arcy's oil concession to Ajax to the Islamic Revolution to the present is a single continuous story, and it is not finished.
