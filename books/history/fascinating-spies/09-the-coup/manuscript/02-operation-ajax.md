# Part Two: Operation Ajax

## Chapter 3 -- Kermit Roosevelt

Kermit Roosevelt Jr. was born in 1916 into American aristocracy. His grandfather Theodore had charged up San Juan Hill, built the Panama Canal, and reshaped American power. His father, Kermit Roosevelt Sr., had accompanied Theodore on expeditions to Africa and the Amazon. The family name opened every door in Washington.

![Kermit Roosevelt Jr., the CIA officer who directed Operation Ajax](../images/placeholders/01-kermit-roosevelt.png)

Roosevelt graduated from Harvard in 1938, taught history briefly, and joined the Office of Strategic Services during the Second World War. When the OSS was dissolved and the CIA created in 1947, Roosevelt moved to the new agency. By 1953, he was head of operations for the Near East and Africa Division -- a thirty-seven-year-old with authority over covert action across the Middle East. He was confident, charming, and convinced that a small team of skilled operatives could reshape the politics of a nation.

He entered Iran in mid-July 1953, crossing the border from Iraq in the back seat of a car. He carried a false passport. His presence in Tehran was known to a handful of CIA and MI6 officers and to the Rashidian brothers. He set up his operational base in a series of safe houses, moving between them to avoid detection. His principal Iranian contact was Assadollah Rashidian. His principal American colleague was the CIA station chief in Tehran, Roger Goiran -- who opposed the operation and was replaced during its planning by a more cooperative officer, Joseph Goodwin.

Roosevelt's first and most urgent task was the Shah. The operational plan required Mohammad Reza Shah to sign two firmans -- royal decrees -- one dismissing Mosaddegh and one appointing General Fazlollah Zahedi as the new prime minister. Zahedi was a retired army officer who had been interned by the British during the Second World War for suspected pro-German sympathies. He was ambitious, cooperative, and willing to serve as the face of the new government.

The Shah would not commit. Roosevelt met with him secretly on multiple occasions, arriving at the royal palace compound at night. The Shah agonized, delayed, and hedged. He wanted assurances from both the American and British governments that they would support him if the operation succeeded and protect him if it failed. He wanted proof that Eisenhower personally backed the plan. Roosevelt arranged for coded signals: a specific phrase broadcast on BBC Persian Service radio, and a personal message from Eisenhower delivered through a trusted intermediary. The Shah listened, wavered, and eventually agreed -- though his commitment remained fragile throughout.

While Roosevelt worked on the Shah, the propaganda campaign was already underway. The CIA funneled money to Iranian newspapers, paying editors and columnists to publish articles attacking Mosaddegh as a tool of the communists, a destroyer of the economy, and a threat to Islam. Agents provocateurs posed as Tudeh Party supporters and committed acts of vandalism and intimidation designed to frighten the middle class and the bazaar merchants. Religious leaders received subsidies. Thugs were hired through the traditional networks of the zurkhaneh -- the gymnasiums that served as gathering places for Tehran's working-class toughs.

![Timeline of Operation Ajax](../images/placeholders/06-ajax-timeline.png)

The Wilber plan called for a gradual escalation: weeks of propaganda and destabilization, followed by the Shah's decrees, followed by military action to arrest Mosaddegh and install Zahedi. The timing was set for mid-August. Roosevelt's team coordinated with military officers -- notably Colonel Nematollah Nassiri of the Imperial Guard -- who would deliver the Shah's decrees and arrest the prime minister.

The plan looked clean on paper. The reality would be different.


## Chapter 4 -- The First Attempt

The operation launched on the night of August 15, 1953. Colonel Nassiri drove to Mosaddegh's residence with the Shah's firmans -- the royal decrees dismissing the prime minister and appointing Zahedi. The plan called for Nassiri to deliver the decrees, after which loyal military units would secure key government buildings, arrest Mosaddegh's allies, and present the country with a fait accompli by morning.

It went wrong immediately. Mosaddegh had been warned. The precise source of the warning remains debated. Some accounts point to a tip from a Tudeh Party member within the military. Others suggest that Mosaddegh's own intelligence chief, General Taqi Riahi, had been tracking unusual military movements for days. Abrahamian argues that the plan was compromised at multiple points -- too many people knew for secrecy to hold.

When Nassiri arrived at Mosaddegh's house, he was arrested. Government forces, already alerted, moved to secure Tehran. Pro-Mosaddegh military units took control of key positions. By dawn on August 16, the coup had failed. The government radio station broadcast the news: a foreign-backed plot to overthrow the prime minister had been detected and crushed.

The Shah panicked. He had been waiting at his Caspian Sea retreat in Ramsar for news of success. Instead, he received word that the plot had been exposed, that his role was public, and that loyalist forces controlled the capital. He and Queen Soraya boarded a small plane and fled -- first to Baghdad, then to Rome. The Pahlavi dynasty, founded by his father thirty years earlier, appeared to be over.

![The Shah's departure and return](../images/placeholders/08-shah-departure-return.png)

In Washington, the CIA declared the operation a failure. Director Allen Dulles, vacationing on Lake Ontario, received the news with dismay. The State Department began drafting plans for a post-Mosaddegh diplomatic approach. Frank Wisner, the CIA's deputy director for plans, cabled Roosevelt in Tehran: the operation was blown, the Shah had fled, and Roosevelt should leave the country immediately.

Roosevelt received the cable. He did not comply.

Years later, in his memoir *Countercoup*, Roosevelt described his reasoning. He believed the situation was retrievable. The Shah's decrees, though their delivery had been botched, were legally valid -- the Shah had signed them before fleeing. Mosaddegh had won a tactical victory but had not consolidated it. The military was divided. The street, Roosevelt calculated, could still be turned.

Historians debate how much of this retrospective account reflects Roosevelt's actual thinking on August 16 versus how much is reconstruction after the fact. The Wilber document is more honest about the chaos. The CIA's internal history describes confusion, recrimination, and serious doubt about whether the operation could be salvaged. Roosevelt, in Wilber's account, was the one who argued most forcefully for a second attempt.

He had three days.

Between August 16 and August 19, Roosevelt worked the phones, the safe houses, and the network. He met with the Rashidian brothers. He contacted General Zahedi, who had gone into hiding. He arranged for copies of the Shah's firmans to be distributed and publicized -- proof that the Shah had legally dismissed Mosaddegh before fleeing. He coordinated with military officers who remained willing to act. And he made contact with the network of street organizers -- the men who could, for a price, put crowds into the streets of Tehran.

The question of who controlled those crowds -- the CIA, the Rashidian brothers, local political bosses, or the crowds themselves -- is central to the scholarly debate about the coup. Roosevelt's account emphasizes American direction. Abrahamian argues that the Iranian participants had their own motivations and that reducing them to CIA puppets misunderstands the domestic political dynamics. De Bellaigue makes a similar point: many of the people who took to the streets on August 19 were acting on their own grievances, not following CIA instructions.

The truth likely sits between the extremes. The CIA provided money, coordination, and a focal point. Iranian actors -- military officers, clerics, bazaar merchants, street toughs -- provided the mass and the muscle. The operation required both.
