# What We Still Don't Know

**What did the British Operation Boot files contain?** MI6 does not acknowledge Operation Boot's existence. The British government has not declassified intelligence files relating to the 1953 coup. The American record, while increasingly complete, tells only half the story. The Rashidian brothers' network was a British asset, built and funded by MI6 over years. The operational details of that network -- its full membership, its financing, its internal communications -- remain sealed. Without the British files, the British contribution to the coup can only be inferred from the American record and from the memoirs of participants.

**How much of the August 19 street mobilization was organized versus spontaneous?** Roosevelt's memoir and the Wilber document describe the crowds as products of CIA-directed organization. Abrahamian and de Bellaigue argue that many participants had their own motivations -- economic grievance, religious conservatism, factional loyalty -- that aligned with the coup's objectives without being directed by the CIA. The relative weight of organized action versus spontaneous participation on August 19 cannot be determined from the available evidence.

**Why did the Tudeh Party fail to act?** The Tudeh had the most disciplined cadres in Iranian politics. On August 19, they stayed off the streets. Whether this was a deliberate decision by the party leadership, a signal from Moscow, or a failure of communication and nerve is unclear. The party's internal records from this period have not been made available to Western scholars.

**What was the full extent of the CIA's financial expenditure?** The approved budget for TPAJAX was approximately one million dollars. How much was actually spent, and how it was distributed among military officers, newspaper editors, clerics, street organizers, and other recipients, is incompletely documented. Some payments were handled through the Rashidian network using British funds, further complicating the accounting.

**Did Eisenhower personally authorize the second attempt on August 19?** Roosevelt's account implies that he acted on his own judgment after being ordered to abort. The Wilber document does not record a specific authorization for the second attempt from Washington. Whether Roosevelt received informal encouragement through back channels, or whether he genuinely acted without authorization, is a gap in the documentary record.


# Key Names and Terms

**Abrahamian, Ervand** -- Iranian-born historian at Baruch College; author of *The Coup: 1953, the CIA, and the Roots of Modern U.S.-Iranian Relations* (2013), the most thorough scholarly account of the coup.

**Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (AIOC)** -- The renamed Anglo-Persian Oil Company, majority-owned by the British government. Its nationalization by Mosaddegh in 1951 triggered the crisis that led to the coup. Renamed British Petroleum (BP) in 1954.

**Arbenz, Jacobo** (1913-1971) -- President of Guatemala, overthrown in the CIA's Operation PBSUCCESS in 1954, which used the Ajax playbook as its template.

**Byrne, Malcolm** -- American historian at the National Security Archive; editor of declassified CIA documents relating to the 1953 coup.

**De Bellaigue, Christopher** -- British journalist and historian; author of *Patriot of Persia: Muhammad Mossadegh and a Very British Coup* (2012).

**Dulles, Allen** (1893-1969) -- Director of Central Intelligence, 1953-1961. Approved and oversaw Operation TPAJAX. Brother of Secretary of State John Foster Dulles.

**Dulles, John Foster** (1888-1959) -- US Secretary of State under Eisenhower. Viewed the Mosaddegh government through the lens of Cold War anticommunism.

**Firman** -- A royal decree issued by the Shah. The firmans dismissing Mosaddegh and appointing Zahedi provided the legal cover for the coup.

**Gasiorowski, Mark** -- American political scientist; co-editor with Malcolm Byrne of *Mohammad Mosaddeq and the 1953 Coup in Iran* (2004).

**Goiran, Roger** -- CIA station chief in Tehran who opposed the coup plan. Replaced before the operation by Joseph Goodwin.

**Kashani, Ayatollah Abol-Ghasem** (1882-1962) -- Populist Shia cleric who initially supported Mosaddegh but broke with him by 1953. His followers participated in the August 19 demonstrations.

**Kinzer, Stephen** -- American journalist and author of *All the Shah's Men: An American Coup and the Roots of Middle East Terror* (2003).

**Majles** (maj-LES) -- The Iranian parliament.

**Mosaddegh, Mohammad** (1882-1967) -- Prime Minister of Iran, 1951-1953. Nationalized the Iranian oil industry. Overthrown in the August 1953 coup. Died under house arrest.

**Nassiri, Nematollah** (1911-1979) -- Colonel of the Imperial Guard who attempted to deliver the Shah's firmans to Mosaddegh on August 15, 1953. Arrested during the failed first attempt. Later became head of SAVAK. Executed after the 1979 revolution.

**National Front** -- The political coalition led by Mosaddegh, comprising nationalists, liberal democrats, bazaar merchants, and some clerical figures.

**Operation Boot** -- MI6 codename for the British plan to overthrow Mosaddegh. Preceded and was absorbed into the CIA's Operation TPAJAX.

**Operation TPAJAX** -- CIA codename for the 1953 coup operation. "TP" was the CIA's country digraph for Iran.

**Rashidian brothers** -- Assadollah, Qodratollah, and Seyfollah Rashidian. Wealthy Iranian brothers who ran MI6's principal agent network in Iran. Central to both Operation Boot and TPAJAX.

**Roosevelt, Kermit Jr.** (1916-2000) -- CIA officer who directed Operation TPAJAX from inside Tehran. Grandson of President Theodore Roosevelt. Author of *Countercoup* (1979).

**SAVAK** -- The Organization of Information and Security of the Nation. Iran's secret police, established in 1957 with American and Israeli assistance.

**Shah, Mohammad Reza Pahlavi** (1919-1980) -- Shah of Iran, 1941-1979. His constitutional authority to dismiss the prime minister provided the legal basis for the coup. Fled Iran during the 1979 revolution. Died in exile in Cairo.

**Tudeh Party** -- The Iranian communist party. Its growing strength was cited as justification for the coup, though scholars debate the actual extent of Soviet influence.

**Wilber, Donald** (1907-1997) -- CIA officer and scholar of Persian architecture. Author of the CIA's internal history of Operation TPAJAX, drafted in March 1954 and partially declassified beginning in 2000.

**Zahedi, Fazlollah** (1897-1963) -- Retired Iranian general installed as prime minister by the coup. Had been interned by the British during the Second World War for suspected pro-German sympathies.

**Zurkhaneh** (zoor-KHAH-neh) -- Traditional Iranian gymnasium. The zurkhaneh networks served as organizing points for street mobilization during the coup.


# Further Reading

**Ervand Abrahamian, *The Coup: 1953, the CIA, and the Roots of Modern U.S.-Iranian Relations* (2013)** -- The most rigorous scholarly account. Abrahamian draws on declassified American documents, Iranian sources, and his deep knowledge of Iranian political history to produce an account that is critical of both the CIA and the popular mythology surrounding the coup. Essential reading.

**Kermit Roosevelt, *Countercoup: The Struggle for the Control of Iran* (1979)** -- The CIA officer's own account. Self-serving, sanitized, and sometimes unreliable, but indispensable as a primary source. Roosevelt's narrative of the August 15-19 sequence, while shaped by self-justification, provides operational detail available nowhere else.

**Malcolm Byrne, ed., *The CIA in Iran* (declassified documents, National Security Archive)** -- The Wilber document and related declassified files, available through the National Security Archive at George Washington University. The primary documentary foundation for any study of the coup.

**Mark Gasiorowski and Malcolm Byrne, eds., *Mohammad Mosaddeq and the 1953 Coup in Iran* (2004)** -- Collected essays by leading scholars, with declassified documents. The most balanced multi-perspective treatment. Gasiorowski's own chapters on the CIA's role are particularly valuable.

**Christopher de Bellaigue, *Patriot of Persia: Muhammad Mossadegh and a Very British Coup* (2012)** -- A biography of Mosaddegh that centers Iranian perspectives. De Bellaigue, a longtime resident of Iran, writes with knowledge of Persian sources and sympathy for his subject without hagiography.

**Stephen Kinzer, *All the Shah's Men: An American Coup and the Roots of Middle East Terror* (2003)** -- The most accessible narrative for general readers. Kinzer writes clearly and dramatically, though some specialists consider his treatment of the Cold War context insufficiently nuanced.

**Darioush Bayandor, *Iran and the CIA: The Fall of Mosaddeq Revisited* (2010)** -- A contrarian account that downplays the CIA's role and emphasizes internal Iranian factors. Not the scholarly consensus, but a useful corrective to narratives that reduce the coup to a purely American operation.

**Ali Rahnema, *Behind the 1953 Coup in Iran: Thugs, Turncoats, Soldiers, and Spooks* (2015)** -- The most detailed account of the Iranian participants, drawing on Persian-language sources. Rahnema's treatment of the August 19 street mobilization is the most granular available.

Each volume begins with a genuine mystery or curiosity and uses it as a doorway into a world of knowledge -- the people, the places, the context, and the open questions.

The same editorial standards apply: parametric knowledge only, honest about gaps and uncertainties, competing scholarly accounts given fair treatment.
