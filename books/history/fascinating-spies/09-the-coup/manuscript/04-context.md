# Part Four: The Record and the Reckoning

## Chapter 7 -- The Documents

The history of the 1953 coup is also a history of its documentation -- what was written down, what was classified, what was leaked, what was declassified, and what remains hidden.

![The CIA internal history -- partial declassification timeline](../images/placeholders/10-cia-internal-history.png)

The most important single document is the CIA's internal history of the operation, written in March 1954 by Donald Wilber. Wilber was a career intelligence officer and a genuine scholar of Persian art and architecture -- the author of several academic books on Iranian mosques and gardens. He had helped plan TPAJAX and wrote the after-action report within months of the coup's success. The resulting document, approximately two hundred pages, described the planning process, the operational sequence, the participants, the propaganda campaign, and the outcome in granular detail. It was classified Top Secret and restricted to a handful of readers within the agency.

The Wilber document leaked in 2000 when the *New York Times* obtained a copy and published it in full. The CIA had previously released a heavily redacted version in 1981 in response to Freedom of Information Act requests. The full text confirmed what historians had suspected: the coup was a joint CIA-MI6 operation, planned in detail, funded with American covert dollars, and executed through a network of Iranian agents. The document also revealed the operation's fragility -- the failed first attempt, the chaos of August 16-18, and the improvisation that characterized the second attempt.

Further declassification followed. In 2013, the CIA acknowledged publicly for the first time that it had played a role in the coup -- a fact that had been an open secret for decades but had never been officially confirmed. The acknowledgment came in a batch of documents released through the State Department's Foreign Relations of the United States series. In 2017, additional CIA documents were declassified, adding detail to the operational picture.

The British files remain more tightly held. MI6 does not officially acknowledge the existence of Operation Boot. The British government's position is that intelligence files from this period are either destroyed, retained under permanent exemption from disclosure, or not yet eligible for release. Historians working on the British side of the operation have relied on peripheral sources -- Foreign Office diplomatic files, memoirs of participants, and inferences drawn from the American record.

What do the documents show? Several things that complicate the popular narrative.

First, the operation was conceived as a British project. The idea of overthrowing Mosaddegh originated in London, the Rashidian network was a British asset, and the operational concept of Operation Boot predated American involvement. The CIA's contribution was resources, personnel, and political backing -- substantial, but layered onto a British foundation.

Second, the Shah's cooperation was uncertain until the last moment. The documents reveal a monarch who was genuinely frightened, who delayed and equivocated, and who had to be pressured, reassured, and nearly coerced into signing the decrees that gave the coup its legal cover. The image of the Shah as a willing partner masks a more complex reality.

Third, the first attempt failed. The clean sequence described in the Wilber plan -- decree, arrest, announcement -- collapsed on August 15-16. The second attempt on August 19, while it succeeded, was substantially improvised. Roosevelt's decision to stay in Tehran after being ordered to leave was an act of insubordination. Whether it was courageous or reckless depends on the reader's perspective, but it was undertaken without authorization.

Fourth, the role of Iranian agency is more significant than the American-centered narrative suggests. The military officers who deployed tanks on August 19 had their own reasons for opposing Mosaddegh. The clerical figures who mobilized their followers had their own grievances. The bazaar merchants who funded and organized street demonstrations were responding to economic conditions created by the British embargo as much as to CIA instructions. The coup succeeded because a sufficient number of Iranians, for a sufficient variety of reasons, were willing to act against the government. The CIA provided the catalyst, the coordination, and the money. It did not provide the underlying conditions.


## Chapter 8 -- The Consequences

The 1953 coup in Iran created a template. Within a year of Ajax, the CIA executed Operation PBSUCCESS in Guatemala, overthrowing President Jacobo Arbenz using a similar combination of propaganda, bribed military officers, and manufactured street chaos. The Guatemala operation borrowed directly from the Ajax playbook. Further operations followed in Congo, Chile, and elsewhere. The institutional lesson the CIA drew from Tehran was that covert action could change governments quickly, cheaply, and deniably.

![The chain of consequences from 1953](../images/placeholders/11-consequences-chain.png)

The lesson was wrong, or at best incomplete. Each subsequent operation carried consequences that unfolded over decades. The Shah's regime, sustained by American support and SAVAK's repression, produced the 1979 revolution and the Islamic Republic. The Guatemala coup produced decades of military dictatorship and civil war. The pattern held: covert regime change solved immediate Cold War anxieties and created long-term catastrophes.

The specific consequences of the Iran coup cascaded through the second half of the twentieth century and into the twenty-first. The Shah's dependence on American support shaped US-Iran relations for a generation. American military and intelligence advisors embedded in Iranian institutions. Arms sales flowed. The Shah became America's primary strategic partner in the Persian Gulf -- the "policeman of the Gulf" in the Nixon Doctrine's framework. When the Shah fell in 1979, the United States lost its most important regional ally overnight.

The Islamic Revolution drew explicit connections to 1953. Khomeini and the revolutionary movement framed the Shah's entire reign as illegitimate, a foreign imposition sustained by foreign power. The seizure of the American embassy was designed as a symbolic reversal of the coup -- the spy center captured, the intelligence files exposed, the relationship of domination inverted. The hostage crisis poisoned US-Iran relations for decades. Its effects persist.

The coup also shaped Iranian political culture in ways that extend beyond any single government. The experience of 1953 embedded a deep suspicion of foreign interference in Iranian political consciousness. Conspiracy theories about American and British manipulation of Iranian affairs are pervasive across the political spectrum. Some of these theories are well-founded -- the coup happened. Others are exaggerated or fabricated. The difficulty of distinguishing real foreign interference from imagined interference is itself a consequence of the coup: when a democracy has been overthrown by foreign intelligence services, distrust of foreign powers becomes rational.

In 2000, Secretary of State Madeleine Albright acknowledged the American role in the coup for the first time at a senior government level, calling it "a setback for Iran's political development." In 2013, the CIA's formal declassification confirmed the operational details. In 2009, President Barack Obama, in a speech in Cairo, referred to the United States' role in "the overthrow of a democratically elected Iranian government" during the Cold War. These acknowledgments came decades too late to alter the relationship.

The scholarly debate continues to evolve as new documents become available. Abrahamian's *The Coup* (2013) argued that the operation was driven more by oil interests than by Cold War anticommunism -- that the Eisenhower administration used the communist threat as a pretext for what was fundamentally a resource grab. Gasiorowski and Byrne's collected volume (2004) presented a more balanced picture, showing how genuine Cold War fears, British imperial interests, and oil economics interacted. De Bellaigue's biography of Mosaddegh (2012) centered Iranian perspectives and agency. Kinzer's *All the Shah's Men* (2003) provided the most accessible narrative for general readers.

The connection to the earlier volumes of this series is direct. Volume 19, *The Shadow War in Persia*, traced the history of foreign intelligence operations in Iran from the D'Arcy Concession in 1901 through the Allied occupation of 1941-1946. Volume 20, *Tehran 1943*, described the wartime conference that brought Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin to the Iranian capital. The 1953 coup was the culmination of fifty years of foreign intervention in Iran -- the moment when covert manipulation of Iranian politics, practiced by Britain since the 1901 oil concession and joined by the United States during the Second World War, reached its most dramatic expression.

The oil that William Knox D'Arcy found at Masjed Soleyman in 1908 shaped every chapter of this story. British intelligence operations in Iran were designed to protect the oil. The Allied invasion of 1941 was launched to secure the oil and the supply corridor. The 1953 coup was triggered by Mosaddegh's attempt to reclaim the oil for Iran. The Shah was restored to protect Western access to the oil. The revolution that destroyed the Shah's regime was fueled by the resentment that half a century of foreign control over Iranian oil had produced.

Kermit Roosevelt's grandson sits in the Roosevelt family's place in the American establishment. The Abadan refinery, prize of empires, is Iranian. The documents keep coming out, each batch revealing a little more of what happened in that Tehran basement in August 1953 -- and a little more of what it cost.
