# Part Three: The Overthrow

## Chapter 5 -- August 19

The morning of August 19, 1953, began with crowds. They emerged from the south of Tehran -- from the bazaar districts, the working-class neighborhoods, the zurkhaneh gymnasiums. They carried portraits of the Shah. They chanted royalist slogans. By mid-morning, they numbered in the thousands.

![The streets of Tehran, August 19, 1953](../images/placeholders/07-august-19-streets.png)

The composition of these crowds tells the story of the coup's mechanics. Some participants were paid agents of the Rashidian network, organized in advance and given specific instructions about where to march and what to shout. Some were followers of Ayatollah Kashani, the populist cleric who had once been Mosaddegh's ally but had broken with him over the prime minister's secular governance and diminishing willingness to share power. Some were bazaar merchants whose businesses had been ruined by the economic crisis of the embargo. Some were soldiers and police officers in civilian clothes. Some were members of street gangs with loyalties to local bosses who had been paid by the CIA or the Rashidians. And some were ordinary people caught up in the momentum of a crowd, drawn by noise and excitement and the sense that something decisive was happening.

Roosevelt monitored events from a safe house, listening to radio reports and receiving updates from runners. The Wilber document records his communications with the network throughout the day. He was coordinating, encouraging, directing -- but the degree to which he controlled events versus riding a wave that had its own momentum is precisely the question that divides historians.

By late morning, the royalist crowds had gained the initiative. Pro-Mosaddegh forces, which had controlled the streets after the failed first attempt, were slow to mobilize. The Tudeh Party, which could have fielded its own disciplined cadres, received no order to act -- a decision that remains one of the enduring puzzles of the day. Some scholars suggest the Tudeh leadership was cautious, fearing that open confrontation would provoke a military crackdown. Others argue that Moscow, already negotiating with the West on other matters, signaled the Tudeh to stand down. The party's own internal records for this period remain inaccessible.

The military made its move in the early afternoon. Pro-Shah officers, coordinated through the network Roosevelt and the Rashidians had assembled, deployed tanks and troops. The sequence was rapid. Loyalist units seized the radio station, the telegraph office, the police headquarters, and the army general staff building. General Zahedi, emerging from hiding, went to the radio station and announced that he was the lawful prime minister, appointed by the Shah's decree.

The decisive battle was at Mosaddegh's residence on Kakh Street. Tanks and infantry surrounded the house. A firefight broke out that lasted several hours. Mosaddegh's personal guard, loyal to the end, resisted until they were overwhelmed. The house was shelled. At least forty people were killed in the fighting around Kakh Street, and the true number may have been higher. Mosaddegh escaped over a back wall during the assault. He surrendered the following day.

![Map of the coup operations in Tehran](../images/placeholders/03-tehran-coup-map.png)

By nightfall, the coup was complete. Zahedi controlled the government. The army controlled the streets. Mosaddegh was under arrest. Roosevelt, from his safe house, sent a cable to Washington reporting success.

The total death toll of August 19 is estimated at between two hundred and three hundred people, though precise figures are difficult to establish. The violence was concentrated in a few hours. The speed of the outcome -- from morning crowds to evening capitulation -- surprised even the operation's planners.

In Rome, the Shah received the news. He had spent the intervening days in visible despair, giving interviews in which he speculated about a life in exile. Now he prepared to return. He flew to Baghdad, then to Tehran, arriving on August 22 to orchestrated celebrations. His three-day absence was rewritten, in the official narrative that followed, as a brief constitutional formality rather than a panicked flight.


## Chapter 6 -- The Restoration

Mosaddegh was tried by a military tribunal in November 1953. He used the trial as a platform, delivering impassioned speeches about sovereignty, constitutionalism, and the right of nations to control their own resources. He was convicted of treason and sentenced to three years in solitary confinement, followed by house arrest for the remainder of his life. He died in 1967 at the age of eighty-four, still under house arrest, in the village of Ahmadabad. The government refused to allow a public funeral.

The oil industry was reorganized. Nationalization was formally reversed. A new consortium replaced the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, but the terms reflected the new balance of power: American oil companies received a forty percent share, the British retained forty percent (through the renamed British Petroleum), and the remaining twenty percent went to French and Dutch firms. Iran's share of revenues increased to fifty percent of profits -- the same terms that Saudi Arabia had negotiated with ARAMCO in 1950, the terms that Mosaddegh had demanded before nationalization. The irony was precise: Iran received through a coup roughly what it could have received through negotiation, minus its sovereignty.

The Shah consolidated power with systematic efficiency. The Tudeh Party was crushed. Its military network, which had been penetrated by Iranian intelligence even before the coup, was rolled up. Hundreds of party members were arrested. Some were executed. The National Front was dismantled. Mosaddegh's allies were imprisoned, exiled, or silenced. The press was brought under control.

SAVAK -- the Organization of Information and Security of the Nation -- was created in 1957 with American and Israeli assistance. It became one of the most feared secret police organizations in the world. SAVAK monitored political dissent, tortured prisoners, and maintained surveillance networks that penetrated every level of Iranian society. The CIA and Mossad provided training, techniques, and equipment. The security state that followed the coup was an American co-production.

The Shah's rule lasted twenty-six years. He modernized Iran's infrastructure, expanded education, and used oil revenues to build a military that became the most powerful in the region. He also suppressed political opposition, concentrated power in the monarchy, alienated the clergy, and created the conditions for the revolution that would destroy his regime.

On February 1, 1979, Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini returned to Iran from exile. Within weeks, the Pahlavi dynasty was finished. The revolutionaries who overthrew the Shah cited 1953 as the original sin -- the moment when Iranian democracy was destroyed by foreign intervention. When revolutionary students seized the American embassy in Tehran on November 4, 1979, taking fifty-two hostages for 444 days, they called the embassy a "den of spies." The phrase reached back twenty-six years to the basement where Kermit Roosevelt had directed Operation Ajax.

Roosevelt himself left Iran shortly after the coup, returning to Washington as a hero within the clandestine service. Allen Dulles reportedly called Ajax a triumph. The CIA immediately began planning a similar operation in Guatemala, which was executed in 1954. Roosevelt was offered the Guatemala operation but declined. He left the CIA in 1958 and moved to the private sector, working as a lobbyist and consultant for American oil companies in the Middle East -- a career trajectory that connected the two sides of the coup's legacy with uncomfortable directness.

He published his memoir, *Countercoup*, in 1979, the same year the Shah fell. The book was self-serving and sanitized, but it confirmed the basic outlines of the operation. He died in 2000 at the age of eighty-four. The CIA's full internal history of the operation he directed was still classified at the time of his death.
