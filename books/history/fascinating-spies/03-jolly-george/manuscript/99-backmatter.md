# What We Still Don't Know

The honest list is substantial. These questions remain genuinely open — the archives have yielded much, but significant gaps persist.

**What was the full extent of Hill's agent network in Moscow?** His memoirs describe an elaborate multi-layered operation. Official records suggest something more modest. The truth may be unrecoverable — agent networks in revolutionary conditions leave fewer paper trails than peacetime operations.

**What happened to Hill's Russian agents?** Hill's memoirs are largely silent on this question. Some were certainly arrested by the Cheka after the Lockhart affair. Whether any were executed, and how many survived, is unclear from available sources.

**Did Hill actually organize sabotage operations in Ukraine?** He claimed extensive operations behind German lines. Independent corroboration is thin. The operations are plausible given British strategic interests but difficult to verify from surviving records.

**What was Hill's precise role in the Lockhart Plot?** He was connected to the other participants but his specific involvement remains debated. His own accounts vary between his two memoirs.

**What intelligence did the SOE-NKVD liaison actually produce?** The wartime files are partially declassified. The full scope of what was exchanged — and what was withheld — by each side has yet to be fully documented.

**How much did the NKVD know about Hill's 1918 operations when they accepted him as liaison in 1941?** Soviet intelligence had extensive files. Whether they briefed their liaison officers on Hill's anti-Bolshevik past, and how that knowledge shaped the relationship, is unknown.

**Which specific claims in Hill's memoirs are fabricated versus embellished versus accurate?** Scholars have made progress on individual claims, but much of the middle ground — stories that are plausible but unverifiable — remains in limbo.

These gaps are the frontier. Every declassification of intelligence archives, every newly opened collection, every doctoral thesis that digs into a previously unexplored corner of the record brings the possibility of answers. Hill's story is still being written.


# Key Names and Terms

**Brest-Litovsk, Treaty of** — The peace treaty signed on March 3, 1918, between Soviet Russia and the Central Powers. Russia ceded vast territories including Finland, the Baltic states, Poland, and Ukraine.

**Cheka** (cheh-KAH) — The All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage, established December 1917. The first Soviet security and intelligence agency, predecessor to the GPU, OGPU, NKVD, and eventually the KGB.

**Cumming, Mansfield** (1859-1923) — The first chief of the Secret Intelligence Service (SIS/MI6), known as "C." Authorized many of the British intelligence operations in Russia.

**Dzerzhinsky, Felix** (jer-ZHIN-skee) (1877-1926) — Polish-born Bolshevik revolutionary, founder and first chairman of the Cheka.

**Hill, George Alexander** (1892-1968) — British intelligence officer who operated in revolutionary Russia 1917-1919 and served as SOE liaison with the NKVD 1941-1945. Author of *Go Spy the Land* (1932) and *The Dreaded Hour* (1936).

**Kaplan, Fanny** (1890-1918) — Socialist Revolutionary who shot and seriously wounded Lenin on August 30, 1918. Executed by the Cheka on September 3, 1918. The assassination attempt triggered the Red Terror.

**Kerensky, Alexander** (1881-1970) — Head of the Russian Provisional Government from July to October 1917. Overthrown by the Bolshevik seizure of power.

**Lockhart, Robert Bruce** (1887-1970) — British diplomat and intelligence officer in Russia 1918. His involvement in the "Lockhart Plot" led to his arrest and eventual exchange. Author of *Memoirs of a British Agent* (1932).

**NKVD** — The People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs, the Soviet security and intelligence agency during 1934-1946. Successor to the OGPU, predecessor to the MGB and KGB.

**Provisional Government** — The Russian government that governed from the February Revolution (March 1917) to the Bolshevik seizure of power (November 1917), led successively by Prince Lvov and Alexander Kerensky.

**Ransome, Arthur** (1884-1967) — British journalist, children's author (*Swallows and Amazons*), and intelligence asset who provided reports to SIS from revolutionary Russia. Subject of Volume 17 in this series.

**Red Terror** — The campaign of mass repression carried out by the Cheka beginning September 5, 1918, in response to the assassination attempt on Lenin and the perceived threat of counter-revolution.

**Reilly, Sidney** (1873-1925) — British intelligence officer, born Sigmund Rosenblum in Odessa, who operated in revolutionary Russia alongside Hill. Lured back to the Soviet Union in 1925 and executed. Subject of Volume 14 in this series.

**SIS** — The Secret Intelligence Service, Britain's foreign intelligence agency, commonly known as MI6.

**SOE** — The Special Operations Executive, established July 1940 to conduct sabotage and subversion in occupied Europe. Disbanded in 1946.

**Skoropadskyi, Pavlo** (1873-1945) — Ukrainian Hetman installed by the German occupation authorities in April 1918, replacing the Ukrainian People's Republic. His government collapsed when Germany withdrew.


# Further Reading

These are the best starting points for a reader who wants to go deeper. They are listed roughly in order of accessibility.

**For the general reader:**

- Hill, George Alexander. *Go Spy the Land: Being the Adventures of IK8 of the British Secret Service*. Cassell, 1932. — Hill's own account of his 1917-1919 operations. Entertaining and unreliable. Essential primary source.

- Hill, George Alexander. *The Dreaded Hour*. Cassell, 1936. — Hill's second memoir, covering overlapping events with somewhat different details.

- Lockhart, Robert Bruce. *Memoirs of a British Agent*. Putnam, 1932. — Lockhart's account of his time in Russia, including the plot that bears his name. More restrained than Hill's memoirs, though not without its own embellishments.

**For the scholarly context:**

- Jeffery, Keith. *MI6: The History of the Secret Intelligence Service 1909-1949*. Bloomsbury, 2010. — The authorized history, with archival access. The single most important secondary source for evaluating Hill's claims.

- Service, Robert. *Spies and Commissars: Bolshevik Russia and the West*. Macmillan, 2011. — Comprehensive scholarly account of the British intelligence community in revolutionary Russia. Places Hill in context alongside Reilly, Lockhart, and others.

- Haslam, Jonathan. *Near and Distant Neighbours: A New History of Soviet Intelligence*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2015. — The Soviet side of the story. Essential for understanding what the Cheka knew about British operations.

**For the broader intelligence context:**

- Foot, M.R.D. *SOE: An Outline History of the Special Operations Executive 1940-46*. BBC, 1984. — Authoritative overview of SOE, including the NKVD liaison in which Hill served.

- Andrew, Christopher. *The Defence of the Realm: The Authorized History of MI5*. Allen Lane, 2009. — Domestic counterintelligence perspective. Relevant for understanding the broader British intelligence apparatus.

- Cook, Andrew. *Ace of Spies: The True Story of Sidney Reilly*. Tempus, 2004. — Scholarly biography of Reilly, Hill's colleague and rival. Good comparative source.
