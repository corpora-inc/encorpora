# Part Three: The Network Unravels

## Chapter 5 — The Cheka Closes In

The Cheka — the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage — was established on December 20, 1917, barely seven weeks after the Bolshevik seizure of power. Its founder and chairman, Felix Dzerzhinsky, was a Polish-born revolutionary whose ascetic personal discipline masked a capacity for systematic ruthlessness that would define Soviet security services for decades.

In its early months, the Cheka was improvised and understaffed. It inherited no established intelligence bureaucracy — the Tsarist Okhrana had been dissolved, its files scattered, its officers dispersed or in hiding. Dzerzhinsky built a new security apparatus almost from scratch, recruiting party loyalists and drawing on the Bolsheviks' conspiratorial experience as a formerly underground movement.

By mid-1918, the Cheka had grown rapidly. The catalyst was not just the British intelligence networks but the broader pattern of anti-Bolshevik resistance. The Russian Civil War was expanding. White armies were forming on the periphery. The Left Socialist Revolutionaries, formerly Bolshevik coalition partners, had broken with the government and attempted an uprising in July 1918. On August 30, Fanny Kaplan shot Lenin outside a Moscow factory, seriously wounding him. The Bolsheviks responded with the Red Terror — a systematic campaign of arrests, hostage-taking, and executions directed at perceived class enemies and foreign conspirators.

The British intelligence networks in Russia collapsed under this pressure. The Lockhart affair gave the Cheka a narrative — a proven foreign conspiracy — and a mandate to hunt down every connected thread. Agents were arrested. Safe houses were raided. Informants were turned or silenced.

Hill's network was caught in this general catastrophe. His memoirs describe a period of frantic activity — burning documents, warning agents, shutting down communication channels, moving constantly to avoid arrest. Some of his agents were arrested. Others went silent, whether from capture, fear, or simple self-preservation. The infrastructure he had built over months was dismantled in weeks.

The Cheka's success against the British networks was partly a matter of resources and determination, partly a matter of penetration. Soviet intelligence histories, drawing on Cheka archives opened after the Cold War, suggest that the Bolshevik security service had informants inside or adjacent to the British operations earlier than the British realized. Jonathan Haslam, in *Near and Distant Neighbours*, documents how the Cheka's counterintelligence capabilities developed with surprising speed, aided by the Bolsheviks' own experience as a clandestine organization that had spent years evading the Tsarist secret police.

Hill's escape from Russia is described differently in his two memoirs. In *Go Spy the Land*, the departure is a tense, well-organized operation involving forged papers, disguises, and nerve-wracking border crossings. In *The Dreaded Hour*, some of the details shift. Scholars have noted that neither account can be fully reconciled with the other, and that both may contain invented elements alongside genuine experiences.

What the independent record confirms is that Hill did escape Russia, that the British intelligence networks in Moscow and Petrograd were effectively destroyed by late 1918, and that the Cheka's counterintelligence operation was broadly successful. The human cost was significant: agents who had worked for the British were arrested, imprisoned, and in some cases executed. Hill's memoirs are largely silent on the fate of his Russian agents — a silence that later scholars have noted with varying degrees of criticism.

The aftermath of the Lockhart Plot and the Red Terror marked the end of the first phase of British intelligence operations in Soviet Russia. The networks were gone. The officers were out. The diplomatic relationship was in ruins. It would take years to rebuild any intelligence presence in the Soviet Union, and when it was rebuilt, it would operate on very different terms.

Hill returned to Britain. He received military decorations for his service. He was debriefed by military intelligence. And then, like many intelligence officers of his generation, he largely disappeared from the public record for over a decade — until his memoirs brought him back.


## Chapter 6 — The Return

In June 1941, Germany invaded the Soviet Union. Operation Barbarossa, the largest military invasion in history, sent over three million German soldiers crashing into Soviet territory along a front stretching from the Baltic to the Black Sea. The Soviet Union, caught off-guard despite numerous intelligence warnings, suffered catastrophic losses in the opening weeks.

Overnight, Britain and the Soviet Union became allies against a common enemy. Winston Churchill, who had been one of the most vocal advocates of intervention against the Bolsheviks in 1918-1920, declared that he would ally with the Devil himself to defeat Hitler. The political transformation was total and immediate.

This unlikely alliance created an equally unlikely intelligence liaison. Britain's Special Operations Executive — SOE, established in July 1940 to "set Europe ablaze" through sabotage and subversion behind enemy lines — needed to coordinate with the Soviet Union. The NKVD, the Soviet security and intelligence agency, was the natural counterpart. Both organizations conducted clandestine operations in occupied Europe. Both had an interest in sharing certain types of intelligence about German forces and occupied territories. Both were deeply suspicious of each other.

George Hill was the man chosen for this improbable job. His qualifications were obvious: he spoke fluent Russian, he had extensive experience in Russia, he understood Soviet institutions from long observation, and he held the rank and standing to serve as a credible liaison. That he had spent 1918 running intelligence operations against the Bolsheviks — the very ancestors of the regime he was now liaising with — added an irony that was presumably not lost on anyone involved.

Hill arrived in Moscow in the autumn of 1941, during one of the darkest moments of the war for the Soviet Union. The German army was approaching Moscow. The Soviet government had evacuated much of its apparatus to Kuibyshev (now Samara), over eight hundred kilometers to the east. The city was under air attack. Panic had gripped parts of the population, with looting and disorder in the streets before order was restored.

![Hill meeting with NKVD liaison officers](../images/placeholders/08-hill-nkvd-liaison.png)

Hill's role as SOE liaison with the NKVD was carefully circumscribed. The relationship was cooperative in theory, suspicious in practice. The two sides exchanged some operational intelligence about German forces. They coordinated on certain aspects of sabotage and subversion in occupied territories. SOE provided some equipment and training materials to the NKVD. The NKVD provided some intelligence about conditions behind German lines.

The limits of the relationship were real. Neither side shared its most sensitive intelligence. The NKVD was simultaneously running its own intelligence operations against Britain — Soviet espionage in wartime London is well documented. SOE was aware of this dual game. Hill, as the man in the middle, had to maintain a working relationship with counterparts who were both allies and adversaries.

M.R.D. Foot, in his authoritative history of SOE, describes the liaison as functional but constrained. Hill kept the relationship going through four years of war, navigating the politics of the alliance, the suspicions of both sides, and the genuine difficulties of coordinating clandestine operations between two fundamentally incompatible political systems.

Hill remained in Moscow until 1945. He witnessed the turning of the war — Stalingrad, Kursk, the long Soviet advance westward. He maintained the SOE-NKVD liaison through periods of relative warmth and periods of deep chill. He was, by all accounts, effective at the job: keeping channels open, managing expectations, and preventing the inevitable frictions from escalating into breaks.

The war ended. The alliance dissolved. The Cold War began. Hill returned to Britain for the last time, carrying the distinction of having served in Russian intelligence operations in two world wars and having worked both against and alongside Soviet intelligence. He died in 1968, largely forgotten by the public but remembered in the intelligence community as one of the more remarkable figures of his generation.
