# Part One: The Soldier's War

## Chapter 1 — The Eastern Front

George Alexander Hill was born in 1892 in Estonia, then part of the Russian Empire. His father was a British merchant based in the Baltic, and Hill grew up bilingual in English and Russian. This accident of birth gave him the single most valuable asset a British intelligence officer could possess in the years ahead: he could pass as a Russian.

Hill's early life moved between Britain and the Russian Empire. He was educated in England but returned regularly to the family's Baltic connections. By the time the First World War began in 1914, he was a young man with military training, fluent Russian, and a working knowledge of the vast, chaotic territory that stretched from the Baltic to the Black Sea.

He joined the British Army and served initially on the Western Front. The trenches of France and Flanders were the war's central slaughterhouse, but for a man who spoke Russian, the Eastern Front held obvious possibilities. By 1917, Hill had been transferred to military intelligence work connected to Britain's alliance with Russia.

The Eastern Front was a different kind of war. The distances were enormous — the front line stretched over a thousand kilometers. The fighting was mobile in ways the Western Front was not, with sweeping advances and retreats across open terrain. The Russian army was vast, poorly supplied, and increasingly demoralized. By early 1917, Russia had suffered catastrophic losses: estimates range from five to eight million casualties since the war began. Desertion was epidemic. Entire units refused orders.

![Map of the Eastern Front showing key positions in 1917](../images/placeholders/02-eastern-front-map.png)

In February 1917, the war on the Eastern Front became something else entirely. Revolution broke out in Petrograd. Tsar Nicholas II abdicated. A Provisional Government took power, pledging to continue the war against Germany and Austria-Hungary. Britain and France desperately needed Russia to keep fighting — if the Eastern Front collapsed, Germany could transfer millions of soldiers westward and potentially break the Allied line in France.

This was the strategic context that shaped Hill's war. Britain was willing to do almost anything to keep Russia in the fight. Military missions, diplomatic pressure, financial support, intelligence operations — all were deployed to prop up the Provisional Government and its commitment to the Allied cause.

Hill arrived in Russia during this period of maximum instability. The Provisional Government under Alexander Kerensky lurched from crisis to crisis, challenged from the left by the Soviets (councils of workers and soldiers) and from the right by military officers who wanted order restored at any price. The army was disintegrating. Soldiers deserted by the tens of thousands, walking home from the front with their rifles. In the cities, food shortages and war-weariness fueled strikes and protests.

For a British intelligence officer, Russia in 1917 was both an opportunity and a nightmare. The opportunity: a vast country full of people willing to share information, in a state of such chaos that normal security barely functioned. The nightmare: nothing was stable, alliances shifted daily, and the entire political landscape could transform overnight.

It did.


## Chapter 2 — Moscow, 1917

On the evening of October 25, 1917 (November 7 by the Western calendar — Russia still used the Julian calendar), armed workers and soldiers loyal to the Bolshevik Party seized key buildings in Petrograd. The Provisional Government collapsed with remarkably little resistance. Within days, Vladimir Lenin and the Bolsheviks controlled Petrograd and were extending their grip across the country.

Moscow fell to the Bolsheviks after a week of fighting that was significantly bloodier than the Petrograd takeover. Artillery was used. Buildings were shelled. The Kremlin changed hands. By early November, Bolshevik authority was established in both capitals, though much of the country remained in flux.

![Revolutionary Moscow — soldiers and crowds in the streets](../images/placeholders/03-revolutionary-moscow.png)

For Hill and the small community of British officials in Russia, the Bolshevik seizure of power created an immediate crisis. The Bolsheviks had promised peace. Their first major act was the Decree on Peace, calling for an immediate end to the war on all fronts. Negotiations with Germany began at Brest-Litovsk in December 1917. By March 1918, Russia had signed a separate peace, surrendering enormous territories — Finland, the Baltic states, Poland, Ukraine, and parts of Belarus — in exchange for an exit from the war.

From Britain's perspective, this was a catastrophe. The Eastern Front was gone. Germany was already transferring divisions westward. Britain's strategic calculation shifted overnight: Russia had gone from essential ally to potential enemy territory. The question for British intelligence was no longer "how do we keep Russia fighting?" but "what can we salvage?"

Hill remained in Moscow through the revolution and its aftermath. His Russian language skills and local knowledge made him too valuable to withdraw. He operated initially under military cover — officially part of the British military mission — but his real work was intelligence gathering and, increasingly, building networks of agents and contacts who could provide information about the Bolshevik government, the German advance, and the shifting military situation.

Moscow in early 1918 was a city transformed. The Bolsheviks had moved the capital from Petrograd to Moscow in March, partly to distance the government from the Finnish and German threat to Petrograd. The Kremlin became the seat of Soviet power. The city filled with party officials, soldiers, petitioners, refugees, and foreign nationals trying to figure out what had happened and what to do next.

Hill operated in this chaotic environment with considerable freedom. The Bolshevik security apparatus — the Cheka, established in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky — was still finding its feet. In the early months, surveillance was sporadic and enforcement uneven. A resourceful intelligence officer who spoke fluent Russian and could move through the city without attracting attention had room to work.

According to Hill's own accounts, he established contacts across Moscow during this period, building the foundation of what would become his agent network. He cultivated sources in government offices, military units, and transportation networks. He made contact with other British intelligence officers operating in Russia, including the man who would become his most famous colleague: Sidney Reilly.

The precise nature and extent of Hill's early network remains difficult to verify independently. His memoirs describe an elaborate operation with multiple agents and sub-networks. Keith Jeffery's authorized history of MI6, drawing on official records, confirms Hill's presence and intelligence role but offers a more modest picture of the early operation's scope. The truth likely lies somewhere between Hill's colorful self-portrait and the dry official record.

What is clear is that by the spring of 1918, Hill was fully embedded in Moscow as a British intelligence officer, with a network of contacts, a cover identity, and a mission that was about to become far more dangerous. The Cheka was growing rapidly. Foreign intelligence officers were becoming targets. And a group of British agents — Hill, Reilly, and the diplomat Robert Bruce Lockhart among them — were about to attempt something that would bring the full weight of Bolshevik counterintelligence down on their heads.
