# A Note to the Reader

In 1918, a British army officer ran agent networks across revolutionary Russia, dodging the Cheka, collaborating with Sidney Reilly, and claiming to have organized sabotage behind German lines in Ukraine. Twenty-three years later, the same man returned to Moscow — this time as the official liaison between Britain's Special Operations Executive and the Soviet NKVD. He spent four years working alongside the very intelligence apparatus that had once tried to arrest him.

His name was George Alexander Hill. His colleagues called him "Dousterswivel" after a character in Walter Scott. Others called him "Douster" or simply "Doust." His published memoirs, *Go Spy the Land* and *The Dreaded Hour*, are vivid, fast-paced, and wildly entertaining. They are also unreliable. Hill told stories about himself the way a river carves a canyon — following the path of least resistance, wearing the facts smooth, sometimes changing course entirely.

Scholars have spent decades sorting Hill's claims from verifiable events. Some of his stories check out. Others are demonstrably false. A substantial middle ground remains genuinely uncertain — plausible enough to resist dismissal, unsupported enough to resist confirmation.

This book follows what we can establish. Where Hill's accounts align with independent sources, we say so. Where they diverge, we show both versions. Where we cannot tell, we label the uncertainty and move on. The goal is the real George Hill — or as close as the evidence allows.
