# Part Two: Behind the Lines

## Chapter 3 — The Agent Runner

By the summer of 1918, George Hill was running one of several British intelligence networks in Bolshevik Russia. He was part of a loose constellation of officers and agents that included Sidney Reilly, operating under commercial cover in Moscow and Petrograd, and Robert Bruce Lockhart, who held a semi-official diplomatic role as Britain's unofficial representative to the Bolshevik government.

![Diagram of the British intelligence networks in Russia, 1918](../images/placeholders/04-agent-network-diagram.png)

The relationship between these men was both collaborative and competitive. They shared information when it suited them and guarded their own sources carefully. Reilly was the flamboyant risk-taker, Hill the more methodical operator, Lockhart the political figure who provided diplomatic cover and high-level access. Their individual accounts of these months differ on important details — who reported to whom, who initiated which operations, and who deserves credit for what intelligence.

Hill's network, as best scholars can reconstruct it, focused on military and transportation intelligence. He cultivated sources within the Red Army and the railway system, gathering information about troop movements, supply lines, and the Bolshevik government's military capabilities. This was practical intelligence with immediate value: Britain needed to understand the military situation in Russia both for its own strategic planning and to assess the viability of the various anti-Bolshevik forces that were beginning to organize across the former empire.

The mechanics of running agents in revolutionary Moscow were brutal in their simplicity. There was no sophisticated communications technology. Messages were carried by hand or hidden in personal effects. Meetings happened in apartments, on park benches, in the back rooms of shops. Payment was in cash or goods — the Bolshevik economy was collapsing, and basic commodities had more purchasing power than money. A bag of flour or a pair of boots could buy loyalty that rubles could not.

Hill described using a network of safe houses across Moscow, with couriers moving between them on foot or by tram. He claimed to have used disguises, forged documents, and multiple cover identities. Some of this is standard tradecraft that any intelligence officer in his position would have employed. Some of it reads like embellishment — the disguise scenes in his memoirs have a theatrical quality that makes historians cautious.

What is independently confirmed is that British intelligence did operate agent networks in Moscow and Petrograd during this period, that Hill was one of the officers involved, and that the networks produced intelligence that was transmitted back to London. The precise scale and productivity of Hill's specific network remains debated.

The summer of 1918 brought a decisive escalation. The so-called "Lockhart Plot" — or "Ambassadors' Plot," which forms the subject of Volume 16 in this series — drew Hill and his colleagues into a scheme that may have aimed at overthrowing the Bolshevik government. The details of who planned what, and how far the plot actually went, remain contested among historians. What is certain is that in early September 1918, the Cheka struck.

![The British Mission building in Moscow](../images/placeholders/05-british-mission-moscow.png)

On September 1, the Cheka raided the British Embassy in Petrograd. Captain Francis Cromie, the naval attache, was killed in the assault — shot on the embassy stairs. Lockhart was arrested in Moscow. Reilly went underground. The Bolsheviks announced the discovery of a vast Anglo-French conspiracy to overthrow the Soviet government and assassinate Lenin.

Hill escaped the immediate dragnet. His operational security — separate from Lockhart's more visible diplomatic operation — gave him some insulation. But Moscow had become extraordinarily dangerous. The Cheka was in a fury. The Red Terror, officially proclaimed on September 5, 1918, gave the security apparatus broad powers of arrest and execution. Foreign intelligence officers were priority targets.

Hill's account of the weeks following the Lockhart Plot describes a tense period of hiding, moving between safe houses, and maintaining contact with remaining agents while planning his escape from Russia. The details vary between his two memoirs, which is one reason scholars treat them cautiously. In *Go Spy the Land*, the escape is dramatic and tightly paced. In *The Dreaded Hour*, some details shift. Independent sources confirm that Hill did leave Russia, but the precise circumstances of his departure are difficult to pin down.


## Chapter 4 — The Saboteur

Hill's most controversial claims concern his operations behind German lines in Ukraine during 1918. According to his memoirs, he organized sabotage networks that disrupted German supply lines, destroyed ammunition dumps, and interfered with the German occupation of Ukraine — all while operating under deep cover in territory controlled by the Central Powers.

Ukraine in 1918 was a territory in flux. Under the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed in March, the Bolsheviks had ceded Ukraine to the Central Powers. German and Austro-Hungarian forces occupied the country, initially supporting the Ukrainian People's Republic and then, from April 1918, the more compliant Hetmanate government under Pavlo Skoropadskyi. The occupation was primarily about resources: Germany desperately needed Ukrainian grain, coal, and raw materials to sustain its war effort.

British strategic interest in disrupting the German occupation of Ukraine was real. Any sabotage that slowed the flow of Ukrainian resources to Germany served the Allied cause. Hill's claim to have organized such operations is therefore strategically plausible.

The problem is evidence. Hill's accounts of Ukrainian sabotage operations are vivid and specific — he describes recruiting networks, planning attacks on railway lines, and destroying supply depots. But independent corroboration for the specific operations he describes is thin. Keith Jeffery, writing the authorized history of MI6 with access to the archives, found some support for Hill's presence in Ukraine but considerably less for the scale of operations Hill claimed.

Robert Service, in *Spies and Commissars*, notes that several British intelligence officers made expansive claims about their operations in this period, and that the chaotic conditions made independent verification of almost any specific claim difficult. The archives of the period are incomplete. German records of sabotage incidents in Ukraine exist but matching them to specific British operations — let alone to Hill personally — is rarely possible.

What scholars can say is this: Hill was likely in or near Ukraine during some of this period. British intelligence did have an interest in disrupting German operations there. Some sabotage did occur. Whether Hill organized it on the scale he claimed, or whether he absorbed other people's operations into his own narrative, or whether some of the incidents he describes happened at all — these questions remain open.

This pattern — a core of verified fact surrounded by a penumbra of plausible but unconfirmed claims — runs through Hill's entire memoir account of his wartime service. He was there. He did intelligence work. He was brave and resourceful. The specifics of his operations, however, are wrapped in a storyteller's polish that makes the historian's job genuinely difficult.

![Map showing Hill's claimed escape route from Russia](../images/placeholders/06-hill-escape-route.png)

Hill left Russia by late 1918 or early 1919 — the precise date is itself uncertain. He returned to Britain carrying intelligence reports and, presumably, a set of experiences that he would spend the next decade and a half shaping into the narratives that became his published memoirs. The soldier's war was over. The spy's afterlife was about to begin.
