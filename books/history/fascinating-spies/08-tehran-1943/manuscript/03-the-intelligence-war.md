# Part Three: The Intelligence War

## Chapter 5 -- The Embassy Compound

Roosevelt's decision to stay at the Soviet embassy compound is one of the most consequential security choices in the history of summit diplomacy. The official justification was straightforward: the American legation was several blocks from the Soviet and British compounds, and traveling between them required a motorcade through Tehran's streets. If there was a genuine assassination threat, concentrating all three delegations in the adjacent Soviet and British compounds eliminated the most vulnerable point -- the daily transit route.

![The Soviet embassy compound in Tehran](../images/placeholders/01-soviet-embassy-tehran.png)

The Soviet embassy compound was a large, walled property in Tehran's diplomatic quarter. Its main building was spacious enough to host both the plenary sessions and Roosevelt's personal quarters. The British legation sat immediately adjacent, sharing a wall with the Soviet compound, and a covered corridor connected the two. Churchill was already based at the British legation. With Roosevelt moving to the Soviet compound, all three leaders and their delegations operated within a single, contiguous security perimeter.

![Diagram of the embassy compound layout and security arrangements](../images/placeholders/09-embassy-security-diagram.png)

The security rationale was genuine. Whether the German assassination threat was real or inflated, the practical risk of moving the American president through Tehran's streets multiple times daily was real. The city was under Allied military occupation, but it was still a working capital with a civilian population, and the security services could not control every rooftop, window, and alleyway along the route. Concentrating the principals in one defensible compound was sound security practice.

The intelligence implications were equally real. By accepting Soviet hospitality, Roosevelt placed himself and his delegation inside a building controlled by the NKVD. Soviet intelligence had every opportunity to install listening devices in the rooms allocated to the American delegation. The technology existed -- microphone surveillance was a mature capability by 1943 -- and the NKVD had both the expertise and the institutional culture to exploit such an opportunity without hesitation.

Sergo Beria, the son of NKVD chief Lavrenti Beria, claimed in his 2001 memoir that the Soviet rooms assigned to Roosevelt were thoroughly bugged and that his father received transcripts of Roosevelt's private conversations throughout the conference. This testimony comes from a source with obvious personal and political motivations, and it has been received with appropriate skepticism. But the claim is consistent with standard Soviet intelligence practice. The NKVD bugged its own citizens' apartments routinely. The notion that they would refrain from bugging the American president's quarters in their own embassy strains credulity.

If the rooms were bugged -- and the balance of probability suggests they were -- Stalin would have had access to Roosevelt's private discussions with his advisors, his strategic calculations, and his negotiating positions before each session. This would have given the Soviet delegation an extraordinary advantage in a conference where the key decisions involved the timing and location of military operations, the postwar treatment of Germany, and the future borders of Poland and Eastern Europe.

Roosevelt may have understood this risk. He was an experienced political operator who had dealt with domestic surveillance and intelligence matters throughout his presidency. Some historians have suggested that Roosevelt accepted the move to the Soviet compound knowing full well that his conversations would be overheard, and that he used this awareness strategically -- saying things in "private" that he wanted Stalin to hear. This interpretation credits Roosevelt with a sophisticated understanding of the intelligence environment, but the evidence for it is thin. The simpler explanation -- that Roosevelt prioritized the security benefits and the diplomatic symbolism of accepting Stalin's hospitality, without fully weighing the intelligence costs -- is at least equally plausible.

What is documented is that Roosevelt used his time at the Soviet compound to cultivate a personal relationship with Stalin. The two men met privately, without Churchill, on multiple occasions during the conference. Roosevelt spoke of his vision for postwar cooperation, the United Nations, and the role of the great powers. Whether Stalin's responses were shaped by intelligence gleaned from bugged conversations is a question the available record cannot answer definitively.


## Chapter 6 -- Cicero and Beyond

The intelligence war around the Tehran Conference extended far beyond the embassy compound. In Ankara, Turkey, 1,200 miles to the west, one of the most remarkable espionage operations of the war was underway at the same time -- and it bore directly on the conference's security.

![The Cicero affair -- Elyesa Bazna photographing documents in the British embassy](../images/placeholders/06-cicero-affair.png)

Elyesa Bazna was an Albanian-born valet employed by Sir Hughe Knatchbull-Hugessen, the British ambassador to Turkey. Beginning in October 1943 -- just weeks before the Tehran Conference -- Bazna used his access to the ambassador's residence to photograph classified documents from the ambassador's personal safe. He sold the photographs to Ludwig Moyzisch, an SD officer operating under diplomatic cover at the German embassy in Ankara. The Germans gave him the codename Cicero.

The documents Bazna photographed included telegrams relating to Allied conference planning, references to the upcoming summit, and material that could have compromised conference security. The scale of the breach was significant: Bazna photographed dozens of documents over a period of months, and the SD initially regarded his material as among the most valuable intelligence of the war.

The Cicero operation intersected with the Tehran Conference in two ways. First, some of the documents Bazna photographed contained references to Allied summit planning, potentially alerting German intelligence to the timing and location of the Big Three meeting. Second, the operation demonstrated the vulnerability of British diplomatic communications at a moment when those communications carried the most sensitive strategic information of the war.

The irony of the Cicero affair is that the Germans largely failed to exploit it. Ribbentrop, the German foreign minister, suspected the material might be a British deception operation and treated it with caution. Kaltenbrunner, who received the SD's reports, forwarded the intelligence up the chain, but the German leadership's response was muted. The photographs were genuine -- the British eventually discovered the leak, and Bazna fled -- but the organizational dysfunctions of the German intelligence apparatus prevented the material from being acted upon effectively.

The British discovered the Cicero breach through a combination of American signals intelligence and the work of Fritz Kolbe, a German Foreign Office official who was secretly passing information to the OSS, the American intelligence service. Kolbe's reports from Berlin included references to a highly placed source in the British embassy in Ankara. The trail eventually led to Bazna, though the damage had already been done.

Beyond the Cicero affair, the intelligence environment surrounding the Tehran Conference was dense with competing operations. The British had their own signals intelligence capabilities through Ultra -- the breaking of German Enigma communications -- which gave them insight into German military operations and, potentially, into German intelligence activities in Iran. The Americans were building their intelligence capabilities through the OSS, which was still a young organization in 1943 but had operatives in the region. Soviet intelligence, the most experienced and aggressive of the three Allied services, operated extensive networks throughout Iran, as described in Volume 19.

![Map of Tehran in 1943 showing the diplomatic quarter](../images/placeholders/03-tehran-1943-map.png)

The German intelligence presence in Iran had been substantially degraded by the time of the conference. The joint Anglo-Soviet invasion of 1941 had disrupted German networks, and the subsequent occupation had made it difficult for German agents to operate openly. The remaining German assets -- radio operators in tribal areas, agents among the Iranian civilian population, stay-behind networks from the pre-invasion period -- were under constant pressure from Allied counterintelligence. Adrian O'Sullivan's research suggests that by late 1943, German intelligence capabilities in Iran were limited but not eliminated.

The Tehran Conference thus took place at the intersection of multiple intelligence operations, some of which are well documented and others of which remain obscure. The conference's decisions were made by three leaders, each of whom was served by intelligence agencies that were simultaneously cooperating against a common enemy and spying on each other. This dual relationship -- alliance and espionage, trust and surveillance -- was the defining characteristic of the intelligence war in Tehran, and it foreshadowed the Cold War that would follow.
