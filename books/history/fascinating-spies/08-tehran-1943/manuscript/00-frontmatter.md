# A Note to the Reader

In November 1943, the three most powerful men on Earth met in secret in a city under foreign military occupation. The meeting's location was dictated by one man's refusal to fly. Its security was shaped by an assassination plot that may never have existed. Its decisions drew the map of the postwar world.

The Tehran Conference -- codenamed Eureka -- brought Franklin Roosevelt, Winston Churchill, and Josef Stalin together for the first time. They had been fighting a common enemy for two years, coordinating strategy by telegram and through emissaries, but they had never sat in the same room. Each leader arrived in Tehran with a different set of priorities, a different vision for the war's conclusion, and a different plan for the world that would follow. The four days they spent together, from November 28 to December 1, 1943, produced agreements that shaped the remainder of the war and the half-century of Cold War that followed.

The conference also generated one of the most durable mysteries of wartime intelligence: Operation Long Jump, the alleged German plot to assassinate all three leaders simultaneously. Soviet sources have long claimed that NKVD agents, including a young Armenian operative named Gevork Vartanian, uncovered and disrupted the plot. Western historians have questioned whether the operation ever progressed beyond the planning stage -- or whether it existed at all. The German archives contain no operational order for the mission. The Soviet files, to the extent they have been opened, present a narrative that serves Soviet intelligence interests. The truth sits somewhere in a space that the surviving evidence illuminates unevenly.

This volume tells the story of the conference, the plot, and the intelligence war that surrounded both. It follows Volume 19, *The Shadow War in Persia*, which traced the transformation of Iran from a declining empire into an occupied country. The Tehran Conference was a direct consequence of that occupation: the Allies met in Iran because they already controlled it. Volume 21, *The Coup*, will follow the thread forward to the Anglo-American operation that overthrew Iranian democracy in 1953. Where the documentary record supports firm conclusions, we draw them. Where it leaves gaps, ambiguities, or competing accounts, we identify them as clearly as we can.
