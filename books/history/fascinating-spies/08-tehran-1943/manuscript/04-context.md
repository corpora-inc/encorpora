# Part Four: Context

## Chapter 7 -- The Decisions

The Tehran Conference produced three categories of outcomes: firm military commitments, preliminary political agreements, and unresolved questions that were deliberately left for later.

The most consequential military commitment was Operation Overlord. Stalin had demanded a cross-Channel invasion since 1942, when the Soviet Union was fighting alone on the European continent and absorbing catastrophic losses. Churchill had resisted, arguing that the Allies lacked the forces and landing craft for a successful assault and that Mediterranean operations offered better returns at lower risk. Roosevelt supported Overlord in principle but had deferred to Churchill's strategic arguments at earlier conferences.

![The commitment to the cross-Channel invasion decided at Tehran](../images/placeholders/11-dday-decision.png)

At Tehran, the debate was settled. Roosevelt sided firmly with Stalin. The conference agreed that Overlord would be launched in May 1944, supported by a simultaneous invasion of southern France (Operation Anvil, later renamed Dragoon). In return, Stalin committed to a major Soviet offensive on the Eastern Front timed to coincide with the Western invasion, preventing Germany from shifting forces to meet the landings. This coordinated strategy -- simultaneous pressure from west and east -- was the military logic that would destroy the Wehrmacht in 1944.

The commitment was more than operational. It represented a strategic choice about the kind of war the Western Allies would fight and, implicitly, about the postwar balance of power. Churchill's Mediterranean strategy would have placed Western forces in southeastern Europe. Overlord committed them to northwestern France. The liberation of Western Europe from the west and Eastern Europe from the east -- the pattern that produced the Iron Curtain -- was a consequence of decisions made at Tehran.

The political discussions at Tehran were preliminary but revealing. The three leaders discussed the postwar treatment of Germany, with all three favoring some form of dismemberment, though they disagreed on the details. Roosevelt proposed dividing Germany into five autonomous states. Churchill suggested separating Prussia from the rest of Germany and creating a Danubian confederation. Stalin listened to both proposals without committing to either.

Poland dominated the political discussions. Stalin insisted that the Soviet-Polish border follow the Curzon Line, which would give the Soviet Union the territory it had annexed under the 1939 Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact. Poland would be compensated with German territory in the west. Churchill accepted this framework in principle, though he worried about the reaction of the Polish government-in-exile in London. Roosevelt, facing an election year and a large Polish-American constituency, avoided making a firm commitment but signaled privately to Stalin that he would not oppose the arrangement.

The Tehran discussions on Poland established the pattern that would be formalized at Yalta in February 1945 and imposed at Potsdam in July 1945: Poland would be shifted westward, losing its eastern territories to the Soviet Union and gaining German territory to compensate. The Polish government would have minimal say in this decision. Millions of people would be displaced. The seeds of postwar Poland's borders -- and its incorporation into the Soviet sphere -- were planted at Tehran.

The three leaders also discussed the creation of an international organization to maintain postwar peace. Roosevelt outlined his vision of the United Nations, with the great powers serving as permanent members of a security council with veto authority. Stalin was skeptical but willing to participate, provided the organization did not threaten Soviet sovereignty or give smaller nations the ability to constrain great-power action. The framework agreed upon at Tehran -- great-power unanimity, permanent membership with veto rights -- became the structure of the UN Security Council.

Stalin also confirmed that the Soviet Union would enter the war against Japan after Germany's defeat, a commitment of major strategic significance that would shape the final year of the Pacific War. The specific terms of Soviet entry -- territorial concessions in Manchuria, the Kuril Islands, and Sakhalin -- were left for later negotiation and would be settled at Yalta.

The Declaration of the Three Powers, issued on December 1, 1943, expressed the leaders' commitment to cooperation in war and peace. The language was deliberately vague on the political questions that divided them. The military commitments were firm. The political arrangements were sketched in outline and left to be filled in as the military situation evolved -- which meant, in practice, that they would be determined by which armies occupied which territory when the fighting stopped.


## Chapter 8 -- The Seeds

The Tehran Conference is sometimes overshadowed in popular memory by Yalta, where the same three leaders met again in February 1945 and where the postwar settlement is often said to have been decided. This framing underestimates Tehran. The fundamental strategic decisions -- the cross-Channel invasion, the Soviet offensive commitment, the acceptance of Soviet territorial claims in Eastern Europe -- were made at Tehran. Yalta elaborated and formalized what Tehran had established. Potsdam implemented it.

![The postwar division of Europe as foreshadowed by the Tehran discussions](../images/placeholders/12-postwar-settlement-map.png)

David Reynolds, in *Summits* (2007), argued that Tehran was the most important of the wartime summits precisely because it was the moment when the three leaders established the personal dynamics and strategic bargains that would govern the alliance for the remainder of the war. Roosevelt's decision to side with Stalin against Churchill on Overlord was a pivotal moment -- it signaled that the United States would work with the Soviet Union as an equal partner rather than deferring to British strategic preferences. Churchill understood the implication immediately. Britain's role as a great power was diminishing, and the postwar order would be shaped by Washington and Moscow.

The intelligence dimensions of the conference cast a long shadow as well. Roosevelt's stay at the Soviet embassy -- whether it resulted in intelligence collection or merely the opportunity for it -- established a precedent that shaped how the Western powers thought about Soviet espionage for decades. The reality that the wartime Allies were spying on each other even as they fought a common enemy would become one of the defining features of the Cold War transition. The cooperation at Tehran was genuine. So was the suspicion. Both coexisted in the same rooms.

Operation Long Jump, whatever its factual basis, became embedded in the mythology of Soviet intelligence. For decades, the story served as evidence that Soviet intelligence had protected the leaders of the free world from Nazi assassination -- a narrative that Moscow deployed for propaganda purposes throughout the Cold War. Gevork Vartanian, who received the Hero of the Soviet Union award in 1984, became a public figure in the Soviet intelligence world, a living emblem of a story that may have been true, partly true, or largely constructed.

For Iran, the Tehran Conference was an episode of supreme irony. The most important diplomatic meeting of the century took place in its capital, and Iran had no seat at the table. The three powers that occupied its territory used its city as a venue for decisions that would shape the postwar world -- decisions in which Iran's sovereignty, interests, and future were at best an afterthought. The Allied occupation would continue until 1946, as described in Volume 19. The consequences of the wartime relationship between the great powers and Iran -- the intelligence networks, the political manipulation, the assumption that Iran existed to serve external strategic interests -- would lead directly to the crisis described in Volume 21: the nationalization of oil, the rise of Mosaddegh, and the Anglo-American coup of 1953.

The four days at Tehran in November 1943 produced a military strategy that won the war and a political framework that organized the peace. The military strategy worked. The political framework produced the Cold War. The intelligence relationships forged and tested at Tehran -- alliance and espionage, cooperation and surveillance -- became the operating model for great-power relations for the next half-century. The secret meeting shaped the world, and the world is still living with the consequences.
