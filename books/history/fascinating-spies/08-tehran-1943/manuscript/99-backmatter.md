# What We Still Don't Know

**Did Operation Long Jump actually exist as a coordinated assassination plan?** Soviet sources describe an elaborate SS commando operation to kill Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin simultaneously. Western historians have found no operational order in the German archives. Skorzeny denied involvement. The question remains open: the German archives are incomplete, and the absence of documentation is suggestive but inconclusive. The Soviet testimony is detailed but comes from an institution with powerful reasons to promote the story. A limited German reconnaissance or intelligence-gathering mission in the Tehran area is plausible; a full-scale assassination operation remains unproven.

**Were Roosevelt's quarters at the Soviet embassy bugged?** Sergo Beria claimed his father received transcripts of Roosevelt's private conversations. The claim is consistent with standard NKVD practice and the technical capabilities of 1943 surveillance. Independent confirmation has not emerged from the Russian archives, which remain largely closed on this subject. The balance of probability suggests surveillance occurred, but the question of what Stalin actually learned -- and how it influenced his negotiating positions -- cannot be answered from the available evidence.

**Did Roosevelt know he was being monitored?** Some historians have suggested that Roosevelt accepted the move to the Soviet compound with full awareness of the surveillance risk and may have used it strategically. The evidence for this interpretation is circumstantial. Roosevelt's private papers and the testimony of his advisors do not clearly address the question.

**What was discussed in the private Roosevelt-Stalin meetings?** Roosevelt met with Stalin bilaterally, without Churchill, on several occasions during the conference. The records of these conversations are incomplete. Roosevelt's interpreters provided accounts, but these were reconstructed from memory rather than stenographic notes. What Roosevelt may have communicated to Stalin privately about Poland, the postwar order, and his own vision for the alliance is only partially documented.

**How much did the Cicero documents compromise the conference?** Bazna photographed documents that included references to Allied summit planning. The extent to which this intelligence reached German operational planners in time to affect decisions about Tehran -- including any potential connection to Operation Long Jump -- is unclear. The German failure to exploit the Cicero material fully suggests that the impact was limited, but the full chain of intelligence dissemination is not documented.


# Key Names and Terms

**Bazna, Elyesa** (1904-1970) -- Albanian-born valet to the British ambassador in Ankara. Codenamed "Cicero" by German intelligence, he photographed classified documents from the ambassador's safe and sold them to the SD from October 1943 to early 1944.

**Beria, Lavrenti** (1899-1953) -- Head of the NKVD (Soviet secret police and intelligence service) from 1938 to 1945. Responsible for Soviet intelligence operations during the Tehran Conference. Executed after Stalin's death.

**Beria, Sergo** (1924-2000) -- Son of Lavrenti Beria. Claimed in his 2001 memoir that Roosevelt's quarters at the Soviet embassy were bugged during the conference.

**Churchill, Winston** (1874-1965) -- British Prime Minister 1940-1945 and 1951-1955. Arrived at Tehran favoring a Mediterranean strategy; left having agreed to Operation Overlord.

**Cicero** -- German intelligence codename for Elyesa Bazna, the source in the British embassy in Ankara.

**Curzon Line** -- The proposed Soviet-Polish border, roughly following the ethnic boundary suggested by Lord Curzon in 1919. Stalin's insistence on this line at Tehran set the framework for postwar Poland's eastern border.

**Eubank, Keith** -- American historian; author of *Summit at Teheran* (1985), the standard English-language monograph on the conference.

**Eureka** -- Allied codename for the Tehran Conference.

**Hopkins, Harry** (1890-1946) -- Roosevelt's closest advisor and personal envoy. Accompanied Roosevelt to Tehran and participated in key meetings.

**Kaltenbrunner, Ernst** (1903-1946) -- Head of the Reich Main Security Office (RSHA), which oversaw the SD and the Gestapo. Named in Soviet accounts as one of the planners of Operation Long Jump. Executed at Nuremberg.

**Knatchbull-Hugessen, Sir Hughe** (1886-1971) -- British ambassador to Turkey 1939-1944. His valet, Bazna, photographed classified documents from his personal safe.

**Kolbe, Fritz** (1900-1971) -- German Foreign Office official who secretly passed intelligence to the American OSS. His reports helped expose the Cicero leak.

**Mayle, Paul D.** -- American historian; author of *Eureka Summit* (1987), a detailed diplomatic history of the Tehran Conference.

**Moyzisch, Ludwig** (1903-1982) -- SD officer at the German embassy in Ankara who served as the handler for Cicero.

**NKVD** -- Narodnyi Komissariat Vnutrennikh Del (People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs). The Soviet secret police and intelligence agency during the war period.

**Operation Anvil/Dragoon** -- The Allied invasion of southern France, agreed at Tehran as a companion operation to Overlord. Launched August 15, 1944.

**Operation Long Jump** (Unternehmen Weitsprung) -- The alleged German plan to assassinate Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin at the Tehran Conference. Its existence is claimed by Soviet sources but unverified by German archival evidence.

**Operation Overlord** -- The Allied cross-Channel invasion of Normandy, committed to at Tehran for May 1944 and executed on June 6, 1944 (D-Day).

**OSS** (Office of Strategic Services) -- The American wartime intelligence agency, predecessor to the CIA. Founded in 1942 under William Donovan.

**Reynolds, David** -- British historian; author of *Summits: Six Meetings That Shaped the Twentieth Century* (2007), which places Tehran in the broader history of summit diplomacy.

**Roosevelt, Franklin Delano** (1882-1945) -- President of the United States 1933-1945. Traveled to Tehran seeking Stalin's commitment to Overlord and a personal relationship that could sustain postwar cooperation.

**SD** (Sicherheitsdienst) -- The intelligence service of the SS, responsible for foreign intelligence and internal security.

**Skorzeny, Otto** (1908-1975) -- SS commando leader who rescued Mussolini in September 1943. Named in Soviet accounts as the intended leader of Operation Long Jump. Denied involvement.

**Stalin, Josef** (1878-1953) -- General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union and supreme wartime commander. His refusal to travel far from Soviet territory dictated the conference's location.

**Ultra** -- The Allied program for decrypting German Enigma communications. Provided strategic intelligence throughout the war, including potential insight into German operations in the Tehran area.

**Vartanian, Gevork** (1924-2012) -- Soviet intelligence operative of Armenian origin, credited by Soviet sources with uncovering Operation Long Jump. Received the Hero of the Soviet Union award in 1984.

**West, Nigel** -- Pseudonym of Rupert Allason, British intelligence historian; author of *Historical Dictionary of World War II Intelligence* (2008).

**Yenne, Bill** -- American author of *Operation Long Jump* (2015), a popular account of the alleged assassination plot.


# Further Reading

**Keith Eubank, *Summit at Teheran* (1985)** -- The most detailed English-language account of the conference itself. Eubank draws on American, British, and published Soviet sources to reconstruct the diplomatic negotiations day by day. Essential for understanding what was discussed and decided.

**Paul D. Mayle, *Eureka Summit: Agreement in Principle and the Big Three at Tehran, 1943* (1987)** -- Complements Eubank with a focus on the diplomatic dynamics between the three delegations. Particularly strong on the interplay between military strategy and political negotiation.

**David Reynolds, *Summits: Six Meetings That Shaped the Twentieth Century* (2007)** -- Places Tehran in the longer history of summit diplomacy, from Munich to Reykjavik. Reynolds's treatment is concise and analytical, excellent for understanding the conference's significance in the broader arc of twentieth-century diplomacy.

**Nigel West, *Historical Dictionary of World War II Intelligence* (2008)** -- An indispensable reference work. West's entries on Operation Long Jump, Cicero, and wartime intelligence operations in Iran provide a sober assessment of the evidence.

**Adrian O'Sullivan, *Nazi Secret Warfare in Occupied Persia* (2014)** -- The definitive study of German intelligence operations in wartime Iran. Essential context for evaluating the plausibility of Operation Long Jump.

**Laslo Havas, *Hitler's Plot to Kill the Big Three* (1967)** -- The first book-length account of Operation Long Jump. Should be read with caution: Havas relied heavily on Soviet-provided material, and the narrative reads more like a thriller than a critical history. Valuable as the foundation of the Long Jump story but unreliable as independent evidence.

**Bill Yenne, *Operation Long Jump* (2015)** -- A readable popular account that synthesizes the available material on the alleged assassination plot. Yenne takes the Soviet narrative more at face value than most Western historians but acknowledges the evidentiary gaps.

**Robert Beitzell, *The Uneasy Alliance: America, Britain, and Russia, 1941-1943* (1972)** -- Covers the diplomatic context leading up to Tehran, including the prolonged negotiations over the conference location and the tensions within the alliance.

**Warren Kimball, *Forged in War: Roosevelt, Churchill, and the Second World War* (1997)** -- The Roosevelt-Churchill relationship is essential background for understanding the dynamics at Tehran. Kimball traces how the balance of power within the Anglo-American partnership shifted as American industrial and military strength grew.

**Vojtech Mastny, *Russia's Road to the Cold War* (1979)** -- Traces Soviet foreign policy from the wartime alliance to the Cold War, with Tehran as a crucial turning point. Essential for understanding Stalin's objectives and methods.

**Ludwig Moyzisch, *Operation Cicero* (1950)** -- The German intelligence officer's own account of handling the Cicero source. Written from memory and with obvious self-justifying tendencies, but the primary source for the German perspective on the Ankara operation.


# About This Series

**Fascinating Curiosities of Human History** is a series of short, intense books about the most interesting things people have done, built, written, and wondered about. Each volume begins with a genuine mystery or curiosity and uses it as a doorway into a world of knowledge -- the people, the places, the context, and the open questions.

The series is designed for curious readers of all ages. Every sentence should be accessible to a bright ten-year-old and fascinating to an adult. We never talk down, we never pad, and we are always honest about what we know and what we do not.

Beginning with Volume 13, the series expands from archaeological mysteries to the hidden histories of espionage -- real spies, real operations, and their real consequences. The same editorial standards apply: parametric knowledge only, honest about gaps and uncertainties, competing scholarly accounts given fair treatment. Espionage history is built on the testimony of professional liars, which makes the truth harder to find and the search for it more fascinating.
