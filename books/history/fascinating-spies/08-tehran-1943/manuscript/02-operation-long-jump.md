# Part Two: Operation Long Jump

## Chapter 3 -- The Plot

The story of Operation Long Jump, as told by Soviet sources, runs as follows. In the autumn of 1943, German intelligence learned that Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin would meet in Tehran. The SS, under the direction of Ernst Kaltenbrunner and with the involvement of Otto Skorzeny -- the commando who had rescued Mussolini from his Italian captors in September 1943 -- developed a plan to assassinate all three Allied leaders simultaneously. The operation was supposedly codenamed Unternehmen Weitsprung -- Operation Long Jump.

![Conceptual illustration of Operation Long Jump](../images/placeholders/04-operation-long-jump.png)

According to the Soviet account, the plan involved infiltrating a team of German agents into Tehran. An advance party of six radio operators was parachuted into the Qashqai tribal region of southern Iran, an area where German intelligence had maintained contacts since the early years of the war. These agents were to make their way to Tehran, establish communications with Berlin, and prepare the ground for a larger team of SS commandos who would carry out the assassination itself. The commandos, led by Skorzeny or one of his subordinates, would exploit the close proximity of the Allied embassy compounds to launch a coordinated assault.

The Soviet narrative credits the NKVD's intelligence network in Tehran with detecting the advance party. Specifically, Soviet sources name Gevork Vartanian, a young Armenian who was part of a network of local agents recruited by Soviet intelligence in Iran. Vartanian, according to his own later accounts, was nineteen years old at the time and had been working for Soviet intelligence since his mid-teens. He and his team of young operatives, known informally as the "Light Cavalry," conducted surveillance operations in Tehran and identified the German radio operators through their transmissions. Soviet counterintelligence intercepted the advance party, which reportedly led Berlin to cancel the main assault team's deployment.

The story gained its first wide circulation through Laslo Havas's 1967 book *Hitler's Plot to Kill the Big Three*, which drew heavily on Soviet-provided material. Over the following decades, the narrative was expanded through Soviet media, including a 1973 film and subsequent books and documentaries. Vartanian himself gave interviews after the Soviet Union collapsed, adding detail to the account and receiving the Hero of the Soviet Union award in 1984 for his role. The Russian government continued to promote the story as a celebrated episode of Soviet intelligence success.

The operational concept, as described, had a certain plausibility. Skorzeny had demonstrated at Gran Sasso that a daring commando raid could succeed against defended targets. Tehran was a city with an active German intelligence presence, as documented by Adrian O'Sullivan in *Nazi Secret Warfare in Occupied Persia* (2014). The Qashqai tribal regions of southern Iran had provided Germany with local allies during both world wars. And the close proximity of the three embassy compounds -- the Soviet, British, and American -- meant that a coordinated attack could theoretically strike all three delegations within a confined area.

The question is whether the plot, as described, actually existed.


## Chapter 4 -- The Evidence

The evidence for Operation Long Jump falls into three categories, and each has significant problems.

The first category is Soviet testimony. The NKVD and its successor agencies provided the foundational narrative. Vartanian's personal account is the most detailed individual testimony. The difficulty is institutional: Soviet intelligence had powerful incentives to promote a story that demonstrated its competence, justified its presence in Iran, and enhanced its prestige relative to British and American services. The NKVD was an organization that fabricated evidence professionally. This does not mean the story is false. It means the testimony of the agency that tells it requires independent corroboration.

![Gevork Vartanian as a young intelligence operative in wartime Tehran](../images/placeholders/05-vartanian-portrait.png)

The second category is German archival evidence. Western historians, beginning with serious examination of the claim in the 1980s and 1990s, searched the captured German records for corroboration. The results were conspicuously thin. No operational order for Unternehmen Weitsprung has been found in the German archives. Skorzeny himself denied involvement in any assassination plot against the Big Three in his postwar writings and interrogations, though Skorzeny's reliability as a source is itself questionable -- he had his own reasons for distancing himself from politically toxic operations after the war. The SS records from this period are incomplete, and it is possible that an operation at the planning stage would have generated fewer documents than a fully executed mission.

Nigel West, in the *Historical Dictionary of World War II Intelligence* (2008), noted the absence of supporting documentation in German files and treated the plot as unverified. David Reynolds, in *Summits* (2007), addressed the story briefly and with skepticism. Other Western historians have ranged from cautious acceptance of a limited German reconnaissance effort to outright dismissal of the assassination narrative.

The third category is circumstantial evidence. Germany did have intelligence assets in Iran. The Abwehr (German military intelligence) and the SD (the SS intelligence service) had both operated networks in the country, as documented in Volume 19 of this series. German radio traffic from Iran was intercepted by multiple Allied services. The British Special Operations Executive and MI6 were aware of German agents in the tribal regions. Some historians have suggested that a reconnaissance or intelligence-gathering mission may have existed, and that Soviet intelligence -- or Soviet propaganda -- inflated it into an assassination plot.

Paul Mayle, in *Eureka Summit* (1987), examined the available evidence and concluded that while some German intelligence activity in the Tehran area during the conference period was likely, the elaborate assassination plot described by Soviet sources was probably exaggerated. Keith Eubank reached a similar conclusion. Bill Yenne's 2015 book *Operation Long Jump* presented a more credulous account but acknowledged the evidentiary gaps.

The most balanced assessment may be this: German intelligence almost certainly knew the conference was planned. Some form of German reconnaissance or intelligence-gathering activity in the Tehran area during this period is plausible. The leap from intelligence activity to a coordinated commando assassination operation led by Skorzeny is where the evidence fails. The Soviet narrative fills this gap with detail that reads like a thriller -- and that is part of the problem. The story is too good, too cinematic, and too perfectly suited to Soviet propaganda needs to accept on Soviet testimony alone.

The question matters because it shaped the conference itself. Whether real or exaggerated, the security threat was used to justify Roosevelt's move to the Soviet embassy compound, a decision with significant intelligence consequences. If the plot was genuine, Soviet intelligence performed a genuine service. If it was fabricated or inflated, the security scare may have been an instrument for getting the American president inside a Soviet-controlled building wired for surveillance. Both possibilities are consistent with the available evidence.

This is a case where honest history requires sitting with ambiguity. The plot is unproven. It is also unrefuted. The absence of German documentation is suggestive but not conclusive -- absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, particularly in the archives of a regime that was collapsing and burning documents by 1945. The presence of Soviet testimony is suggestive but not conclusive either -- the source has institutional credibility problems that cannot be wished away. What we can say with confidence is that the story of Operation Long Jump, whatever its factual basis, served Soviet strategic interests both during the war and for decades afterward.
