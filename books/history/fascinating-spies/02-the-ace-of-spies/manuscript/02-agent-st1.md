# Part Two: Agent ST1

## Chapter 3 -- The Secret Intelligence Service

The British Secret Intelligence Service in 1917 was a small organization stretched far beyond its capacity. Founded eight years earlier under Captain Mansfield Cumming -- who signed his correspondence with a single letter "C," a tradition that continues to this day -- SIS was responsible for all British intelligence gathering abroad. Its budget was modest. Its staff was tiny. The war had multiplied the demand for intelligence by orders of magnitude, and the institution had responded by recruiting anyone who seemed useful, regardless of background or respectability.

Reilly was designated Agent ST1 -- the first agent registered by SIS's station in New York, where he had been operating since 1914. The "ST" prefix denoted the station; the "1" denoted his position in its registry. This bureaucratic detail would later be inflated into myth -- "the first and greatest spy" -- but the designation was administrative, not honorific.

![Revolutionary Moscow in 1918](../images/placeholders/04-revolutionary-moscow.png)

In early 1918, SIS sent Reilly to Russia. The Bolshevik Revolution of October 1917 had overthrown the Provisional Government and brought Lenin's party to power. The new government was negotiating with Germany at Brest-Litovsk, and the terms being discussed would take Russia out of the war entirely, freeing dozens of German divisions to transfer to the Western Front. For Britain and France, this was a strategic catastrophe. The Allied governments were desperate to find any means of keeping Russia in the war, or failing that, of preventing the Germans from exploiting their eastern victory.

Moscow in the spring of 1918 was a city in revolutionary transformation. The old imperial institutions had collapsed. The new Soviet structures were still forming. Food was scarce. Armed bands roamed the streets. The Cheka -- the Bolshevik secret police, forerunner of the KGB -- was beginning its campaign of terror against real and imagined enemies of the revolution. Foreign nationals moved through this chaos with varying degrees of cover and protection.

Into this world came three men whose operations would become entangled in ways that historians are still sorting out: Sidney Reilly, George Hill, and Bruce Lockhart.

Robert Bruce Lockhart was the unofficial British representative to the Bolshevik government -- not an ambassador (Britain had not recognized the new regime) but a diplomatic agent sent to maintain contact and assess the situation. George Hill was a military intelligence officer running sabotage operations. Reilly was the SIS agent. Their mandates overlapped. Their methods differed. Their later accounts of what happened would contradict each other on important points.

Lockhart, in his 1932 memoir *Memoirs of a British Agent*, portrayed himself as a diplomat drawn reluctantly into conspiracy. Hill, in his own memoir *Go Spy the Land* (1932), emphasized his sabotage work. Reilly left no memoir -- he was dead by 1925 -- and his version of events comes to us filtered through others, principally Robin Bruce Lockhart (Bruce Lockhart's son), who published *Reilly: Ace of Spies* in 1967.


## Chapter 4 -- The Plot

The plan that became known as the "Lockhart Plot" or the "Ambassadors' Plot" was, depending on which account you credit, either a serious attempt to overthrow the Bolshevik government or a chaotic improvisation that never had a realistic chance of success.

The core idea, as reconstructed from multiple sources: Reilly had made contact with Latvian soldiers serving in the Kremlin guard. The Latvian Rifles were an elite unit that the Bolsheviks relied on for security. If these soldiers could be turned, they could seize key government buildings, arrest the Bolshevik leadership -- Lenin, Trotsky, and others -- and create conditions for a counter-revolutionary government to take power.

![The Lockhart Plot network showing key conspirators](../images/placeholders/05-lockhart-plot-network.png)

Reilly's own ambitions may have gone further. Some accounts suggest he envisioned himself as the power behind a new government. Others describe a more limited objective: creating enough chaos to force the Bolsheviks to reverse course on the German peace and resume fighting on the Eastern Front. The scope of Reilly's personal vision is unknowable because the only source for it is Reilly himself, filtered through intermediaries.

The plot unraveled in late August 1918. On August 30, Fanny Kaplan shot Lenin outside a factory in Moscow. On the same day, a military commissar named Moisei Uritsky was assassinated in Petrograd. The Bolsheviks responded with the Red Terror -- mass arrests, summary executions, and a systematic crackdown on all perceived enemies.

Whether the shooting of Lenin was connected to the Lockhart-Reilly conspiracy is debated. The Cheka arrested Lockhart. Hill escaped. Reilly fled Moscow, crossing into the British-controlled zone through a series of border crossings that later accounts embellished into legend. The Latvian soldiers who had been Reilly's key contacts turned out to include agents provocateurs planted by the Cheka. The conspiracy had been at least partially penetrated from the beginning.

The aftermath was severe. The Bolshevik government used the plot to justify expanded repression. Lockhart was expelled. Several Russian agents who had worked with Reilly were executed. The episode convinced the Soviet security services that Western intelligence agencies were actively working to destroy the revolution -- a conviction that shaped Soviet counterintelligence for decades.

For Reilly, the failure was personal as well as operational. He had staked everything on the plot's success. He had promised his SIS handlers results. He had promised the anti-Bolshevik Russians who worked with him that Britain would support them. He delivered nothing. His reputation within SIS, whatever it had been before, was now complicated. He was brave, certainly. He was resourceful. But was he effective? The record after 1918 suggests that SIS was increasingly uncertain.

![The Lubyanka -- Cheka and later OGPU headquarters in Moscow](../images/placeholders/06-lubyanka.png)

Reilly spent the years between 1918 and 1925 in a kind of restless exile. He lived in London, Paris, and New York. He involved himself in various anti-Bolshevik schemes and organizations, most of which accomplished nothing. He continued to represent himself to SIS as an indispensable agent with irreplaceable Russian contacts. He continued to spend money faster than he earned it. And he continued to believe that the Bolshevik regime was fragile, that it could be overthrown by the right combination of internal conspiracy and external pressure, and that he was the man to do it.

This combination of conviction, vanity, and desperation made him the perfect target for what came next.
