# Part One: The Man With No Past

## Chapter 1 -- The Ghost

Sidney Reilly told different people different stories about where he came from. He claimed to be the son of an Irish sea captain. He claimed Russian nobility. He told at least one associate he was born in Odessa. The truth, pieced together by biographers working from fragmentary Russian records, is that he was born Sigmund Georgievich Rosenblum, probably on March 24, 1873, in or near the town of Kherson in the southern Russian Empire. His mother was Paulina, and his father was almost certainly Grigory Rosenblum, a contractor. Some sources place the family in Odessa; the distinction matters less than the fact that Reilly himself worked to obscure it.

![Portrait of Sidney Reilly in multiple identities](../images/placeholders/01-reilly-portrait.png)

The Jewish communities of the Russian Empire's Pale of Settlement lived under legal restrictions that governed where they could reside, what professions they could enter, and where they could attend university. A young man of intelligence and ambition born into this world faced a choice: accept the constraints or escape them. Reilly chose escape, and he did so with a thoroughness that erased his origins almost completely.

The details of his early years are a fog of contradictions. Richard Spence, who made the most sustained attempt to reconstruct Reilly's pre-London life in *Trust No One* (2002), found fragments: possible involvement with revolutionary circles in Odessa, possible travel to South America, a possible period in the Far East. The word "possible" recurs because Reilly left almost no verifiable trace before his arrival in Britain. Andrew Cook, whose 2004 biography drew on newly declassified files, concluded that much of what Reilly later claimed about his youth was invention.

What can be established is that the young Rosenblum left the Russian Empire sometime in the 1890s. He may have traveled through Europe and South America. He surfaced in London by the turn of the century, and the transformation had begun. The Jewish boy from the Pale of Settlement was becoming an English gentleman -- or at least a convincing performance of one.

![Map showing Reilly's claimed and documented origins](../images/placeholders/02-reilly-identity-map.png)

This pattern of self-invention would define his entire career. Reilly understood something that most people in the early twentieth century did not: identity was a technology. In an era before centralized databases, biometric records, or international information-sharing, a man who was willing to forge documents, learn languages, and commit fully to a role could become anyone. Reilly became many people, and he did it with a fluency that astonished even the professionals who later employed him.


## Chapter 2 -- Becoming Reilly

The man who arrived in London in the late 1890s was no longer Sigmund Rosenblum. The precise sequence of identity changes is disputed, but the destination is clear: by 1899, he was using the name Sidney Reilly.

The name itself may have come from his first wife. In March 1898, Margaret Reilly Thomas -- the wife of the Reverend Hugh Thomas -- became entangled with the young man then calling himself Sigmund Rosenblum. Reverend Thomas died shortly afterward, on March 12, 1898, officially of natural causes. Andrew Cook's research raised questions about the circumstances of that death without being able to resolve them. Rosenblum married Margaret Thomas on August 22, 1898. He took her maiden name. Sidney Reilly was born.

The marriage gave him a British identity and, through Margaret's connections, access to a social world far above his origins. Reilly was charismatic, multilingual -- he spoke Russian, English, French, and German with varying degrees of fluency -- and possessed of an appetite for risk that could appear as either courage or recklessness depending on the outcome.

![SIS headquarters at Broadway Buildings](../images/placeholders/03-sis-broadway.png)

His early contacts with British intelligence are difficult to date. The Secret Intelligence Service -- SIS, later known as MI6 -- was formally established in 1909 under Mansfield Cumming. But informal intelligence networks predated the institution. Reilly may have performed services for British interests as early as 1901-1902, possibly in connection with arms dealing or industrial intelligence. The documentary evidence is thin. Cook found traces of early intelligence work; Spence found others, pointing in slightly different directions. Keith Jeffery's authorized history of MI6, published in 2010, places Reilly's formal recruitment later, in the context of the First World War.

What is clear is that by the time the war began in 1914, Reilly was known to British intelligence, had connections in Russia that SIS found valuable, and was willing to undertake dangerous work. He was also, by this point, a man of considerable means -- though the sources of his wealth were opaque. He dealt in arms, in patents, in pharmaceutical products. He speculated in currencies and commodities. He moved money across borders with a facility that suggested either genius or fraud, and possibly both.

Margaret Thomas Reilly was still legally his wife. She would remain so. But Reilly had already moved on to other women, other identities, other countries. The pattern that would persist throughout his life was established: simultaneous lives, maintained in parallel, each unknown to the others. He married Nadine Zalessky in 1916 while still married to Margaret. He would later marry Pepita Bobadilla in 1923. None of these marriages were dissolved before the next one began.

This was a man of extraordinary gifts operating without moral constraints. The British intelligence establishment found him useful precisely because he would do things that properly socialized officers would refuse. He was expendable in a way that gentlemen were not. And he was effective in ways that gentlemen could not be.
