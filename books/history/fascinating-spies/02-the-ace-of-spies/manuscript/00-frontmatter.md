# A Note to the Reader

The most famous spy of the early twentieth century was born under a name he abandoned, lived under at least seven identities, married multiple women in multiple countries without divorcing any of them, and died in a Soviet prison under circumstances that took decades to confirm. We are still uncertain of his real name.

Sidney Reilly -- or Sigmund Georgievich Rosenblum, or Shlomo Rosenblum, or Pedro, or any of several other names he used across a career that spanned four countries and two revolutions -- was designated Agent ST1 by the British Secret Intelligence Service. He was, in a sense, their first numbered agent. He plotted to overthrow the Bolshevik government in 1918, ran networks of informants across revolutionary Russia, conducted financial fraud on an impressive scale, and was eventually lured back into Soviet territory by one of the most elaborate deception operations in the history of intelligence: the Trust.

He was shot in a forest outside Moscow in November 1925. Or so the evidence now suggests. For decades, even the fact of his death was uncertain.

This book tells the story of what we can verify, labels what remains uncertain, and explains why the gaps matter. Espionage history presents a particular challenge: the primary sources are the testimony of professional liars. Reilly's own accounts of his early life were fabrications. The memoirs of his colleagues contradict each other at key points. The Soviet archives, partially opened after 1991, confirmed some claims and demolished others. The declassified British files, released in stages, revealed an institutional relationship more complicated than the legend suggested. Where we can distinguish between the documented record and the accumulated myth, we will. Where we cannot, we will say so.
