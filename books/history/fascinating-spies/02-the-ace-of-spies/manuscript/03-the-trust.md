# Part Three: The Trust

## Chapter 5 -- The Trap

The Trust -- formally the Monarchist Organization of Central Russia, or MOCR -- was one of the most successful deception operations in intelligence history. It was created by the OGPU, the Soviet secret police (successor to the Cheka), in 1921. Its purpose was to identify, monitor, and neutralize anti-Bolshevik opposition both inside the Soviet Union and among the Russian emigre communities abroad.

The method was elegant. The OGPU created a fictitious underground anti-Bolshevik organization and staffed it with its own agents and turned operatives. This fake organization then made contact with genuine anti-Bolshevik groups in the emigre community, presenting itself as a powerful internal resistance movement that was preparing for the overthrow of the Soviet regime. It invited external support. It welcomed foreign intelligence contacts. It gathered names, networks, plans, and methods from everyone who engaged with it.

![Diagram of the Trust deception operation](../images/placeholders/07-the-trust-diagram.png)

The Trust was run by Artur Artuzov, one of the most capable officers in the OGPU. Its principal front man was Alexander Yakushev, a former czarist official who played the role of a senior figure in the underground resistance. The operation was sophisticated: the Trust provided its contacts with just enough genuine intelligence to maintain credibility, while feeding disinformation that served Soviet strategic interests. It collected money from emigre organizations and foreign intelligence services. It identified agents and networks that could then be rolled up at a time of the OGPU's choosing.

SIS was aware of the Trust's existence. Whether it was aware that the Trust was a Soviet operation is a more complicated question. The official British assessment appears to have been cautious -- SIS officers had doubts, but the intelligence product coming through the Trust channel was good enough to maintain the relationship. The temptation was powerful: if the Trust was genuine, it represented the most important penetration of the Soviet system that any Western intelligence service possessed. The cost of dismissing it, if it was real, was enormous. This is the classic dilemma of counterintelligence, and the OGPU exploited it with precision.

Reilly encountered the Trust through emigre circles in Paris. By 1925, he had been out of active operations for years. His finances were precarious. His standing with SIS was diminished. The Trust offered what he most wanted to believe: that the Bolshevik regime was crumbling from within, that a powerful internal opposition existed, and that a man of his talents could play a decisive role in the coming counter-revolution.

The OGPU knew exactly who Reilly was. They knew his history, his psychology, his vanity, and his desperation. They tailored their approach accordingly. The Trust's representatives told Reilly that the underground needed his experience, his contacts, his operational genius. They told him that a brief visit to Soviet Russia would allow him to assess the situation personally and coordinate with the internal resistance. They offered what every intelligence officer fears and desires: the chance to be the man who changed history.


## Chapter 6 -- The Last Crossing

In September 1925, Sidney Reilly crossed the Finnish-Soviet border. He carried false papers. He was met by Trust operatives who were, in reality, OGPU agents. He entered the Soviet Union believing he was attending a meeting of the anti-Bolshevik underground. He was walking into a trap.

![The Finnish-Soviet border zone where Reilly crossed](../images/placeholders/08-finnish-border.png)

The details of what happened next were obscured for decades. The Soviet government initially said nothing. The emigre community heard rumors. SIS received fragmentary reports. Reilly's wife Pepita spent years trying to determine whether her husband was alive or dead.

The sequence, as reconstructed from Soviet archives partially opened after 1991 and from the research of historians including Andrew Cook and Boris Volodarsky: Reilly was arrested by the OGPU shortly after crossing the border, probably on September 28, 1925. He was taken to Moscow and held in the Lubyanka, the OGPU headquarters. He was interrogated over a period of weeks.

The interrogation produced information that the OGPU found valuable -- details about SIS methods, emigre networks, and the operational practices of Western intelligence services. How much Reilly revealed, and under what conditions, is documented in Soviet files that have been only partially released. Some accounts suggest he cooperated extensively; others suggest he resisted. The truth may be that he did both, at different stages, as the reality of his situation became clear.

On November 5, 1925, according to the most credible reconstruction, Reilly was taken from the Lubyanka to a location in the woods near Moscow. He was shot. The OGPU officer who carried out the execution was likely Ibrahim Nahumovich Eittingon, though some accounts name other officers. The body was buried in an unmarked location.

![Timeline of Reilly's identities from Rosenblum to his death](../images/placeholders/09-reilly-identities-timeline.png)

The Soviet government did not confirm Reilly's death for years. The ambiguity served their purposes: it kept the emigre community uncertain, and it protected the Trust, which continued to operate until 1926. The full story emerged only in stages -- through defectors, through partial archival releases, through the patient work of historians cross-referencing fragmentary sources across multiple archives in multiple countries.

Reilly was fifty-two years old. He had lived under at least seven documented identities. He had been married to at least three women simultaneously. He had plotted to overthrow a government, spied for the world's most important intelligence service, defrauded business partners across two continents, and been executed by the secret police of the country where he was born.

The Trust continued to operate for another year after Reilly's execution. It was exposed in 1927, partly through the defection of one of its operatives. The damage it had done to Western intelligence networks in Russia was substantial. The lesson it taught -- that the most dangerous deception operation is the one that tells you what you want to hear -- became a foundational principle of counterintelligence. It remains relevant.
