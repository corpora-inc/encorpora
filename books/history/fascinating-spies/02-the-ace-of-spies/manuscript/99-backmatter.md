# What We Still Don't Know

**What was Reilly's real name?** He was almost certainly born Sigmund Georgievich Rosenblum, but even this is reconstructed from fragmentary records. The place of birth -- Odessa, Kherson, or somewhere else in the southern Russian Empire -- is disputed. His date of birth is uncertain. The fundamental biographical facts that anchor most historical figures in the record are, for Reilly, contested.

**What did Reilly actually do before he arrived in London?** His early years are reconstructed from his own claims (unreliable), scattered archival fragments, and inference. Richard Spence's research found traces in multiple countries; Andrew Cook's found others. The two accounts do not fully align. A decade or more of Reilly's life -- roughly the 1890s -- remains a biographical void filled with speculation.

**How serious was the 1918 plot?** The "Lockhart Plot" or "Ambassadors' Plot" is described differently by every participant. Reilly's version (through intermediaries) suggests a serious, near-successful conspiracy. Lockhart's memoir minimizes his own involvement. The Cheka's account portrays the entire operation as penetrated from early on by its agents provocateurs. The true scope of the conspiracy -- how many people were involved, how close it came to execution, whether it had any realistic chance of success -- remains debated.

**Was Reilly betrayed by someone inside SIS?** Some accounts suggest that Reilly's final mission was set up with the knowledge, or even the encouragement, of elements within British intelligence who wanted him eliminated. The evidence for this is circumstantial and contested. The declassified files do not resolve the question.

**What did Reilly reveal under interrogation?** Soviet files indicate that Reilly provided information during his weeks in the Lubyanka. How much, and how willingly, is documented in archives that have been only partially released. The full record of his interrogation, if it survives, has not been made public.

**How exactly did Reilly die?** The most credible reconstruction places his execution on November 5, 1925, in a forest near Moscow. The identity of the executioner, the precise location, and the disposition of the body remain uncertain. The Soviet government did not officially confirm the death for years, and even after confirmation, the details were released in stages that left room for doubt.

**What was the full extent of the Trust's penetration of Western intelligence?** The Trust operated from 1921 to 1926. It had contacts with SIS, with emigre organizations across Europe, and possibly with other Western intelligence services. The full damage assessment was never made public. How much intelligence was compromised, how many agents were identified, and how many operations were disrupted by the Trust's activities is unknown.

These gaps are characteristic of intelligence history from this period. The organizations involved were secretive by design. The participants were trained to deceive. The archives were curated to protect institutional reputations. What survives is enough to tell a remarkable story, but the full truth is likely irrecoverable.


# Key Names and Terms

**Agent ST1** -- Reilly's designation within the Secret Intelligence Service. The "ST" prefix denoted the SIS station (in this case, likely New York or a related designation); "1" was his registration number within that station.

**Artuzov, Artur** (ar-TOO-zov) -- OGPU officer who directed the Trust deception operation. One of the most capable counterintelligence officers of the early Soviet period.

**Bolshevik** (BOHL-sheh-vik) -- A member of the radical socialist faction led by Vladimir Lenin that seized power in Russia in October 1917. From the Russian word for "majority."

**Cheka** (CHEH-kah) -- The All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage, the Soviet secret police organization established in December 1917. Predecessor of the OGPU, NKVD, and KGB.

**Cook, Andrew** -- British historian; author of *Ace of Spies: The True Story of Sidney Reilly* (2004); the revisionist biography that used declassified files to challenge the established legend.

**Cumming, Mansfield** -- The first chief of the Secret Intelligence Service (SIS/MI6), serving from its founding in 1909 until his death in 1923. Signed correspondence with a "C" in green ink, establishing a tradition that continues.

**Fleming, Ian** (1908-1964) -- British author and former Naval Intelligence officer; creator of James Bond. Knew people connected to Reilly and the early SIS world.

**Hill, George** -- British military intelligence officer who ran sabotage operations in revolutionary Russia. Author of *Go Spy the Land* (1932). Subject of Volume 15 of this series.

**Lockhart, Robert Bruce** (1887-1970) -- British diplomatic agent in revolutionary Moscow. Author of *Memoirs of a British Agent* (1932). Subject of Volume 16 of this series.

**Lockhart, Robin Bruce** (1920-1990) -- Son of Robert Bruce Lockhart; author of *Reilly: Ace of Spies* (1967), the biography that created the popular legend.

**Lubyanka** (loo-BYAHN-kah) -- A building in central Moscow that served as the headquarters of the Cheka, OGPU, NKVD, and KGB. Also contained an infamous prison where political prisoners were held and interrogated.

**OGPU** (oh-geh-peh-OO) -- The Joint State Political Directorate, the Soviet secret police organization from 1923 to 1934. Successor to the GPU and predecessor of the NKVD.

**Pale of Settlement** -- The western region of the Russian Empire within which Jewish subjects were generally required to reside. Abolished after the 1917 Revolution.

**Ransome, Arthur** (1884-1967) -- British journalist, later children's author (*Swallows and Amazons*), who was in Russia during the revolution and had complex connections to both SIS and the Bolshevik leadership. Subject of Volume 17 of this series.

**Red Terror** -- The campaign of mass political repression and executions carried out by the Bolshevik government beginning in September 1918, partly in response to the assassination attempt on Lenin and the Lockhart Plot.

**Reilly, Sidney** (c. 1873-1925) -- Born Sigmund Georgievich Rosenblum. British intelligence agent designated ST1 by SIS. Executed by the OGPU in November 1925.

**SIS** -- Secret Intelligence Service, the British foreign intelligence agency, also known as MI6. Founded in 1909 under Mansfield Cumming.

**Spence, Richard** -- American historian; author of *Trust No One: The Secret World of Sidney Reilly* (2002); the most detailed treatment of Reilly's early life and disputed origins.

**The Trust** -- Common name for the Monarchist Organization of Central Russia (MOCR), a fictitious anti-Bolshevik underground created and controlled by the OGPU from 1921 to 1926 as a deception operation to identify and neutralize opposition.

**Yakushev, Alexander** -- Former czarist official recruited by the OGPU to serve as the principal front man for the Trust. Presented himself to emigre organizations and foreign intelligence services as a leader of the anti-Bolshevik underground.


# Further Reading

**Andrew Cook, *Ace of Spies: The True Story of Sidney Reilly* (2004)** -- The essential revisionist biography. Cook drew on declassified British and Russian files to challenge the legend established by Robin Bruce Lockhart's earlier book. Thorough, skeptical, and well-documented. The best single book on Reilly.

**Robin Bruce Lockhart, *Reilly: Ace of Spies* (1967)** -- The biography that created the popular legend. Vivid and compelling as narrative, but subsequent research has shown that it accepted too much of the Reilly myth at face value. Essential for understanding how the legend was constructed.

**Richard Spence, *Trust No One: The Secret World of Sidney Reilly* (2002)** -- The most detailed examination of Reilly's early life and the murky period before his emergence in London. Spence is more willing than Cook to follow speculative trails, but his research uncovered material that no other biographer found.

**Keith Jeffery, *MI6: The History of the Secret Intelligence Service 1909-1949* (2010)** -- The authorized institutional history. Essential for understanding SIS as an organization and Reilly's place within it. Measured and bureaucratic in tone, which is itself informative.

**Christopher Andrew, *The Defence of the Realm: The Authorized History of MI5* (2009)** -- The domestic intelligence perspective. Useful for understanding the institutional landscape in which Reilly operated.

**Robert Service, *Spies and Commissars: Bolshevik Russia and the West* (2011)** -- The revolutionary context. Service places the intelligence operations of 1917-1918 within the broader story of the Bolshevik consolidation of power and the Western response.

**Robert Bruce Lockhart, *Memoirs of a British Agent* (1932)** -- Lockhart's own account of his time in revolutionary Moscow. A primary source, with all the limitations that implies. Carefully written to minimize Lockhart's own culpability.

**George Hill, *Go Spy the Land* (1932)** -- Hill's memoir of intelligence work in revolutionary Russia. More restrained than Lockhart's account and focused on operational detail.


# About This Series

**Fascinating Curiosities of Human History** is a series of short, intense books about the most interesting things people have done, built, written, and wondered about. Each volume begins with a genuine mystery or curiosity and uses it as a doorway into a world of knowledge -- the people, the places, the context, and the open questions.

The series is designed for curious readers of all ages. Every sentence should be accessible to a bright ten-year-old and fascinating to an adult. We never talk down, we never pad, and we are always honest about what we know and what we do not.

Beginning with Volume 13, the series expands from archaeological mysteries to the hidden histories of espionage -- real spies, real operations, and their real consequences. The same editorial standards apply: parametric knowledge only, honest about gaps and uncertainties, competing scholarly accounts given fair treatment. Espionage history is built on the testimony of professional liars, which makes the truth harder to find and the search for it more fascinating.
