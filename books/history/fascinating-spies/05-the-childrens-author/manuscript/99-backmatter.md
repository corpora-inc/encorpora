# What We Still Don't Know

**Was Ransome formally recruited by SIS?** The distinction between a formal agent and an informal source matters to intelligence historians but may have been blurred in practice. Ransome provided information to SIS officers. Whether he was given a designation, signed any agreement, or received payment for his reports has not been confirmed by the released files. The SIS archives for this period remain largely classified.

**What exactly did Ransome report to SIS?** The content of his intelligence reporting is known in fragments -- references in other files, summaries in institutional histories. The original reports, if they survive, have not been released. The quality, scope, and strategic value of his intelligence contribution cannot be fully assessed.

**Did the Bolsheviks know Ransome was reporting to British intelligence?** This is one of the most consequential unanswered questions. If they knew, then his access was controlled -- they were feeding him what they wanted London to hear. If they did not know, then his intelligence was more valuable but his personal risk was greater. The Soviet archives have not yielded a definitive answer.

**What was Evgenia Shelepina's role?** She was Trotsky's secretary, then Ransome's companion, then his wife. Did the Bolshevik leadership place her with Ransome, or did the relationship develop independently? Did she report on Ransome to Soviet intelligence? Did she facilitate his intelligence work for SIS? Her perspective is almost entirely absent from the historical record. She left no memoir, no interviews, no correspondence on this subject that has been made public.

**What happened to the diamonds?** Ransome carried Bolshevik diamonds to Stockholm. The ultimate disposition of these diamonds -- their value, their recipients, the purposes they funded -- is documented only in fragments. The full financial trail has not been reconstructed.

**Why did MI5 and SIS never reconcile their assessments?** One service used Ransome as a source; the other investigated him as a suspect. This institutional failure is documented but unexplained. Whether it reflected bureaucratic rivalry, genuine disagreement about the evidence, or simple lack of communication remains unclear.

**Did Ransome carry any psychological trace of the Russian years into his later life?** His autobiography omits or minimizes the intelligence dimension. His children's books contain no detectable echoes of the espionage. But the absence of evidence is evidence of nothing. What Ransome thought about his double life in his later decades -- whether it haunted him, whether he rationalized it, whether he simply set it aside -- is irrecoverable.


# Key Names and Terms

**Andrew, Christopher** -- British historian; author of *The Defence of the Realm: The Authorized History of MI5* (2009); the institutional perspective on Ransome's suspected Bolshevism.

**Bolshevik** (BOHL-sheh-vik) -- A member of the radical socialist faction led by Vladimir Lenin that seized power in Russia in October 1917. From the Russian word for "majority."

**Brogan, Hugh** (1936-2012) -- British historian; author of *The Life of Arthur Ransome* (1984); the first major biography, written before the intelligence files were released.

**Carnegie Medal** -- Britain's most prestigious award for children's literature, first awarded in 1936. Ransome's *Pigeon Post* won the inaugural prize.

**Chambers, Roland** -- British biographer; author of *The Last Englishman: The Double Life of Arthur Ransome* (2009); the fullest account of the intelligence connection, drawing on the 2005 MI5 file release.

**Cheka** (CHEH-kah) -- The All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage, the Soviet secret police organization established in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky. Predecessor of the OGPU, NKVD, and KGB.

**Daily News** -- London newspaper for which Ransome served as foreign correspondent in Russia during the revolutionary period. Later merged to become the *News Chronicle*.

**Dzerzhinsky, Felix** (dzer-ZHIN-skee) (1877-1926) -- Polish-born Bolshevik revolutionary; founder and head of the Cheka, the Soviet secret police. Known for asceticism and ruthlessness. Ransome played chess with him.

**Evgenia Shelepina** (yev-GEH-nyah sheh-LEH-pee-nah) -- Personal secretary to Leon Trotsky; became Arthur Ransome's companion and later his wife. Married Ransome in 1924. Died 1975.

**Hill, George** -- British military intelligence officer who ran sabotage operations in revolutionary Russia. Author of *Go Spy the Land* (1932). Subject of Volume 15 of this series.

**Jeffery, Keith** (1952-2016) -- Irish historian; author of *MI6: The History of the Secret Intelligence Service 1909-1949* (2010); the authorized institutional history.

**Lenin, Vladimir** (1870-1924) -- Leader of the Bolshevik Revolution and first head of the Soviet state. Ransome met and interviewed Lenin on multiple occasions.

**Lockhart, Robert Bruce** (1887-1970) -- British diplomatic agent in revolutionary Moscow. Author of *Memoirs of a British Agent* (1932). Subject of Volume 16 of this series.

**MI5** -- The Security Service, Britain's domestic counterintelligence agency. Maintained a file on Ransome as a suspected Bolshevik agent or sympathizer.

**Ransome, Arthur** (1884-1967) -- British journalist, intelligence asset, and children's author. Author of the *Swallows and Amazons* series (1930-1947). Subject of this volume.

**Reilly, Sidney** (c. 1873-1925) -- Born Sigmund Georgievich Rosenblum. British intelligence agent designated ST1 by SIS. Subject of Volume 14 of this series.

**Service, Robert** -- British historian; author of *Spies and Commissars: Bolshevik Russia and the West* (2011); the broader context of Western intelligence in revolutionary Russia.

**SIS** -- Secret Intelligence Service, the British foreign intelligence agency, also known as MI6. Used Ransome as a source of information on the Bolshevik government.

**Swallows and Amazons** -- Ransome's first and most famous children's novel, published in 1930. Set in the Lake District, it follows the Walker and Blackett children on sailing adventures. Launched a series of twelve novels.

**Trotsky, Leon** (1879-1940) -- Bolshevik revolutionary; Commissar for Foreign Affairs, then Commissar for War. Ransome had a working relationship with Trotsky and met him regularly during the revolutionary period. Evgenia Shelepina was Trotsky's personal secretary.


# Further Reading

**Roland Chambers, *The Last Englishman: The Double Life of Arthur Ransome* (2009)** -- The essential biography for understanding both sides of Ransome's life. Chambers drew on the newly released MI5 files and Ransome's personal papers to produce a nuanced, well-documented account. The best single book on Ransome.

**Hugh Brogan, *The Life of Arthur Ransome* (1984)** -- The first major biography, sympathetic and thorough, but written before the intelligence files were released. Essential for understanding Ransome's literary career and personal life; incomplete on the intelligence dimension.

**Keith Jeffery, *MI6: The History of the Secret Intelligence Service 1909-1949* (2010)** -- The authorized institutional history. Essential for understanding SIS as an organization and Ransome's place within the broader intelligence picture of the revolutionary period.

**Christopher Andrew, *The Defence of the Realm: The Authorized History of MI5* (2009)** -- The MI5 perspective. Useful for understanding why the domestic security service viewed Ransome with such suspicion, and for the broader context of British counterintelligence in the early twentieth century.

**Robert Service, *Spies and Commissars: Bolshevik Russia and the West* (2011)** -- The widest lens on the period. Service places Ransome within the broader story of Western engagement with revolutionary Russia, alongside the other intelligence figures of the era.

**Arthur Ransome, *The Autobiography of Arthur Ransome* (1976, posthumous)** -- Edited by Rupert Hart-Davis. Covers 1884-1932 with selective treatment of the intelligence dimension. A primary source, with all the limitations of a carefully curated self-portrait.

**Arthur Ransome, *Six Weeks in Russia in 1919* (1919)** -- Ransome's own account of his visit to revolutionary Russia. Sympathetic to the Bolsheviks, vivid in detail, and useful as a primary source for his access and his political sympathies.

**Arthur Ransome, *The Crisis in Russia* (1921)** -- Ransome's assessment of the Soviet state during the Civil War period. More measured than *Six Weeks*, but still fundamentally sympathetic.


# About This Series

**Fascinating Curiosities of Human History** is a series of short, intense books about the most interesting things people have done, built, written, and wondered about. Each volume begins with a genuine mystery or curiosity and uses it as a doorway into a world of knowledge -- the people, the places, the context, and the open questions.

The series is designed for curious readers of all ages. Every sentence should be accessible to a bright ten-year-old and fascinating to an adult. We never talk down, we never pad, and we are always honest about what we know and what we do not.

Beginning with Volume 13, the series expands from archaeological mysteries to the hidden histories of espionage -- real spies, real operations, and their real consequences. The same editorial standards apply: parametric knowledge only, honest about gaps and uncertainties, competing scholarly accounts given fair treatment. Espionage history is built on the testimony of professional liars, which makes the truth harder to find and the search for it more fascinating.
