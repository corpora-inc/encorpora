# Part Two: The Double Life

## Chapter 3 -- Between Two Services

The British intelligence presence in revolutionary Russia was fragmented and improvised. The Secret Intelligence Service -- SIS, later MI6 -- collected foreign intelligence. MI5 handled domestic security and counterintelligence. The War Office ran military intelligence. The Foreign Office had its own channels. These organizations did not always share information, did not always agree on priorities, and sometimes reached contradictory conclusions about the same individuals.

Arthur Ransome became a case study in this institutional confusion. SIS found him useful. He had access to senior Bolsheviks that no British intelligence officer could replicate. He reported on the internal dynamics of the Soviet government, on policy debates, on military dispositions. The information he provided was detailed and, by the accounts that have been declassified, largely accurate. From the SIS perspective, Ransome was an asset -- perhaps an informal one, perhaps a conscious collaborator, but undeniably a source of valuable intelligence.

MI5 reached a different conclusion. Their files, released in 2005, show that the domestic security service viewed Ransome with deep suspicion. He was living with a Bolshevik official's secretary. He was writing propaganda favorable to the Soviet government. He moved freely in Bolshevik circles at a time when the British government regarded the Bolsheviks as enemies. MI5 considered the possibility that Ransome was a Bolshevik agent, consciously or unconsciously serving Soviet interests while providing just enough intelligence to SIS to maintain his cover.

![Evgenia Shelepina, Trotsky's secretary and Ransome's future wife](../images/placeholders/05-evgenia-shelepina.png)

The diamond courier episode crystallized the ambiguity. In 1919, Ransome carried a quantity of Bolshevik diamonds from Moscow to Stockholm. The diamonds were intended to fund Bolshevik operations in Scandinavia and western Europe. Ransome's role as courier is documented. His motivation is disputed. Roland Chambers, in *The Last Englishman*, argues that Ransome performed the service partly to maintain his access and partly because Evgenia's position depended on his continued usefulness to the Bolshevik leadership. Others have suggested that Ransome was simply sympathetic to the cause. The SIS files, insofar as they have been released, do not indicate that British intelligence authorized the diamond run.

This is the central puzzle of Ransome's Russian years. He was providing intelligence to one British service while the other suspected him of working for the enemy. He was performing services for the Bolsheviks -- including courier work -- while simultaneously reporting on their activities to London. He maintained personal relationships with senior Soviet officials while corresponding with British intelligence officers.

![MI5 surveillance file on Arthur Ransome](../images/placeholders/06-mi5-surveillance-file.png)

Evgenia Shelepina's role in this arrangement is the least documented and potentially the most important. She was Trotsky's secretary, which meant she handled sensitive communications and had access to information that any intelligence service would covet. When she attached herself to Ransome, did the Bolshevik leadership view this as a useful channel of influence over a sympathetic British journalist? Did she report on Ransome to her superiors? The archives do not say. Evgenia herself left no memoir and gave no interviews about this period. Her agency in the story is almost entirely invisible.

![Map of the diamond courier route from Moscow to Stockholm](../images/placeholders/07-diamond-courier-route.png)

What is clear is that by 1920, Ransome's position in Russia was becoming untenable. The Civil War was raging. Conditions in Moscow and Petrograd were desperate. His relationship with Evgenia was deepening, and extracting her from Soviet Russia would require delicate maneuvering. His usefulness to both sides was diminishing as the revolutionary situation stabilized into the hard structures of the Soviet state.


## Chapter 4 -- The Chess Player

Felix Dzerzhinsky was the founder and head of the Cheka, the Soviet secret police. He was a Polish revolutionary of ascetic habits and absolute conviction -- a man who signed death warrants by the hundreds and was known among the Bolshevik leadership for his incorruptibility and his fanaticism. He was also, by the accounts of those who knew him, a compelling conversationalist and an avid chess player.

Arthur Ransome played chess with Dzerzhinsky. This fact, confirmed in Ransome's own writings and corroborated by other sources, is one of the most striking details in a biography full of striking details. The author of children's sailing stories sat across a chessboard from the man responsible for the Red Terror. They talked. The nature of their conversations is not recorded in detail, but the relationship -- however casual -- speaks to the depth of Ransome's immersion in the Bolshevik world.

![Ransome and Dzerzhinsky at chess](../images/placeholders/11-ransome-dzerzhinsky-chess.png)

The MI5 files, when they were finally released, confirmed what scholars had long suspected: British counterintelligence maintained an active interest in Ransome throughout the early 1920s. Surveillance reports tracked his movements. His mail was intercepted. His contacts were noted. The MI5 assessment was that Ransome was, at minimum, dangerously compromised by his personal attachments and political sympathies, and at maximum, a conscious agent of the Bolshevik government.

SIS, meanwhile, continued to find him useful. The two services did not reconcile their assessments. This failure of institutional coordination -- one arm of British intelligence using a man as a source while the other arm investigated him as a suspect -- was characteristic of the period. The British intelligence community in the early 1920s was small, under-resourced, and poorly coordinated. Ransome fell through the gap between the services.

![Map of Ransome's Baltic movements 1919-1924](../images/placeholders/10-baltic-movements-map.png)

The question of whether Ransome was a double agent -- in the technical sense of an agent controlled by one service while pretending to work for another -- is probably the wrong question. The categories of the Cold War do not map cleanly onto the fluid, improvised world of revolutionary-era intelligence. Ransome was a journalist who talked to everyone, sympathized with the revolution, loved a Bolshevik official, provided information to his own government, and performed services for the Bolsheviks. He was all of these things simultaneously, and the attempt to force him into a single category distorts the reality.

Roland Chambers reached a measured conclusion in *The Last Englishman*: Ransome was an enthusiast who got in over his head, maintained his position by being useful to multiple parties, and was neither the committed spy that MI5 feared nor the innocent journalist that Ransome himself later claimed to have been. The truth was messier than any of the clean narratives.

By 1924, Ransome had managed to extract Evgenia from Soviet Russia. They traveled through the Baltic states -- Estonia, Latvia -- making their way westward. Behind them was a decade of revolution, civil war, espionage, and entanglement. Ahead of them was England, and a second life so different from the first that it would seem to belong to a different person entirely.
