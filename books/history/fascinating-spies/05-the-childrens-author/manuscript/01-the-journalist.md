# Part One: The Journalist

## Chapter 1 -- Russia Before the Storm

Arthur Michell Ransome was born on January 18, 1884, in Leeds, the eldest son of Cyril Ransome, a professor of history at Yorkshire College. His childhood was comfortable, bookish, and unremarkable. He attended Rugby School, where he was unhappy. He failed to get into university. He drifted to London in 1902 with the intention of becoming a writer.

![Dual portrait of Ransome as journalist and children's author](../images/placeholders/01-ransome-dual-portrait.png)

His early literary career was modest. He worked as an office boy at a publishing house, wrote essays and criticism, and published a study of Edgar Allan Poe and a book on Bohemia in London. He married Ivy Walker in 1909. The marriage was unhappy almost from the start. A daughter, Tabitha, was born in 1910.

By 1913, Ransome needed to escape. His marriage was failing, his literary career had stalled after a disastrous libel suit brought by Lord Alfred Douglas over Ransome's book about Oscar Wilde, and London had become suffocating. He chose Russia. He told people he was going to study folklore. He had been learning Russian and was fascinated by Russian fairy tales, which he would later collect and retell in *Old Peter's Russian Tales* (1916).

He arrived in St. Petersburg in the spring of 1913. Russia was still the empire of Nicholas II -- autocratic, industrializing unevenly, culturally vibrant, politically volatile. Ransome found a world that captivated him. He learned the language with growing fluency. He moved among writers, journalists, and intellectuals. He traveled. He began to understand Russia in a way that few British observers of his generation managed, because he lived there rather than visiting.

![Map of Russia showing Ransome's movements 1913-1924](../images/placeholders/02-ransome-russia-map.png)

When the war began in August 1914, Ransome was in Russia and stayed. He began reporting for the *Daily News*, sending dispatches that were valued for their ground-level perspective. He was thirty years old, spoke passable Russian, had no institutional affiliations, and possessed a journalist's instinct for being where things were happening. These qualities would make him useful to people on multiple sides of the conflicts that were about to tear Russia apart.

The February Revolution of 1917 ended the Romanov dynasty. Ransome was there. The Provisional Government of Alexander Kerensky took power, and Ransome reported on the chaotic months that followed -- the political maneuvering, the continuing war, the growing Bolshevik movement. He met many of the key figures. His journalism was sympathetic to the revolutionary impulse, which reflected his genuine convictions but would later complicate his position with the British government.


## Chapter 2 -- The Revolution

On October 25, 1917, the Bolsheviks seized power. Ransome witnessed it. His dispatches to the *Daily News* described the revolution with an immediacy that few foreign correspondents could match, because he had access that few of them had cultivated. He knew the Bolshevik leaders. He spoke their language. He sympathized with their stated goals, even when he was troubled by their methods.

![The Daily News offices where Ransome filed his dispatches](../images/placeholders/03-daily-news-offices.png)

In the months that followed, Ransome became something more than a journalist. He met Lenin. He developed a working relationship with Trotsky, who was then Commissar for Foreign Affairs and later Commissar for War. He spent time at the Commissariat, had conversations with senior Bolsheviks, and gained a level of access to the new regime that was extraordinary for a foreign reporter.

It was through Trotsky's office that he met Evgenia Petrovna Shelepina. She was Trotsky's personal secretary -- young, intelligent, and positioned at the center of the Bolshevik government's communications with the outside world. Ransome fell in love with her. This personal attachment to a woman embedded in the Bolshevik apparatus would become one of the defining complications of his life.

![Ransome among the Bolsheviks in Moscow](../images/placeholders/04-ransome-bolsheviks.png)

Ransome's reporting from this period was sympathetic to the Bolsheviks in ways that generated alarm in London. He wrote a pamphlet, *The Truth About Russia*, published in 1918, that presented the Bolshevik case in terms favorable enough to function as propaganda. He followed it with *Six Weeks in Russia in 1919*, based on a visit during which he again met Lenin and other leaders. His accounts portrayed the revolution as a genuine popular movement rather than the criminal conspiracy that much of the British press described.

The British government read his dispatches with interest and suspicion in roughly equal measure. Ransome was providing detailed, firsthand information about the internal workings of the Bolshevik government -- information that was valuable regardless of his political sympathies. He was also, in the view of MI5, a potential Bolshevik sympathizer or agent who was using his position as a journalist to advance Soviet interests.

Both assessments contained truth. Ransome genuinely admired aspects of the revolution. He also genuinely provided information to British officials. The question of whether he was a journalist who talked to intelligence officers, an intelligence asset who used journalism as cover, or something that fit neither category cleanly -- this question has never been fully resolved.

His position in revolutionary Moscow placed him in the same circles as the other British figures who appear in this series. Sidney Reilly was running agents. Bruce Lockhart was conducting quasi-diplomatic negotiations. George Hill was organizing sabotage. Ransome was reporting, sympathizing, informing, and falling in love. The categories overlapped in ways that would take decades to untangle.
