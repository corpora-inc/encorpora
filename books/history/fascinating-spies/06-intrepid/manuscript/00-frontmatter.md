# A Note to the Reader

In the summer of 1940, a Canadian businessman nobody had heard of set up an office in Rockefeller Center and began running the largest covert intelligence operation ever conducted on American soil. His mission: to bring the United States into World War II. His methods included propaganda, espionage, sabotage, wiretapping, mail interception, political manipulation, and the systematic undermining of the American isolationist movement. Every one of these activities was illegal under the laws of the country where he was conducting them.

His name was William Samuel Stephenson. He had been born in Winnipeg, made a fortune in steel and radio, and caught the attention of Winston Churchill through a private intelligence network that predated the war. In June 1940, with France falling and Britain standing alone, Churchill sent Stephenson to New York to coordinate all British secret intelligence, propaganda, and covert operations across the Western Hemisphere. The organization he built -- British Security Coordination, or BSC -- would grow to nearly a thousand employees and operate from a suite of offices that most people in the building never knew existed.

Stephenson's story became famous in 1976, when a book called *A Man Called Intrepid* turned him into a global celebrity. The problem is that the book mixed real history with fabrication, and decades of subsequent scholarship have been required to sort the truth from the legend. Timothy Naftali's devastating 1993 article "Intrepid's Last Deception" showed that several of the book's most dramatic claims were invented. Thomas Troy, a CIA historian, spent years reconstructing what actually happened between Stephenson and William "Wild Bill" Donovan, the man who founded America's first central intelligence agency. The real story, stripped of embellishment, is remarkable enough.

This book tells what the evidence supports, labels what remains uncertain, and explains why a Canadian businessman running an illegal intelligence operation from midtown Manhattan in 1940-1945 matters for understanding how the modern intelligence world was built.
