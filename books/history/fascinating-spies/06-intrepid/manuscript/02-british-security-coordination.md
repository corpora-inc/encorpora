# Part Two: British Security Coordination

## Chapter 3 -- Room 3603

The International Building at 630 Fifth Avenue, in the heart of Rockefeller Center, was one of the most prestigious business addresses in New York. The BSC offices occupied portions of the 35th and 36th floors. The suite number -- Room 3603 -- became shorthand for the entire operation. H. Montgomery Hyde later used it as the title of his second, expanded account of BSC published in 1963.

![Rockefeller Center exterior in the 1940s](../images/placeholders/03-rockefeller-center.png)

Behind the passport-control cover, BSC grew into something unprecedented in the history of intelligence. At its peak, the organization had sections devoted to intelligence gathering, counter-espionage, communications security, propaganda and political warfare, special operations (sabotage and subversion), and training. Each section had its own staff, its own methods, and its own chain of reporting, all coordinated through Stephenson's personal authority.

The organizational structure reflected the British model. BSC served as the Western Hemisphere representative for three separate London-based organizations: SIS (Secret Intelligence Service, responsible for foreign intelligence), SOE (Special Operations Executive, responsible for sabotage and subversion in occupied Europe), and the Security Executive (responsible for counter-espionage). In London, these organizations were rivals, jealous of their turf and suspicious of each other. In New York, Stephenson unified them under one command. This consolidation was one of his genuine achievements -- and one reason the American intelligence officers who worked with BSC later built the OSS and CIA as unified organizations rather than as separate competing services.

![BSC organizational chart](../images/placeholders/05-bsc-org-chart.png)

The communications capability was critical. BSC operated its own cipher and communications systems, independent of normal diplomatic channels. Messages between New York and London were encrypted using one-time pads and transmitted by radio and cable. The volume was enormous: by some accounts, BSC processed over 100,000 secret communications during the war. The communications section also intercepted and decoded messages sent by Axis agents and diplomats operating in the Western Hemisphere -- work that complemented Bletchley Park's code-breaking operations in England.

BSC's counter-espionage work produced real results. The organization helped identify and neutralize German intelligence networks operating in North and South America. German agents in Brazil, Argentina, and Mexico were tracked, their communications intercepted, their networks mapped. Some of this work was done in cooperation with the FBI under J. Edgar Hoover; some was done behind the FBI's back. The relationship between BSC and the FBI was one of the most fraught aspects of Stephenson's operation. Hoover cooperated when it suited him, obstructed when it did not, and remained suspicious of British intelligence throughout the war.

The scale of BSC's operations in Latin America is often overlooked. German influence in South America was real. Several Latin American countries had large German immigrant communities, German-owned businesses, and established trade relationships with the Reich. German intelligence operated networks across the continent. BSC ran counter-operations in more than a dozen countries, from Mexico to Argentina, with varying degrees of success and varying degrees of legality under local laws.

By 1943, BSC had become an intelligence empire. The organization employed hundreds of people across the Western Hemisphere, managed networks of agents, ran propaganda operations through dozens of media outlets, and served as the primary liaison between British intelligence and the emerging American intelligence establishment. All of it operated from a suite of offices in a Manhattan skyscraper, under a cover story that would not have survived serious investigation.


## Chapter 4 -- Selling the War

The propaganda operations were BSC's most consequential work -- and the most legally precarious. Nicholas John Cull's *Selling War* (1995) remains the definitive study of how British propaganda sought to undermine American neutrality, and BSC was the primary instrument.

The challenge was formidable. In 1940, American isolationism was powerful, organized, and popular. The America First Committee, founded in September 1940, drew support from prominent figures including Charles Lindbergh, the most famous aviator in the world. Polls showed that a majority of Americans opposed entering the war. The Neutrality Acts had legal force. Any visible British effort to manipulate American opinion would have handed the isolationists their strongest argument: that foreign powers were trying to drag America into another European war.

![Propaganda operation schematic](../images/placeholders/06-propaganda-operation.png)

BSC's propaganda section operated through intermediaries. Stories were planted in sympathetic newspapers and wire services. Journalists who were willing to publish pro-British material -- some knowingly, some unknowingly -- were cultivated as assets. BSC produced reports, analyses, and news items that were fed to American media through front organizations, friendly editors, and cooperative columnists. The goal was to shape the information environment: to make the case for intervention appear to arise organically from American journalism rather than from a British intelligence operation.

The targets included isolationist organizations and their leaders. BSC investigated the America First Committee and its prominent members, looking for compromising information -- financial connections to German sources, personal scandals, anything that could be used to discredit the isolationist cause. Some of this material was genuine; some was manufactured. The line between intelligence gathering and political dirty tricks was deliberately blurred.

BSC also worked to influence specific policy outcomes. When legislation favorable to Britain was before Congress -- Lend-Lease, the Destroyers-for-Bases deal -- BSC mobilized its propaganda assets to support the measure. When hostile legislation appeared, BSC worked to undermine it. This was foreign interference in American democratic processes, conducted systematically and at scale, by a wartime ally operating with the tacit approval of the President.

The ethical questions are significant. BSC's propaganda helped bring the United States into a war against Nazi Germany -- a cause that most people today would consider just. The methods, however, involved deception, manipulation, and the violation of American sovereignty. Cull's research shows that BSC's operations were more extensive and more aggressive than the British government acknowledged for decades afterward. The BSC official history, a massive document compiled at the end of the war and kept classified until portions were released in the 1990s, provides a detailed accounting of propaganda operations that their organizers clearly believed were justified by the stakes.

The question of effectiveness is harder to answer. Pearl Harbor, on December 7, 1941, rendered the debate moot -- Japan's attack unified American opinion overnight in a way that years of propaganda had failed to achieve. BSC's advocates argued that their work had prepared the ground, that American opinion had been shifting before Pearl Harbor, and that BSC's propaganda accelerated the shift. Critics noted that the attack itself accomplished in hours what BSC had been working toward for eighteen months. The counterfactual -- what would have happened without BSC's propaganda campaign -- is inherently unanswerable.

What is documented is that BSC's propaganda operations continued throughout the war, shifting focus from the interventionist campaign to counter-propaganda against Axis information operations in Latin America and to support for the Allied cause more broadly. The organization that had been built to sell a war adapted to fighting one.
