# Part One: The Prize

## Chapter 1 -- The D'Arcy Concession

The story begins with a French archaeologist. In 1891, Jacques de Morgan, leading excavations at the ancient site of Susa in southwestern Persia, noticed natural petroleum seepages in the region. He mentioned these observations in his published reports. A decade later, those reports reached the desk of Antoine Kitabgi, a former Persian customs official living in Paris, who understood their commercial implications and began looking for an investor.

![Map of Persian oil regions and the D'Arcy Concession boundaries](../images/placeholders/01-persian-oil-map.png)

He found William Knox D'Arcy. Born in Devon, England, in 1849, D'Arcy had emigrated to Australia as a young man and made a fortune in the Mount Morgan gold mine in Queensland -- one of the richest gold deposits ever discovered. By the time Kitabgi approached him, D'Arcy was back in London, wealthy, restless, and willing to gamble on a new prospect. The two men met through intermediaries in early 1901.

The concession D'Arcy negotiated with the Persian government of Shah Mozaffar ad-Din was signed on May 28, 1901. Its terms reflected the vast asymmetry of power between a declining Persian state and a wealthy Western investor backed by the implicit support of the British government. D'Arcy received exclusive rights to explore for and exploit petroleum and natural gas across 480,000 square miles -- every province of Persia except the five northern provinces bordering Russia, which were excluded to avoid provoking St. Petersburg. The concession would run for sixty years. The Shah received 20,000 pounds in cash, 20,000 pounds in shares of the future company, and a promise of sixteen percent of annual net profits. The definition of "net profits" would be set by the company.

D'Arcy poured money into exploration. For seven years, the results were disappointing. Drilling at Chiah Surkh, in what is now western Iran, produced traces of oil but nothing commercially viable. D'Arcy's personal fortune was draining away. By 1905, he was looking for partners to share the financial burden. The Burmah Oil Company, a Glasgow-based firm with existing operations in Burma, agreed to invest, taking a controlling interest in the exploration company in exchange for fresh capital.

![The oil strike at Masjed Soleyman, 1908](../images/placeholders/02-masjed-soleyman.png)

The breakthrough came at Masjed Soleyman. On May 26, 1908, at a depth of 1,180 feet, the drill struck oil. The gusher that erupted was visible for miles. George Reynolds, the field engineer who had supervised years of frustrating work in brutal conditions, sent a telegram to the company's Glasgow office. The message was brief and transformative.

Within a year, the Anglo-Persian Oil Company was incorporated in London. D'Arcy became a director. The British government, under the prodding of Winston Churchill -- then First Lord of the Admiralty -- recognized the strategic implications immediately. The Royal Navy was converting from coal to oil-fired engines, a decision that would give British warships superior speed and range. Securing a reliable oil supply became a matter of national survival. In 1914, on the eve of the First World War, the British government purchased a controlling fifty-one percent stake in the Anglo-Persian Oil Company for two million pounds. The British state was now an oil company, and the oil company was in Persia.

This single transaction fused British strategic interests with Persian petroleum in a bond that would last decades. Every subsequent British decision about Persia -- diplomatic, military, and covert -- was shaped by the need to protect that investment. The intelligence operations that followed were consequences of this fusion.


## Chapter 2 -- The Great Game in Persia

Persia had been a playing field for rival empires long before oil was discovered. Throughout the nineteenth century, Britain and Russia competed for influence across Central and South Asia in what contemporaries called the Great Game. Persia occupied a central position in this contest: it sat between British India and the Russian Empire, and control of Persian territory could threaten either power's security.

![Map showing Anglo-Russian spheres of influence after the 1907 Convention](../images/placeholders/03-anglo-russian-spheres.png)

The British interest was defensive. Russia's expansion southward through the Caucasus and Central Asia, completed by the 1880s, raised the possibility that Russian forces could one day reach the Persian Gulf, the Indian Ocean, or the frontiers of British India. Britain maintained influence in Persia through diplomacy, subsidies to tribal leaders, and a network of consular officials who doubled as intelligence collectors. The Indian Political Service, which managed British relations with states along India's frontiers, treated southern Persia as an extension of its operational area.

Russia's interest was equally strategic. The warm-water ports of the Persian Gulf attracted Russian planners who operated from a coastline locked in ice for months each year. Russian officers, merchants, and agents penetrated northern Persia steadily through the late nineteenth century. The Cossack Brigade -- a Persian military unit trained, officered, and funded by Russia -- gave St. Petersburg a direct instrument of influence inside the Persian state. Founded in 1879, the brigade was commanded by Russian officers and reported to the Russian legation as much as to the Shah.

The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 formalized the division. Britain and Russia agreed to split Persia into three zones: a Russian sphere of influence in the north, including Tehran; a British sphere in the southeast, covering the approaches to India and, crucially, the oil-bearing regions of the southwest; and a neutral zone in between. The Persian government was informed of this arrangement. It was given no vote.

The convention papered over the rivalry without ending it. Both powers maintained intelligence networks throughout Persia. British agents operated from consulates in Isfahan, Kerman, and Shiraz. Russian agents worked through the Cossack Brigade and commercial networks in the north. Both recruited among the tribal confederations -- the Bakhtiari, the Qashqai, the Lurs -- whose armed horsemen controlled territory that neither the Persian government nor any foreign power could police from Tehran.

The First World War shattered this uneasy equilibrium. The Ottoman Empire entered the war on the side of the Central Powers in November 1914, and Persia -- officially neutral -- became a battleground. German agents, operating from the Ottoman frontier, worked to turn the southern tribes against Britain and disrupt oil production. Wilhelm Wassmuss, a German consul turned intelligence operative, achieved remarkable success among the Qashqai and Tangistani tribes, organizing resistance that tied down thousands of British and Indian troops. The British formed the South Persia Rifles, a locally recruited force under British officers, to counter the tribal threat to the oil installations.

Russian forces occupied much of northern Persia. Ottoman and Russian armies fought across Persian territory in campaigns that devastated the civilian population. The Persian government, unable to enforce its own neutrality, watched as foreign armies marched through its territory, recruited its subjects, and fought their wars on its soil.

By the end of the war, Persia was exhausted. The Qajar dynasty that had ruled since the eighteenth century was discredited. The country's infrastructure was shattered. Famine had killed millions. The Russian Revolution of 1917 removed one imperial player from the board, but the power vacuum it created was filled by new dangers. Bolshevik forces probed the northern frontier. The British, momentarily dominant, attempted to formalize their control through the Anglo-Persian Agreement of 1919, which would have made Persia effectively a British protectorate. The agreement provoked fierce opposition inside Persia and was never ratified by the Majles, the Persian parliament.

The stage was set for a new kind of intervention.
