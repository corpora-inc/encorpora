# Part Four: Context

## Chapter 7 -- The Azerbaijan Crisis

The wartime agreement between Britain, the Soviet Union, and the United States required all foreign troops to leave Iran within six months of the end of hostilities. The war in Europe ended on May 8, 1945. The deadline was March 2, 1946. The British and Americans withdrew on schedule. The Soviets did not.

In November 1945, Soviet-backed separatist movements declared autonomous republics in the Iranian provinces of Azerbaijan and Kurdistan. The Azerbaijan People's Government, led by Ja'far Pishevari, was supported by Soviet troops that prevented the Iranian army from entering the province. A parallel Kurdish Republic of Mahabad was proclaimed under Qazi Muhammad. Both entities operated under Soviet military protection and adopted reforms -- land redistribution, language rights, expanded education -- that served Soviet ideological interests.

![Map of the Azerbaijan Crisis showing Soviet occupation zone and separatist republics](../images/placeholders/11-azerbaijan-crisis-map.png)

The crisis that followed was the first direct confrontation between the Western powers and the Soviet Union in the postwar world. Some historians mark it as the opening episode of the Cold War.

Iran brought the matter to the newly created United Nations Security Council in January 1946 -- one of the first disputes the body addressed. The American position, under President Harry Truman, hardened over the course of the crisis. The traditional account holds that Truman sent Stalin a blunt ultimatum demanding Soviet withdrawal, though the documentary evidence for such an ultimatum is disputed. What is documented is sustained American diplomatic pressure, channeled through the UN and through direct communications, combined with British concern about Soviet proximity to the oil fields.

The Soviet Union withdrew its forces in May 1946, after negotiating an oil concession agreement with the Iranian government of Prime Minister Ahmad Qavam. The agreement would have given the Soviet Union a joint oil company in northern Iran -- a mirror of the British concession in the south. Once Soviet troops were gone, the Iranian army moved north. The Azerbaijan autonomous government collapsed in December 1946. Pishevari fled to the Soviet Union. The Kurdish Republic of Mahabad fell shortly afterward; Qazi Muhammad was hanged.

The Iranian Majles then voted down the Soviet oil concession, refusing to ratify the agreement Qavam had negotiated under duress. The Soviets received nothing. The episode demonstrated a pattern that would recur throughout the Cold War: a smaller country using great-power competition as leverage, playing each side against the other, and sometimes paying a devastating price for the gamble.

The Azerbaijan Crisis established several precedents. It demonstrated that the Soviet Union would use military occupation to create puppet states along its borders. It demonstrated that the United States would oppose such expansion -- at least when oil was involved. And it demonstrated that the United Nations could serve as a forum for challenging great-power aggression, even if its enforcement mechanisms were limited.

For Iran, the crisis reinforced the central lesson of the preceding half-century: the country's location and resources made it a permanent target. Sovereignty depended on balancing external pressures, and no balance was permanent.


## Chapter 8 -- The Oil Thread

![Timeline of foreign interventions in Iran from 1901 to 1953](../images/placeholders/10-iran-intervention-timeline.png)

The story told in this volume -- from D'Arcy's concession in 1901 to the Azerbaijan Crisis in 1946 -- has a single thread running through it: petroleum. Every major foreign intervention in Iran during this period was driven, directly or indirectly, by the strategic value of Iranian oil.

The D'Arcy Concession gave Britain a commercial stake in Persia. The British government's purchase of a majority share in the Anglo-Persian Oil Company in 1914 transformed that commercial stake into a strategic one. The 1921 coup that brought Reza Khan to power served British interests by installing a strong ruler who could maintain order in a country that contained vital British infrastructure. The Allied invasion of 1941 was driven by the need to protect the oil fields, secure the supply corridor, and deny both to the enemy. The Azerbaijan Crisis of 1946 was, at its core, a struggle over who would control the oil resources of northern Iran.

![Comparison chart of the D'Arcy, 1933, and proposed Soviet oil concession terms](../images/placeholders/12-oil-concession-comparison.png)

Daniel Yergin, in *The Prize* (1991), traced this connection across the entire twentieth century. The conversion of the Royal Navy from coal to oil before the First World War created a dependency that shaped British foreign policy for decades. Churchill understood this in 1914 when he argued for the government purchase of Anglo-Persian shares. The strategic logic was simple: a navy that runs on oil must control its oil supply, or it is at the mercy of whoever does.

The same logic applied to every power that depended on petroleum. Germany's lack of domestic oil reserves drove its interest in Persian oil -- and in disrupting British access to it. The Soviet Union's desire for an oil concession in northern Iran after the Second World War reflected both economic interest and strategic calculation: controlling a source of oil that might otherwise fuel an adversary.

The intelligence operations described in this volume were instruments of this petroleum-driven competition. Spies, saboteurs, tribal proxies, coups, and invasions were all means to the same end: ensuring that the oil flowed in the right direction, to the right power, on the right terms. The people of Iran -- whose country contained the oil, whose labor extracted it, and whose sovereignty was repeatedly violated to secure it -- were treated as incidental to the contest.

This pattern did not end in 1946. The nationalization crisis of 1951-1953, when Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh attempted to bring Iranian oil under Iranian control, provoked the most consequential covert operation of the Cold War: the Anglo-American coup of August 1953, known by its CIA codename, Operation Ajax. That story is told in Volumes 20 and 21 of this series. The Tehran Conference of 1943, where Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin met in the occupied Iranian capital to plan the final campaigns of the Second World War, is the subject of Volume 20. The coup itself -- Kermit Roosevelt, MI6, the CIA, and the destruction of Iranian democracy -- is the subject of Volume 21.

The shadow war in Persia was never really about Persia. It was about what lay beneath Persian soil. Understanding that fact is essential to understanding everything that followed.
