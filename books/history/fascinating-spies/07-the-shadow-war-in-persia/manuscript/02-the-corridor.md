# Part Two: The Corridor

## Chapter 3 -- Reza Khan

On the night of February 21, 1921, approximately 3,000 soldiers of the Persian Cossack Division marched on Tehran. They were led by Reza Khan, a forty-three-year-old brigadier general who had risen through the ranks from a childhood of poverty in the Alborz Mountains. By morning, the government had fallen. Zia al-Din Tabatabaei, a young journalist with British connections, was installed as prime minister. Reza Khan became commander of the army. Within two years, Reza Khan had pushed Zia aside. Within four years, he had deposed the last Qajar shah and crowned himself Reza Shah Pahlavi, founding a new dynasty.

![Reza Khan in military uniform during the rise to power](../images/placeholders/04-reza-khan.png)

The question that historians still debate is how much of this was a British operation.

The circumstantial evidence is substantial. General Edmund Ironside, the British military commander in northern Persia, had been looking for a strong Persian officer to take control of the Cossack Division after the Russian officers who had commanded it were dismissed. Ironside's diaries, published decades later, describe meetings with Reza Khan and a deliberate effort to promote him. The British legation in Tehran was aware of the coup in advance. Cyrus Ghani, drawing on Persian and British archival sources in *Iran and the Rise of Reza Shah* (1998), concluded that British officials facilitated the coup, though the degree of direction versus encouragement remains ambiguous.

The direct documentary evidence is incomplete. Key British files from this period remain classified or have been weeded -- a term of archival art meaning that documents were removed before the files were opened to researchers. Ervand Abrahamian, in *Iran Between Two Revolutions* (1982), noted that the British role has been argued in three ways: that Britain planned and executed the coup using Reza Khan as a tool; that Britain merely encouraged a coup it knew was coming; or that Reza Khan acted largely on his own initiative with British acquiescence after the fact. The evidence supports the middle position most comfortably, but certainty is impossible with the documentary gaps that exist.

What is clear is the outcome. Reza Khan consolidated power with systematic efficiency. He suppressed tribal autonomy, built a national army, imposed conscription, and created a centralized state apparatus where none had existed. He built roads, railways, and schools. He banned the veil and mandated Western dress. He changed the country's international name from Persia to Iran in 1935, asserting an identity older than any foreign label.

![The Abadan refinery complex in the 1930s](../images/placeholders/05-abadan-refinery.png)

His relationship with the Anglo-Persian Oil Company -- renamed the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company in 1935 -- was complicated. Reza Shah resented the terms of the D'Arcy Concession, which he viewed as colonial extraction. In 1932, he dramatically cancelled the concession, provoking a diplomatic crisis. The dispute was resolved through negotiation: the 1933 agreement extended the concession to 1993 but increased Iran's share of profits and reduced the concession area. The fundamental structure remained unchanged. A British-controlled company extracted Iranian oil, refined it at the enormous Abadan refinery -- the largest in the world -- and shipped it to fuel the British Empire. Iran received a fraction of the revenue.

The intelligence dimension of this period is difficult to reconstruct with precision. The British maintained their traditional networks: consular officials, tribal contacts, commercial intermediaries. The Soviet Union, despite the upheaval of its own revolution and civil war, rebuilt an intelligence presence in northern Iran through trade missions and Comintern contacts. The Germans, as we shall see, were building something new.

Reza Shah attempted to play these competing powers against each other. He invited German technical advisors, awarded contracts to German firms, and cultivated a relationship with Berlin as a counterweight to British and Soviet pressure. By the late 1930s, Germany was Iran's largest trading partner. German engineers built factories, railways, and infrastructure projects across the country. German residents in Iran numbered approximately 2,000 by 1941.

This German connection would prove fateful.


## Chapter 4 -- The German Card

Germany's interest in Persia predated the oil age. During the First World War, the Kaiser's government ran an ambitious intelligence campaign across the Middle East, attempting to turn Muslim populations against the British and Russian empires. In Persia, this effort centered on tribal insurgency. German agents, working from the Ottoman frontier, distributed weapons, money, and promises to tribal leaders willing to attack British positions and oil installations.

![German intelligence agents operating in wartime Persia](../images/placeholders/06-german-agents.png)

The most effective of these agents was Wilhelm Wassmuss, sometimes called the "German Lawrence" -- a comparison he would have rejected. Wassmuss had served as German consul in Bushire before the war and spoke fluent Persian. When the war began, he escaped British custody, trekked across southern Persia, and organized the Qashqai and Tangistani tribes into an armed resistance that disrupted British operations for years. He achieved this with minimal resources: a few subordinates, crates of rifles, bags of gold coins, and a talent for persuasion. His campaign forced Britain to divert thousands of troops to protect the oil pipeline from the fields to the coast.

Wassmuss's operation was tactically effective and strategically futile. Germany lost the war. Wassmuss was stranded in Persia, his promises to the tribes unfulfilled, and eventually returned to Germany impoverished. But the precedent was established: Persia's tribal geography made it vulnerable to covert operations by any power willing to invest in local proxies.

The interwar period saw Germany rebuild its presence through commerce rather than espionage. Under the Weimar Republic and then the Third Reich, German trade with Iran expanded rapidly. By the late 1930s, Germany accounted for approximately forty-one percent of Iran's foreign trade. German firms built the Trans-Iranian Railway. German technicians staffed Iranian factories. German teachers worked in Iranian schools. This commercial penetration served multiple purposes: it generated intelligence, it built goodwill, and it created a network of German nationals who could be activated for covert purposes if war came.

The Abwehr -- German military intelligence under Admiral Wilhelm Canaris -- maintained agents in Iran throughout the 1930s. Their primary mission was intelligence collection: monitoring British military dispositions, mapping oil infrastructure, and assessing the reliability of Iranian military and tribal forces. The SD -- the intelligence service of the SS under Reinhard Heydrich -- also had operatives in the region, sometimes working at cross-purposes with the Abwehr, as was typical of the competitive bureaucratic culture of the Third Reich.

When the Second World War began in September 1939, Iran declared neutrality. Reza Shah had no desire to repeat the experience of 1914-1918, when Persia's neutrality had been violated by every belligerent. But neutrality required enforcement, and Iran lacked the military strength to deter great powers. The German community in Iran became a focal point of British anxiety. Were these engineers, merchants, and teachers also agents? How many were Abwehr or SD operatives? What could they do if ordered to sabotage the oil fields or incite tribal rebellion?

Adrian O'Sullivan, in *Nazi Secret Warfare in Occupied Persia* (2014), documented the German intelligence infrastructure in detail. The picture he drew was of a network that was real but limited -- capable of intelligence collection and potential sabotage, but far short of the existential threat that British intelligence reports sometimes implied. The gap between the actual German capability and the British perception of it matters, because the perception drove the decision to invade.

British intelligence assessments in 1940-1941 painted an alarming picture. German agents were said to be cultivating tribal leaders, stockpiling weapons, and preparing to seize the oil fields or destroy them. The fall of France in June 1940 and Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 transformed the strategic calculus. Iran was no longer a peripheral concern. It sat between the oil fields that fueled the British war effort and the Soviet Union that was now fighting for survival. The corridor through Iran was the only reliable supply route from the Western Allies to the Soviet front.

The German presence in Iran -- real, exaggerated, or somewhere in between -- provided the justification for what came next.
