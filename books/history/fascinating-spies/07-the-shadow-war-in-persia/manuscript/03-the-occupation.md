# Part Three: The Occupation

## Chapter 5 -- The Invasion

On August 25, 1941, British and Soviet forces invaded Iran simultaneously from opposite directions. The British attacked from the south and west: troops from India crossed the border from Baluchistan and Iraq, while the Royal Navy sailed into the Persian Gulf and seized the port of Bandar Shahpur. Soviet forces attacked from the north, crossing the border at multiple points and advancing toward Tabriz and Tehran. The Iranian military, equipped with obsolete weapons and organized for internal security rather than defense against great powers, collapsed within days.

![Map of the Allied invasion routes, August 1941](../images/placeholders/07-allied-invasion-map.png)

The stated justification was the German presence in Iran. Britain and the Soviet Union had issued a joint demand on August 16 that Iran expel all German nationals. Reza Shah stalled, proposing partial measures. The Allies interpreted his response as refusal and invaded nine days later.

The real reasons were strategic. Three imperatives converged. First, the Abadan refinery and the southwestern oil fields were essential to the British war effort; any risk of their destruction or seizure by hostile forces was intolerable. Second, Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union on June 22, 1941 -- Operation Barbarossa -- created an urgent need for a supply route from the Western Allies to the Soviet front. The Arctic convoys through Murmansk were dangerous and seasonal. The Trans-Siberian route through Vladivostok was long and vulnerable to Japanese interdiction. Iran offered a third corridor: supplies could be shipped to Persian Gulf ports, transported by road and rail across Iran, and delivered to the Soviet border. Third, the presence of German agents in Iran, whatever their actual capability, provided a politically useful casus belli for an invasion that would have happened regardless.

The military operation was brief and one-sided. The Iranian air force was destroyed on the ground within hours. The navy -- four coastal vessels -- was sunk or captured in the Persian Gulf. Ground resistance lasted three days in most sectors. Iranian casualties were approximately 800 killed. British casualties were 22 killed. Soviet casualties were unreported but believed to be light.

Reza Shah abdicated on September 16, 1941, under direct Allied pressure. The British had decided he was too dangerous to leave in power -- too sympathetic to Germany, too capable of organizing resistance. He was exiled, first to Mauritius, then to South Africa, where he died in 1944. His twenty-one-year-old son, Mohammad Reza, was placed on the throne. The young shah would rule for thirty-eight years, until the revolution of 1979, but in August 1941, he was a figurehead installed by foreign armies.

The invasion of Iran was a violation of international law that the Allies barely bothered to justify beyond the immediate wartime emergency. Iran was a neutral country. Its neutrality was guaranteed by no treaty, defended by no ally, and respected by no one when strategic imperatives demanded otherwise. The precedent was clear: a country sitting on vital resources, positioned on a critical supply route, and too weak to defend itself would be occupied whenever the great powers found it convenient. This lesson was absorbed by every subsequent Iranian government.


## Chapter 6 -- The Persian Corridor

The supply route through Iran became one of the decisive logistics operations of the Second World War. Between 1941 and 1945, the Persian Corridor delivered approximately 4.2 million long tons of Lend-Lease supplies to the Soviet Union -- roughly one-quarter of all Western aid that reached the Soviet front. The materiel included trucks, jeeps, locomotives, aircraft, ammunition, food, clothing, steel, and petroleum products.

![Map of the Persian Corridor supply route from Gulf ports to Soviet border](../images/placeholders/08-persian-corridor-map.png)

The scale of the logistical challenge was enormous. T.H. Vail Motter, in the official US Army history *The Persian Corridor and Aid to Russia* (1952), documented an operation that required building or rebuilding virtually every piece of transportation infrastructure in the country. The ports of Bandar Shahpur and Khorramshahr were expanded to handle oceanic cargo volumes. The Trans-Iranian Railway, built by Reza Shah to connect the Persian Gulf to the Caspian Sea, was taken over by the Allies and operated at capacity. Roads that had been designed for donkey caravans were widened, graded, and paved to carry convoys of American-built trucks.

The United States entered the corridor operation after Pearl Harbor. The Persian Gulf Command, established in December 1943 as a subordinate element of the US Army's Services of Supply, eventually employed approximately 30,000 American military personnel in Iran. They operated the railway, drove the trucks, managed the ports, and assembled vehicles and aircraft that arrived in crates from American factories. At its peak, the corridor delivered 282,097 long tons in a single month -- July 1944.

![Lend-Lease supplies being loaded and transported through Iran](../images/placeholders/09-lend-lease-supplies.png)

The intelligence infrastructure that accompanied this operation was substantial. British, Soviet, and American intelligence services all operated in wartime Iran, sometimes cooperating, sometimes competing, and sometimes working at direct cross-purposes. The British maintained their traditional networks, now supplemented by military intelligence officers attached to the occupation forces. The Soviet NKVD operated freely in the northern occupation zone, establishing a presence that would persist after the war. American intelligence -- first the Office of Strategic Services, then its successors -- arrived with the Persian Gulf Command and began building its own networks.

The German intelligence threat, which had provided the justification for the invasion, did persist in reduced form throughout the occupation. O'Sullivan documented Abwehr and SD efforts to maintain agent networks, contact tribal leaders, and plan sabotage operations against the corridor's infrastructure. A German operation codenamed "Franz" parachuted agents into Iran in 1943 to organize tribal resistance. The operation failed; the agents were captured or killed. Other German plans -- including a reported scheme to assassinate the Allied leaders at the Tehran Conference in November 1943 -- remain subjects of debate among historians. The Tehran plot is addressed in Volume 20 of this series.

The occupation transformed Iranian society. The presence of tens of thousands of foreign troops created economic disruption: inflation, shortages of basic goods, and the diversion of transportation infrastructure from civilian to military use. The political effect was equally significant. The young Mohammad Reza Shah, installed by the Allies, lacked the authority of his father. Political parties, suppressed under Reza Shah, proliferated. The Tudeh Party -- the Iranian communist party, supported by the Soviet occupation forces -- became a significant political force. Tribal leaders, freed from the centralizing pressure of Reza Shah's army, reasserted their autonomy.

Iran during the occupation was a country run by foreigners. The Allies controlled the oil, the transportation network, the ports, and the major cities. The Iranian government administered what remained. The sovereignty of the country existed in theory. The reality was military occupation dressed in diplomatic courtesies.

The question that mattered was: what would happen when the war ended and the occupation was supposed to end with it?
