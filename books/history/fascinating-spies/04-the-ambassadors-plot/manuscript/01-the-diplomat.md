# Part One: The Diplomat

## Chapter 1 -- The Mission

Robert Hamilton Bruce Lockhart was born in 1887 in Anstruther, a fishing town on the coast of Fife in Scotland. His family was comfortably middle-class. His uncle was a successful rubber planter in Malaya. Lockhart attended Fettes College in Edinburgh -- the same school that would later claim Ian Fleming as an alumnus -- and displayed talents for languages and rugby in roughly equal measure.

![Portrait of R.H. Bruce Lockhart, circa 1918](../images/placeholders/01-lockhart-portrait.png)

At nineteen, Lockhart went to Malaya to work on his uncle's rubber estate. He stayed for several years, learned Malay, developed a taste for adventure, and acquired a reputation for charm and indiscretion in roughly equal proportions. He returned to Britain and entered the consular service, a path that led him to Moscow in 1912 as vice-consul.

The pre-war posting was formative. Lockhart learned Russian, made contacts across Moscow society, and developed an understanding of Russian politics that few British officials possessed. He was recalled in 1917 after a scandal involving a Russian woman -- a pattern that would repeat. But his Russian expertise made him valuable, and when the Bolshevik Revolution of November 1917 upended British foreign policy, Lockhart was one of the few people the War Cabinet could turn to who actually knew the country.

In January 1918, the British government sent Lockhart back to Russia as head of a special mission. His title was deliberately vague. Britain had not recognized the Bolshevik government and was not going to. But the Bolsheviks controlled most of European Russia, and someone had to talk to them. Lockhart's instructions, such as they were, came from the War Cabinet: establish contact with the Bolshevik leadership, assess their staying power, and above all prevent Russia from making a separate peace with Germany that would free millions of German troops for the Western Front.

The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed on March 3, 1918, ended the war between Russia and the Central Powers on terms devastating to Russia. The Bolsheviks ceded Finland, the Baltic states, Poland, Ukraine, and parts of the Caucasus -- roughly a third of the old empire's population and agricultural land. Lenin accepted because he had no choice. The Russian army had disintegrated. Soldiers were voting with their feet, deserting by the hundreds of thousands. Continuing the war was a theoretical position, and Lenin dealt in realities.

![Map showing Lockhart's route from London to Petrograd to Moscow](../images/placeholders/02-petrograd-moscow-map.png)

For the British, Brest-Litovsk was a catastrophe. The entire Eastern Front had vanished. Germany could now concentrate its forces in France. The spring offensive of March 1918 -- Ludendorff's Kaiserschlacht -- nearly broke the Allied lines. In this context, Lockhart's diplomatic mission acquired a sharper edge. Diplomacy was still the official policy, but the question of Allied military intervention in Russia -- landing troops at Murmansk, Archangel, or Vladivostok to reopen an Eastern Front or at least prevent German access to Russian resources -- moved from theoretical to operational.

Lockhart, in his memoirs, presents himself as initially sympathetic to the Bolsheviks and opposed to intervention. He met Trotsky several times and found him impressive. He believed, or later claimed to have believed, that accommodation with the Bolsheviks was preferable to confrontation. Historians debate how sincere this stance was. Jonathan Schneer argues that Lockhart's sympathies were genuine but eroded rapidly as events unfolded. Robert Service sees the diplomatic posture as more calculating from the start.

What is clear is that by the summer of 1918, Lockhart had moved from diplomacy to conspiracy. The transition was gradual, driven by events, and the precise moment when he crossed the line from diplomat to intelligence operative is impossible to pin down -- which may be exactly how he wanted it.


## Chapter 2 -- Moscow, 1918

To understand the world Lockhart inhabited, you need to picture Moscow in the first half of 1918. The Bolshevik government had moved the capital from Petrograd in March, fearing that the German army, which had resumed its advance during the Brest-Litovsk negotiations, might take the old imperial capital. The government installed itself in the Kremlin. Moscow became the center of revolutionary power.

![The British Embassy building in Moscow, 1918](../images/placeholders/03-british-embassy.png)

The city was chaotic. Food was scarce. The old social order had collapsed, but the new one had not yet solidified. Former aristocrats sold their possessions on street corners. Workers' committees ran factories with varying degrees of competence. The Cheka -- the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage, established in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky -- was rapidly expanding its powers, but in early 1918 it was still a relatively small organization, feeling its way.

The diplomatic community existed in a strange limbo. Most Allied governments had not recognized the Bolshevik regime. Their representatives remained in Russia in various unofficial capacities, observing, reporting, and increasingly scheming. The French had their own intelligence operatives. The Americans had Raymond Robins of the Red Cross, an unofficial channel to Lenin. And the British had Lockhart.

Lockhart operated from the old British Embassy. He had a small staff. He met regularly with Bolshevik officials, including Trotsky and Chicherin, the Commissar for Foreign Affairs. He sent reports to London. And he watched the Allied intervention debate with growing urgency. By spring 1918, British and French troops had landed at Murmansk, initially with Bolshevik acquiescence, to protect Allied war supplies from falling to the Germans. But the scope and purpose of the intervention kept expanding, and Bolshevik cooperation curdled into suspicion and then hostility.

The Allied community in Moscow formed a tight circle. Lockhart knew the French consul general, Fernand Grenard. He knew the American representatives. And he was increasingly in contact with British intelligence operatives who were arriving in Russia under various covers -- most importantly Sidney Reilly and George Hill.

Reilly, as covered in Volume 14, was already operating in Russia under multiple identities. Hill, covered in Volume 15, had been running intelligence operations since 1917. Neither man was under Lockhart's formal authority. The lines of command were tangled. Lockhart was the senior British civilian representative. Reilly worked for Mansfield Cumming's Secret Intelligence Service. Hill reported to military intelligence. In theory, they should have coordinated through Lockhart. In practice, each man ran his own operations with minimal oversight.

This was the world of British intelligence in 1918: improvised, under-resourced, and dependent on individuals who operated with extraordinary autonomy and very little institutional support. It produced both remarkable successes and spectacular failures. The Lockhart Plot would prove to be the most consequential of the failures.

It was also in this Moscow that Lockhart met Moura Budberg -- born Baroness Moura Zakrevskaya, married to Count Benckendorff, later companion to Maxim Gorky and H.G. Wells, suspected by multiple intelligence services of working for the Soviets. Their relationship, begun in early 1918, would become one of the more tangled personal stories in intelligence history. Lockhart fell deeply in love. Whether Moura's feelings were equally straightforward, or whether she was performing a role assigned by the Cheka, is a question that biographers have answered differently. Nina Berberova's biography presents her as a survivor who used every available tool -- including romantic attachment -- to navigate impossible circumstances. Lockhart's memoirs are passionate but, characteristically, omit operational details.
