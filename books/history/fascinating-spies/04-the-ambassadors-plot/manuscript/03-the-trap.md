# Part Three: The Trap

## Chapter 5 -- The Cheka

Felix Edmundovich Dzerzhinsky was a Polish aristocrat turned revolutionary who had spent most of his adult life in Tsarist prisons and Siberian exile. Lenin appointed him to head the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission -- the Cheka -- in December 1917. Dzerzhinsky brought to the job an ascetic temperament, absolute ideological conviction, and an organizational talent that would make the Cheka the most feared institution in revolutionary Russia.

![Felix Dzerzhinsky and the early Cheka organization](../images/placeholders/06-dzerzhinsky-cheka.png)

By mid-1918, the Cheka was growing rapidly but remained a work in progress. It had perhaps a few thousand operatives in Moscow, a network of regional offices of varying competence, and broad authority to arrest, interrogate, and execute enemies of the revolution. Its methods were crude by the standards of later Soviet intelligence, but its willingness to use violence was unlimited. The Cheka had already conducted summary executions on a significant scale.

The Lettish trap theory -- the argument that the Cheka controlled the Lockhart Plot from the beginning -- rests on several pieces of evidence. First, Colonel Berzin, the Latvian officer who approached Lockhart, was almost certainly acting under Cheka direction by the time the plot reached its operational phase. The Cheka's own files, partially opened after the Soviet collapse, indicate that Berzin was reporting to Dzerzhinsky's deputy, Yakov Peters, throughout the conspiracy.

Second, the plot's exposure was suspiciously clean. On August 30, 1918, Fanny Kaplan shot Lenin outside a factory in Moscow, seriously wounding him. On the same day, Leonid Kannegiesser, a young military cadet, assassinated Moisei Uritsky, the head of the Petrograd Cheka. These attacks -- by a Socialist Revolutionary and a cadet with unclear political affiliations -- triggered the immediate exposure of the Lockhart Plot and the arrest of virtually everyone connected to it. The simultaneity suggests coordination, or at least that the Cheka was ready to move and the assassination attempts provided the pretext.

Third, the Cheka's response was too swift and too comprehensive to have been improvised. Within hours of the Kaplan shooting, Cheka agents raided the British mission. Lockhart was arrested at his apartment. Reilly escaped but his networks were rolled up. The French consul general, Grenard, and several French intelligence operatives were also detained. The precision of the operation suggests the Cheka knew exactly who to arrest and where to find them.

The counter-argument -- that the plot was genuine and the Cheka discovered it through intelligence work rather than manufacturing it -- also has evidence. Lockhart's payments to Berzin were real. Reilly's networks were real. The Allied intervention was real. It is entirely possible that Berzin was initially genuine in his disaffection and was turned by the Cheka after the conspiracy was already underway -- that the Cheka infiltrated a real plot rather than creating a fictitious one.

![Timeline of critical events, summer 1918](../images/placeholders/09-summer-1918-timeline.png)

Jonathan Schneer's 2020 account argues for a middle position: the plot had real elements, but the Cheka gained control of it early enough that the operational phase was largely theater. Robert Service emphasizes the chaos and cross-purposes of the period, suggesting that clean narratives -- either "genuine plot" or "pure provocation" -- impose more coherence on events than they actually possessed.

The honest answer is that we do not know with certainty. The Cheka's archives have been selectively opened. Key documents may have been destroyed or remain classified. The participants who survived all had reasons to reshape their accounts. And the events themselves unfolded in conditions of such chaos that a single authoritative narrative may be unrecoverable.


## Chapter 6 -- The Aftermath

Lockhart's arrest on August 31, 1918, triggered a diplomatic crisis. He was detained at the Cheka's Lubyanka headquarters and interrogated by Yakov Peters. Lockhart maintained that he was a diplomatic representative and that his detention violated international law. The Bolsheviks countered that he was an accredited diplomat to no recognized government, operating as a spy and conspirator, and entitled to no diplomatic protection.

![Moura Budberg, circa 1920](../images/placeholders/07-moura-budberg.png)

Moura Budberg's role during Lockhart's detention remains one of the more intriguing aspects of the affair. She was permitted to visit him in prison -- an extraordinary concession that has fueled speculation about her relationship with the Cheka. Was she allowed access because the Cheka wanted to use her as a channel for communication with Lockhart? Was she reporting on his state of mind? Or had she negotiated access through her own contacts and survival instincts? Berberova's biography suggests that Moura operated in a gray zone, maintaining relationships with both sides simultaneously, belonging fully to neither.

Lockhart was held for approximately a month. The British government, which had arrested Maxim Litvinov -- the Bolshevik representative in London -- in retaliation, negotiated an exchange. Lockhart was released in early October 1918 and returned to Britain via Finland and Scandinavia. Reilly had already escaped through the northern ports. Hill made his way out through different channels.

![Map of diplomatic consequences -- arrests and expulsions across Europe](../images/placeholders/10-diplomatic-consequences.png)

The immediate consequence of the plot's exposure was the Red Terror. On September 5, 1918, the Soviet government formally decreed a campaign of mass repression against class enemies and counter-revolutionaries. The Red Terror had been building before the Lockhart affair -- the Cheka had been conducting executions since early 1918 -- but the assassination attempts against Lenin and Uritsky, combined with the exposure of the Allied conspiracy, provided both the justification and the catalyst for systematic state violence.

![The Red Terror -- arrests and executions, autumn 1918](../images/placeholders/11-red-terror.png)

The historical debate over the Red Terror's relationship to the Lockhart Plot is significant. The Bolshevik government presented the Terror as a defensive response to foreign aggression and internal conspiracy. The plot's exposure allowed the regime to frame repression as self-defense against demonstrable enemies. Whether the Terror would have occurred without the Lockhart affair -- given the pressures of civil war, foreign intervention, and ideological commitment to class warfare -- is a counterfactual that historians answer differently based on their broader interpretations of Bolshevik governance.

The longer-term diplomatic consequence was the poisoning of Anglo-Soviet relations for a generation. The Lockhart Plot confirmed the Bolsheviks' conviction that the Western powers were actively trying to destroy their regime. This conviction was, as the plot demonstrated, largely correct. The resulting mutual hostility shaped Soviet foreign policy through the 1920s and into the 1930s, contributing to the atmosphere of suspicion that made collective security against Nazi Germany so difficult to achieve.

Lockhart returned to Britain and spent the interwar years as a journalist, banker, and minor diplomat. He published *Memoirs of a British Agent* in 1932, a beautifully written and carefully selective account that made him famous. The book is vivid on atmosphere, acute on personality, and systematically vague on operational details. It remains one of the classic memoirs of the intelligence world -- admired, widely read, and treated by historians with the caution that any memoir by a professional intelligence operative deserves.
