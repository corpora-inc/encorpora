# Part Four: Context

## Chapter 7 -- Three Versions

The Lockhart Plot left behind three British accounts of the same events, written by the three men most deeply involved. They cannot all be true. The contradictions are instructive.

![Diagram comparing the three British accounts -- Lockhart, Reilly, Hill](../images/placeholders/12-three-versions-diagram.png)

Lockhart's *Memoirs of a British Agent* (1932) is the most polished and the most widely read. Lockhart presents himself as a diplomat drawn reluctantly into conspiracy by circumstances -- the Allied intervention, the intransigence of his own government, the impossibility of maintaining a purely diplomatic role in a country descending into civil war. The plot, in his telling, was real but limited. He emphasizes his contacts with Latvian officers as a diplomatic channel rather than a conspiratorial one. He is notably vague about money, about the operational details of what the Latvian units were supposed to do, and about the degree to which London authorized his actions. The memoir is an exercise in controlled revelation: enough detail to be compelling, enough omission to be safe.

Reilly's account survives only in fragments -- letters, debriefing reports, and the biography written by Robin Bruce Lockhart (Bruce Lockhart's son) in 1967. Reilly's version is more dramatic. He presents the plot as a genuine and nearly successful attempt to overthrow the Bolshevik government, thwarted by bad luck and betrayal. In Reilly's telling, the Latvian Riflemen were ready to act, the Bolshevik leadership was within reach, and the operation failed because of inadequate support from London and premature exposure. Reilly portrays himself as the driving force of the conspiracy, with Lockhart as a supportive but cautious diplomat and Hill as a capable subordinate.

Andrew Cook's revisionist biography of Reilly (2004) used declassified files to demonstrate that Reilly systematically exaggerated his role and fabricated elements of his earlier career. This does not necessarily mean his account of the Lockhart Plot is false, but it means it cannot be taken at face value. Reilly was a man who constructed his identity through narrative, and each story served a purpose.

Hill's account, contained in his memoirs and debriefing reports, is the most restrained. Hill presents himself as a professional intelligence officer running agent networks and sabotage operations, with the Latvian conspiracy as one element among several. His version gives Lockhart more agency in the plot than Lockhart claims and Reilly less than Reilly claims. Hill's account is the least dramatic and, partly for that reason, often treated by historians as the most reliable -- though Hill too had reasons to shape his narrative.

The contradictions are concentrated around three questions. First, who initiated the contact with the Latvian officers? Lockhart says he was approached. Reilly implies he made the running. Hill suggests the initiative came from the Latvian side with Reilly as the primary British contact. Second, what was the scope of the planned operation? Lockhart minimizes it. Reilly maximizes it. Hill describes something in between. Third, what did London know? Lockhart implies he acted largely independently. Reilly suggests he had backing from Cumming's SIS. Hill's account is ambiguous.

These contradictions may reflect genuine differences in knowledge -- each man saw a different part of the operation. They may reflect deliberate shaping of the narrative for personal or institutional reasons. Or they may reflect the fundamental problem of intelligence history: the participants are professional deceivers, and their accounts are performances, not confessions.

The Cheka's version -- that the entire operation was a provocation controlled from the beginning -- adds a fourth narrative that contradicts all three British accounts in different ways. If the Cheka controlled Berzin from the start, then the British accounts are all describing their own manipulation, and the differences between them reflect differences in how completely each man was deceived.


## Chapter 8 -- The Legacy

The Lockhart Plot of August 1918 was a small event with large consequences. The conspiracy itself -- a handful of British operatives, some Latvian officers, an uncertain amount of money, and a plan that depended on more things going right than any reasonable assessment would predict -- was modest in scale. Its effects were disproportionate.

For Anglo-Soviet relations, the plot confirmed a pattern of mutual hostility that would persist for decades. The Bolsheviks had evidence -- real evidence, confirmed by the participants' own later admissions -- that Britain had actively conspired to overthrow their government. This was invoked repeatedly in Soviet propaganda and diplomacy through the 1920s and 1930s. When Britain attempted to build a collective security arrangement against Nazi Germany in the late 1930s, Soviet suspicion of British intentions was rooted in concrete historical experience, of which the Lockhart Plot was a founding episode.

For British intelligence, the affair was a formative failure. The ease with which the Cheka penetrated or manufactured the conspiracy exposed the amateurism of British intelligence operations in Russia. The lessons fed into the professionalization of SIS during the interwar years -- better tradecraft, more institutional control over agents, less reliance on freelance operatives like Reilly. Keith Jeffery's authorized history of MI6 traces a direct line from the Moscow failures of 1918 to the institutional reforms of the 1920s.

For the individuals involved, the consequences varied. Lockhart survived and prospered, becoming a successful author and journalist. His memoirs made him famous. He served in various governmental capacities during World War II and died in 1970, a respected establishment figure. Reilly was less fortunate: he was lured back into the Soviet Union in 1925 by the Trust -- a Cheka/OGPU deception operation -- and was captured and executed. His death validated the pattern the Lockhart Plot had established: British intelligence operatives in Russia were playing a game where the other side held most of the cards. Hill survived the war, continued in intelligence work, and left the most operationally detailed account of the three.

Moura Budberg lived until 1974, moving through literary and intelligence circles in London and Paris. She was companion to Gorky, lover to Wells, friend to countless writers and diplomats, and suspected by MI5 of being a Soviet agent throughout. Whether she was recruited by the Cheka during the Lockhart affair, or whether she simply learned to navigate between worlds with extraordinary skill, remains unresolved. The files, such as they are, do not settle the question.

The Lockhart Plot connects forward to the rest of this series in specific ways. Volume 14, on Reilly, covers the Trust operation that destroyed him -- an operation that built directly on the Cheka's experience of managing the Lockhart conspiracy. Volume 15, on Hill, traces the development of British military intelligence techniques that grew partly from the Moscow failures. Volume 17, on Arthur Ransome, introduces the most paradoxical figure in the constellation -- a children's author who was simultaneously a journalist, a Bolshevik sympathizer, and an SIS agent, and who was in Moscow during the events of 1918. Volume 19, on Persia, and Volume 21, on the 1953 Iranian coup, show British covert operations maturing from the improvisations of 1918 into the professional operations of the Cold War.

The central question of the Lockhart Plot -- was it a genuine conspiracy or a Cheka provocation? -- may never be answered definitively. The archives are incomplete. The participants were liars by profession. The events unfolded in conditions that made clean narratives impossible. What remains is a story about the boundaries of knowledge in intelligence history, about the difficulty of reconstructing truth from the testimony of professional deceivers, and about a moment in 1918 when the relationship between Britain and revolutionary Russia was set on a course of mutual suspicion that would shape the twentieth century.
