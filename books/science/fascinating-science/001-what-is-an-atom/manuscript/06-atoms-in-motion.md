# Atoms in Motion

Atoms are always moving. They never stop. At any temperature above absolute zero, atoms vibrate, rotate, and travel through space. This motion is what we experience as heat.

In a solid, atoms vibrate in place. They are locked in a fixed structure. They shake back and forth, but they do not leave their positions. This is why solids hold their shape. The atoms in a block of ice vibrate, but they stay in a rigid crystal pattern.

In a liquid, atoms move more freely. They slide past each other. They stay close together, but they are not locked in position. This is why liquids flow. The atoms in a glass of water are constantly moving and rearranging, but they stay near each other.

In a gas, atoms move fast and spread apart. They fly in straight lines until they hit another atom or a wall. They bounce off each other and change direction. This is why a gas fills any container you put it in. The atoms in a room full of air are zooming in every direction at hundreds of meters per second.

Temperature is a measure of how fast atoms are moving. Hot means the atoms are moving fast. Cold means the atoms are moving slowly. When you heat water on a stove, you are making the water molecules move faster. When the molecules move fast enough, they escape the liquid and become steam.

Absolute zero is the coldest possible temperature. It is about minus 273 degrees Celsius. At absolute zero, atoms have the minimum possible energy. Nothing can be colder than absolute zero. Scientists have come very close to reaching absolute zero in laboratories, but no one has ever reached it exactly.

Pressure is caused by atoms hitting surfaces. The air in a tire pushes outward because air molecules are constantly bouncing off the inside walls of the tire. More molecules or faster molecules mean more pressure. This is why a tire feels harder when the air is hot.
