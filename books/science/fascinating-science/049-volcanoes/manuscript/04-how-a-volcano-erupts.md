# How a Volcano Erupts

An eruption begins deep underground. Rock in the mantle melts into magma. Magma is less dense than the solid rock around it. Because it is less dense, it rises. It pushes upward through cracks and weak zones in the crust.

Magma collects in underground chambers. A magma chamber is a large pool of molten rock beneath a volcano. The chamber may sit anywhere from a few kilometers to tens of kilometers below the surface. Magma can stay in a chamber for thousands of years before erupting. New magma keeps flowing in from below, building pressure.

Pressure is the key to eruption. Magma contains dissolved gases. The main gases are water vapor, carbon dioxide, and sulfur dioxide. While the magma is deep underground, the weight of the rock above keeps these gases dissolved. As magma rises and the pressure decreases, the gases begin to come out of solution. They form bubbles. This is similar to what happens when you open a bottle of carbonated water. The release of pressure allows dissolved gas to form bubbles.

The bubbles expand as they rise. They make the magma frothy and less dense. This drives the magma upward even faster. Eventually the pressure is too great for the rock above to contain it. The volcano erupts.

The style of eruption depends on the magma's viscosity and gas content. Viscosity is a measure of how easily a liquid flows. Low-viscosity magma is runny. It flows easily. Gas escapes from it gently. Eruptions of low-viscosity magma tend to be effusive. Effusive means the lava flows out rather than exploding. Hawaiian volcanoes erupt this way. Lava pours from the crater in rivers and fountains. A crater is the bowl-shaped depression at the top of a volcano.

High-viscosity magma is thick and sticky. Gas cannot escape easily. Pressure builds until the magma explodes. These eruptions are called explosive eruptions. They send columns of ash, gas, and rock fragments high into the atmosphere. The fragments are called tephra. Tephra includes everything from fine ash to boulders. Mount St. Helens in 1980 was an explosive eruption. The blast leveled forests and sent an ash cloud across multiple states.

Some eruptions produce pyroclastic flows. A pyroclastic flow is a fast-moving current of hot gas, ash, and rock fragments. Pyroclastic flows can travel at speeds exceeding 700 kilometers per hour. Their temperatures can exceed 700 degrees Celsius. They destroy everything in their path. Pyroclastic flows are the deadliest volcanic hazard.

Not every eruption is the same. Some volcanoes erupt continuously for years with gentle lava flows. Others sleep for centuries and then explode with catastrophic force. The behavior depends on the magma. The magma depends on the geology beneath the volcano. Every volcano has its own character.
