# Volcanoes Build the World

Volcanoes are destructive. They also build. Over the long span of geologic time, volcanoes have created much of the world we live in.

Earth's atmosphere exists in part because of volcanoes. Early in Earth's history, volcanoes released enormous quantities of gas. The primary gases were water vapor, carbon dioxide, and nitrogen. The water vapor condensed and eventually formed the oceans. The nitrogen became the dominant gas in the atmosphere. Nitrogen makes up about 78 percent of the air you breathe today. Without volcanic outgassing, Earth would have no atmosphere and no oceans. Outgassing is the release of gases from the interior of a planet.

Volcanoes create new land. Every island in Hawaii was built by volcanic eruptions over a hotspot in the Pacific. Iceland was built by eruptions along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Many islands across the Pacific, Atlantic, and Indian Oceans are volcanic in origin. The ocean floor itself is made of volcanic rock, created at mid-ocean ridges where tectonic plates pull apart.

Volcanic soil is among the most fertile on Earth. When lava and ash break down over time, they release minerals into the soil. These minerals, including potassium, phosphorus, and calcium, are essential nutrients for plants. Regions with volcanic soil support abundant agriculture. The slopes of Mount Etna in Sicily are covered in vineyards and orchards. The island of Java in Indonesia has rich volcanic soil that supports one of the densest populations on Earth.

Volcanoes influence the global climate. Large eruptions inject sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere. The sulfur dioxide converts to sulfuric acid aerosols. An aerosol is a tiny particle suspended in the atmosphere. These aerosols reflect incoming sunlight, cooling the planet. The eruption of Mount Tambora in Indonesia in 1815 caused the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816. Crops failed across Europe and North America. Temperatures dropped by several degrees in some regions.

Over longer timescales, volcanic carbon dioxide emissions have helped regulate Earth's temperature. When volcanic activity increases, more carbon dioxide enters the atmosphere, warming the planet. When it decreases, the planet cools. This volcanic thermostat has operated for billions of years.

Volcanoes provide geothermal energy. Geothermal energy is heat from inside Earth that can be used to generate electricity. In Iceland, geothermal power plants produce about 25 percent of the country's electricity. Hot water from volcanic systems heats homes and greenhouses. Geothermal energy is renewable and produces very low emissions.

Volcanoes also create unique ecosystems. The barren landscape after an eruption does not stay barren long. Pioneer species colonize the new rock. Pioneer species are the first organisms to inhabit a disturbed environment. Lichens break down rock. Mosses take root. Grasses follow. Eventually, forests grow. The process of ecological succession on volcanic land shows how life reclaims what eruptions destroy.

Volcanoes are a paradox. A paradox is something that seems contradictory but is true. They destroy and create. They kill and give life. They are the planet's way of renewing itself. Earth without volcanoes would be a colder, quieter, and less habitable world.
