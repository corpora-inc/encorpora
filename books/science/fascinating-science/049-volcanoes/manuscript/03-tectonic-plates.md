# Tectonic Plates

Earth's outer shell is broken into large pieces called tectonic plates. A tectonic plate is a rigid slab of rock that includes the crust and the upper part of the mantle. Together, the crust and this upper mantle layer form the lithosphere. The lithosphere is Earth's rigid outer shell.

There are about 15 major tectonic plates and several smaller ones. The Pacific Plate is the largest. The North American Plate, the Eurasian Plate, and the African Plate are others. These plates are not stationary. They move. They slide, collide, and pull apart. The movement is slow. Most plates move between 2 and 10 centimeters per year. That is roughly the speed your fingernails grow.

The plates move because of convection currents in the mantle beneath them. Hot rock rises from deep in the mantle. It spreads sideways beneath the lithosphere. It drags the plates along. Then it cools and sinks back down. This cycle repeats continuously.

Most volcanoes form at the boundaries where plates meet. There are three types of plate boundaries. Each type produces different volcanic activity.

At divergent boundaries, plates pull apart. Divergent means moving away from each other. Magma rises from the mantle to fill the gap. This creates new crust. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a divergent boundary. It runs down the center of the Atlantic Ocean. Iceland sits on this ridge. Iceland has many active volcanoes because magma constantly rises to the surface there.

At convergent boundaries, plates push together. Convergent means moving toward each other. When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate slides beneath the lighter continental plate. This process is called subduction. The subducting plate descends into the mantle, where intense heat melts it. The melted rock rises as magma and feeds volcanoes above. The Andes Mountains in South America formed this way. The Cascade Range in the northwestern United States formed this way too.

At transform boundaries, plates slide past each other horizontally. Transform boundaries produce earthquakes, but they do not typically produce volcanoes. The San Andreas Fault in California is a transform boundary.

Some volcanoes form far from plate boundaries. These are called hotspot volcanoes. A hotspot is a location where a plume of exceptionally hot rock rises from deep in the mantle. The plume melts through the plate above it. The Hawaiian Islands formed over a hotspot. The Pacific Plate moves over the stationary hotspot. Each island formed as the plate carried it past the plume. The oldest islands are farthest from the hotspot. The youngest island, Hawaii, sits directly over it today.

Tectonic plates are the reason volcanoes exist where they do. The map of volcanoes is the map of plate boundaries, with hotspots scattered in between.
