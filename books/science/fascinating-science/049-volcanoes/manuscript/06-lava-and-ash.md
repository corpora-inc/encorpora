# Lava and Ash

When a volcano erupts, it releases material in three forms: liquid, solid, and gas. Each form behaves differently and creates different hazards and landscapes.

Lava is the liquid form. It is magma that has reached the surface. Lava temperatures typically range from 700 to 1,200 degrees Celsius. The temperature determines the color. The hottest lava glows white or yellow. Slightly cooler lava glows orange. As it cools further, it turns red, then dark.

There are different types of lava flows. Pahoehoe is smooth and ropy. The word is Hawaiian. Pahoehoe forms when fluid lava flows slowly and its surface cools into a smooth, folded skin. Aa is rough and jagged. Aa forms when lava flows faster and its surface breaks into sharp, irregular chunks. Pillow lava forms when lava erupts underwater. The water cools the outer surface instantly, forming rounded, pillow-shaped blobs.

Lava flows destroy everything they cover. They bury roads, buildings, and farmland. But lava flows are usually slow enough for people to evacuate. They rarely kill directly. The greater danger comes from what the volcano throws into the air.

Ash is the most widespread volcanic product. Volcanic ash is not like the ash from a fire. It is made of tiny fragments of pulverized rock and glass. Pulverized means broken into very fine pieces. The fragments are sharp and hard. A large eruption can send ash high into the stratosphere. The stratosphere is the layer of atmosphere above the weather zone. Ash in the stratosphere can travel around the world.

Falling ash buries landscapes. A few centimeters of ash can collapse roofs under its weight. Ash clogs engines, contaminates water supplies, and damages lungs. Fine ash particles can stay airborne for days or weeks.

Larger fragments thrown from a volcano are called volcanic bombs. A volcanic bomb is a mass of molten rock that solidifies as it flies through the air. Some are the size of a fist. Some are the size of a car. They land with tremendous force near the volcano.

Volcanic gas is the invisible product of eruption. The most abundant gas is water vapor. Sulfur dioxide is another common volcanic gas. When sulfur dioxide reaches the upper atmosphere, it combines with water to form sulfuric acid droplets. These droplets reflect sunlight back into space. A large eruption can cool global temperatures for months or years. The 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines lowered average global temperatures by about 0.5 degrees Celsius for nearly two years.

Carbon dioxide is also released. In rare cases, carbon dioxide collects in low-lying areas because it is heavier than air. In 1986, a cloud of carbon dioxide erupted from Lake Nyos in Cameroon. The gas flowed downhill and suffocated more than 1,700 people.

The materials a volcano produces are dangerous. They are also the raw ingredients of new land, new soil, and new atmosphere. Destruction and creation emerge from the same eruption.
