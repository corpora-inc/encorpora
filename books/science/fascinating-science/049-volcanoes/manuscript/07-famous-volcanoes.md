# Famous Volcanoes

Certain eruptions have changed the course of history. They have destroyed cities, altered climates, and reshaped maps. Each famous eruption teaches something about the power beneath Earth's surface.

Mount Vesuvius stands near the city of Naples in southern Italy. In 79 CE, it erupted without warning. A column of ash and pumice rose high into the sky. Pumice is a lightweight volcanic rock full of gas bubbles. Ash rained down on the Roman cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum. Then pyroclastic flows swept through. The flows buried both cities under meters of hot ash and rock. Thousands of people died. The cities were lost for nearly 1,700 years. When archaeologists excavated Pompeii, they found buildings, streets, and even food preserved in ash. Vesuvius is still active. More than three million people live in its shadow today.

Krakatoa is a volcanic island in Indonesia. In 1883, it produced one of the loudest eruptions in recorded history. The sound was heard nearly 5,000 kilometers away. The eruption destroyed most of the island. It generated tsunamis that killed more than 36,000 people. A tsunami is a large ocean wave caused by an underwater disturbance. Ash circled the globe and produced vivid red sunsets for months. Global temperatures dropped measurably. A new volcanic island, Anak Krakatoa, has been growing from the same location since 1927. Anak means child. Krakatoa's child is still erupting.

Mount St. Helens is in Washington state in the United States. On May 18, 1980, an earthquake triggered a massive landslide on its north face. The landslide was the largest recorded in history. It released the pressure on the magma inside. The volcano exploded sideways. The lateral blast flattened 600 square kilometers of forest. Ash rose 24 kilometers into the atmosphere. Fifty-seven people died. The eruption reduced the mountain's height by about 400 meters. Today, scientists monitor Mount St. Helens constantly. A new lava dome grows slowly inside the crater.

Mount Pinatubo is in the Philippines. In June 1991, it erupted after 600 years of dormancy. Dormancy means a period of inactivity. The eruption ejected about 10 cubic kilometers of material. It was the second largest eruption of the twentieth century. The sulfur dioxide it released formed a global haze that cooled the planet by about 0.5 degrees Celsius for two years.

Eyjafjallajokull is a volcano in Iceland. In 2010, it erupted beneath a glacier. The interaction of lava and ice produced enormous clouds of fine ash. The ash cloud shut down air travel across Europe for six days. More than 100,000 flights were canceled. The eruption was small by volcanic standards. But its location and timing demonstrated how even a modest eruption can disrupt modern civilization.

These eruptions remind us that volcanoes operate on their own schedule. They do not follow human plans. They can sleep for centuries and wake without notice.
