# How Clouds Build Charge

Lightning begins inside a thundercloud. A thundercloud is a tall, dense cloud called a cumulonimbus. Cumulonimbus clouds can tower more than 40,000 feet into the sky. Inside these clouds, a process of charge separation takes place. Charge separation means positive and negative charges move to different locations.

A thundercloud contains water droplets, ice crystals, and hail. Hail is a ball of ice. These particles are constantly moving inside the cloud. Strong updrafts push them upward. An updraft is a current of rising air. Gravity pulls them back down. As particles collide, electrons transfer from one particle to another.

The exact mechanism of charge separation is still studied by scientists. The most accepted explanation is the ice-crystal mechanism. When small ice crystals collide with larger hail pellets, electrons transfer from the ice crystals to the hail. The ice crystals become positively charged. The hail becomes negatively charged.

Updrafts carry the light, positively charged ice crystals to the top of the cloud. The heavier, negatively charged hail sinks to the bottom. This creates a charge structure inside the cloud. The top of the cloud becomes mostly positive. The bottom of the cloud becomes mostly negative. A small pocket of positive charge often exists at the very base of the cloud.

The negative charge at the base of the cloud affects the ground below. Negative charges in the cloud repel negative charges on the ground surface. The ground directly below the cloud becomes positively charged. This is called electrostatic induction. Induction is the process of creating a charge in an object without touching it.

As the charge separation grows, the voltage between the top and bottom of the cloud increases. The voltage between the cloud base and the ground also increases. Air is normally an insulator. An insulator is a material that does not conduct electricity easily. But when the voltage becomes high enough, even air can be forced to conduct. The air molecules break apart into ions. This process is called dielectric breakdown.

The charge buildup inside a thundercloud is enormous. A single thundercloud can contain the energy equivalent of a small nuclear weapon. This energy builds over minutes as updrafts and collisions continue to separate charges. The cloud becomes an enormous natural battery, storing electrical energy until the voltage is high enough to overcome the insulating properties of air.

When the voltage reaches about three million volts per meter of air, the air breaks down. Electricity flows. Lightning strikes. The charge that took minutes to build discharges in a fraction of a second.
