# The Strike

A lightning strike is not a single event. It is a complex sequence of steps that happens in a fraction of a second. The process involves invisible leaders, return strokes, and multiple pulses of current. Current is the flow of electric charge.

The strike begins with a stepped leader. A stepped leader is an invisible channel of ionized air that extends from the cloud toward the ground. Ionized means the air molecules have lost electrons and become electrically conductive. The leader moves in steps, each about 150 feet long. It branches as it moves, searching for the easiest path to the ground. The stepped leader carries negative charge from the cloud.

As the stepped leader approaches the ground, the positive charge on the ground surface intensifies. Positive streamers rise from tall objects on the ground. A streamer is a short channel of ionized air that reaches upward. Streamers rise from trees, buildings, towers, and even people. The streamers reach toward the descending leader.

When a streamer connects with the stepped leader, a complete circuit forms between the cloud and the ground. A circuit is a closed path through which electricity can flow. This connection point is usually about 100 to 300 feet above the ground. The moment of connection is called the attachment point.

Once the circuit is complete, the return stroke begins. The return stroke is the bright flash you see. It travels from the ground up to the cloud at about one-third the speed of light. That is roughly 62,000 miles per second. The return stroke carries enormous current — typically about 30,000 amperes. An ampere is a unit of electric current. A household circuit carries about 15 amperes.

The return stroke heats the air in the channel to about 54,000 degrees Fahrenheit. This extreme heat causes the air to expand explosively. The rapid expansion creates a shock wave. A shock wave is a wave of pressure that moves faster than the speed of sound. This shock wave is what we hear as thunder.

Most lightning flashes contain multiple strokes. After the first return stroke, additional leaders called dart leaders may travel down the same channel. Each dart leader triggers another return stroke. A typical flash contains three to five strokes. These multiple strokes are why lightning appears to flicker. The entire sequence of strokes takes less than half a second.

The peak power of a lightning return stroke can exceed one trillion watts. A watt is a unit of power. One trillion watts is more power than all the electrical generators in the United States combined. But this power lasts only for a few millionths of a second, so the total energy in a single bolt is modest — roughly enough to power a light bulb for a few months.
