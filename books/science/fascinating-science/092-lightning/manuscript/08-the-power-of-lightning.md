# The Power of Lightning

Lightning is one of the most powerful electrical events on Earth. Each bolt carries enormous energy, voltage, and current. Understanding the numbers behind lightning reveals just how extraordinary it is.

A typical lightning bolt has a voltage of about 300 million volts. A volt is a unit of electrical potential. A standard wall outlet in the United States provides 120 volts. Lightning provides about 2.5 million times more voltage than a wall outlet. This enormous voltage is what forces electricity through air, which normally does not conduct.

The current in a lightning bolt averages about 30,000 amperes. Some powerful bolts carry over 200,000 amperes. An ampere is a measure of how much charge flows per second. A smartphone charger uses about one ampere. Lightning uses tens of thousands of times more.

The peak power of a lightning bolt can reach one trillion watts. A watt is a measure of energy transferred per second. One trillion watts is called a terawatt. The entire United States uses about one terawatt of electrical power at any given moment. For a brief instant, a single lightning bolt matches the power output of an entire nation.

But power and energy are different. Energy is power multiplied by time. A lightning bolt delivers its power for only a tiny fraction of a second. The total energy in a typical bolt is about one to five billion joules. A joule is a unit of energy. This is roughly the energy in 50 liters of gasoline. It sounds like a lot, but it is delivered in microseconds. A microsecond is one millionth of a second.

Could we harvest lightning for electricity? In theory, yes. In practice, it is not feasible. Lightning is unpredictable. You cannot control where or when it strikes. The energy arrives in an incredibly short burst. No existing technology can capture and store energy that arrives so quickly. The total energy in a single bolt could power one household for about a week. But the cost of building a capture system would far exceed the value of the energy collected.

Lightning influences technology in important ways. It can damage power lines, electronic equipment, and communication systems. A direct strike on a power line can send a surge of electricity into homes and businesses. A surge is a sudden spike in voltage. Surge protectors are devices that block these spikes. They divert excess voltage safely into the ground.

Lightning also affects aviation. Airplanes are struck by lightning about once per year on average. Modern aircraft are designed to handle lightning strikes. The aluminum skin conducts electricity around the outside of the plane. Passengers inside are safe. But lightning can still damage sensors and navigation equipment.

Scientists study lightning using high-speed cameras, rocket-triggered experiments, and satellite observations. Rocket-triggered lightning is a technique where a small rocket trailing a wire is launched into a thundercloud. The wire provides a path for lightning to follow. This allows scientists to study lightning up close in a controlled way.

Lightning is a spectacular display of nature's electrical power. It pushes the limits of voltage, current, and temperature. It is brief but immense. Every flash reminds us that the atmosphere is an active electrical system, constantly charging, discharging, and reshaping the world below.
