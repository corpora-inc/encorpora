# Lightning and Life

Lightning is dangerous. It kills about 2,000 people worldwide every year. It injures thousands more. Understanding lightning safety can save lives.

The safest place during a thunderstorm is inside a large building or a hard-topped vehicle. A car is safe not because of its rubber tires but because of its metal body. If lightning strikes a car, the electric current flows through the metal exterior and into the ground. This is called a Faraday cage effect. A Faraday cage is an enclosure made of conducting material that blocks electric fields.

If you are caught outside during a thunderstorm, avoid tall objects. Do not stand under a lone tree. Lightning tends to strike the tallest object in an area. Stay away from water. Water conducts electricity. Stay away from metal fences and poles. If you feel your hair stand up, it may mean a positive streamer is rising from your body. This is extremely dangerous. Crouch low immediately with your feet together. Do not lie flat on the ground.

Lightning rods protect buildings. Benjamin Franklin invented the lightning rod in the 1750s. A lightning rod is a metal pole mounted on the top of a building. It connects to a wire that runs down the building and into the ground. When lightning strikes, it hits the rod instead of the building. The current flows through the wire safely into the ground. Lightning rods do not attract lightning from far away. They provide a safe path for lightning that would have struck nearby anyway.

Lightning plays a role in nature beyond destruction. It helps fertilize the soil. When lightning passes through the atmosphere, it breaks apart nitrogen molecules. Nitrogen is a gas that makes up 78 percent of Earth's atmosphere. Nitrogen molecules normally do not react with other substances easily. But lightning splits them apart. The nitrogen atoms combine with oxygen to form nitrogen oxides. Rain washes these nitrogen oxides into the soil. Plants need nitrogen to grow. Lightning produces about 13,000 tons of nitrogen fertilizer per year worldwide.

Some scientists believe lightning may have helped start life on Earth. Billions of years ago, Earth's atmosphere was very different from today. It contained gases like methane, ammonia, and hydrogen. In 1953, scientists Stanley Miller and Harold Urey conducted an experiment. They sent electric sparks through a mixture of these gases. The sparks produced amino acids. Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins. Proteins are essential for all life. This experiment suggested that lightning could have helped create the first organic molecules on early Earth.

Lightning also starts wildfires. In dry regions, lightning is the leading natural cause of forest fires. These fires can be destructive, but they also serve an ecological purpose. Many ecosystems depend on periodic fires to clear dead material and allow new growth. Some pine trees only release their seeds after a fire.

Lightning is both a threat and a gift to life on Earth. It destroys and creates. It kills and fertilizes. It is a force that shapes ecosystems and may have even helped spark the very first living things.
