# A Lifetime of Beats

The average human heart beats about 2.5 billion times in a lifetime. That number is staggering. No mechanical pump in the world matches this reliability.

In infancy, the heart beats fast. A newborn's heart rate is 120 to 160 beats per minute. Babies are small. Their hearts are small. Small hearts must beat faster to pump enough blood. As a child grows, the heart grows too. The heart rate gradually slows.

By adulthood, the resting heart rate settles to 60 to 100 beats per minute. This rate stays fairly stable for decades. In old age, the heart may slow slightly. The heart muscle stiffens over time. The valves may thicken. The electrical system may become less reliable. But the heart keeps beating.

Heart disease can disrupt this rhythm. Coronary artery disease is the most common form. Coronary arteries are the blood vessels that supply the heart muscle itself. The heart needs its own blood supply to function. Coronary artery disease happens when fatty deposits called plaques build up inside these arteries. Plaques narrow the arteries. Less blood reaches the heart muscle.

If a plaque ruptures, a blood clot can form. A blood clot is a solid lump of blood. If the clot blocks a coronary artery completely, part of the heart muscle is starved of oxygen. That muscle tissue begins to die. This is a heart attack. The medical term is myocardial infarction. Myocardial means "related to the heart muscle." Infarction means "tissue death from lack of blood."

Quick treatment can save heart muscle during a heart attack. Doctors may insert a stent. A stent is a tiny mesh tube. It holds the artery open. Doctors may also perform bypass surgery. They take a healthy blood vessel from another part of the body and use it to reroute blood around the blockage.

Heart failure is a different condition. In heart failure, the heart is too weak to pump enough blood to meet the body's needs. It does not mean the heart has stopped. It means the heart cannot keep up. Fluid may build up in the lungs and legs.

For some patients, the only option is a heart transplant. A heart transplant replaces a failing heart with a healthy heart from a donor. The first successful human heart transplant was performed in 1967 by Dr. Christiaan Barnard in South Africa. Today, thousands of heart transplants are performed each year.

Science has also created artificial hearts. These mechanical devices can keep blood flowing while a patient waits for a transplant. They are not permanent replacements yet. But research continues.

Your heart is remarkable. It works without rest. It adapts to your needs. It responds to joy, fear, exercise, and sleep. It is the engine of your life. Take care of it, and it will take care of you.
