# The Ocean Floor

The ocean floor is not flat. It has a landscape as dramatic as anything on land. Mountains, valleys, plains, and ridges stretch across the bottom of the sea. Most of this landscape exists in total darkness.

The edge of every continent extends underwater before dropping into the deep ocean. This underwater edge is called the continental shelf. The continental shelf is the gently sloping land beneath shallow coastal water. It can extend hundreds of kilometers from shore. The water over the continental shelf is relatively shallow, usually less than 200 meters deep. Most ocean life lives here. Most fishing happens here.

At the outer edge of the continental shelf, the ground drops steeply. This steep slope is the continental slope. It descends from about 200 meters to about 4,000 meters. The continental slope marks the true edge of the continent.

At the base of the continental slope, sediment collects in a gentle incline called the continental rise. Sediment is loose material like sand, mud, and the remains of dead organisms. The continental rise transitions into the abyssal plain.

The abyssal plain is the flat, deep floor of the ocean. Abyssal means relating to the deepest parts of the ocean. These plains are among the flattest places on Earth. They sit at depths between 3,000 and 6,000 meters. A thick layer of sediment covers the rock beneath. The sediment accumulates very slowly. One centimeter can take a thousand years to build up.

Running through the ocean floor is the mid-ocean ridge. A ridge is a long, elevated chain of underwater mountains. The mid-ocean ridge system is the longest mountain range on Earth. It stretches more than 65,000 kilometers. It winds through every ocean basin. At the center of the ridge, tectonic plates pull apart. Tectonic plates are the large pieces of Earth's outer shell. Magma rises to fill the gap. Magma is molten rock from deep inside Earth. When magma cools at the ridge, it forms new ocean floor. The ocean floor is constantly being created.

The deepest parts of the ocean are the trenches. A trench forms where one tectonic plate slides beneath another. This process is called subduction. The Mariana Trench in the western Pacific is the deepest. Its floor lies nearly 11,000 meters below the surface. The pressure at that depth is crushing. Pressure is the force exerted by the weight of water above. At the bottom of the Mariana Trench, the pressure is more than 1,000 times the pressure at the surface.

Scattered across the ocean floor are seamounts. A seamount is an underwater mountain that does not reach the surface. There are tens of thousands of seamounts. Some are extinct volcanoes. If a seamount rises above the water, it becomes an island. The Hawaiian Islands formed this way.
