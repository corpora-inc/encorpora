# Zones of the Ocean

Scientists divide the ocean into zones based on depth. Each zone has different conditions. The conditions include light, temperature, and pressure. Life adapts differently in each zone.

The sunlight zone is the top layer. It extends from the surface to about 200 meters deep. Sunlight penetrates this zone. Penetrate means to pass through. This is where photosynthesis happens. Photosynthesis is the process by which organisms use sunlight to make food from carbon dioxide and water. Phytoplankton live here. Phytoplankton are tiny, plant-like organisms that drift in the water. They produce more than half of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere. Most familiar ocean animals live in this zone. Fish, dolphins, sea turtles, and sharks all depend on the sunlight zone.

Below the sunlight zone is the twilight zone. It extends from 200 meters to about 1,000 meters. Some light reaches this zone, but not enough for photosynthesis. The water is dimmer and cooler. Many animals here migrate vertically each day. They rise to the sunlight zone at night to feed. They descend during the day to hide from predators. This daily migration is the largest movement of animals on Earth.

The midnight zone extends from 1,000 to 4,000 meters. No sunlight reaches this zone. The water is cold, usually just above freezing. Pressure is intense. Many organisms in this zone produce their own light. This ability is called bioluminescence. Bioluminescence is the production of light by a living organism. Anglerfish use a glowing lure to attract prey. Jellyfish flash light to confuse predators. In the midnight zone, light is a tool for survival.

The abyssal zone extends from 4,000 to 6,000 meters. The temperature hovers near freezing. The pressure is enormous. Food is scarce. Most nutrition comes from marine snow. Marine snow is a constant rain of dead organisms, waste, and debris drifting down from above. Animals here tend to be small. They move slowly. They conserve energy because food is rare.

The hadal zone is the deepest zone. It exists only in ocean trenches. It extends from 6,000 meters to the bottom, nearly 11,000 meters in the deepest places. The name comes from Hades, the Greek underworld. Conditions are extreme. The pressure can crush most submarines. Yet life exists here. Scientists have found shrimp-like amphipods, sea cucumbers, and microbes in the hadal zone. These organisms have adapted to pressures that would destroy most life forms.

Each zone is a distinct world. The ocean is not one environment. It is many environments stacked on top of each other. A journey from the surface to the bottom of a trench passes through more changes in conditions than a journey from the equator to a pole on land.
