# The Ocean We Have Not Seen

We have explored more of the moon's surface than the ocean floor. We have mapped distant planets in greater detail than our own seabed. The ocean remains Earth's greatest frontier.

About 80 percent of the ocean floor has never been mapped in high resolution. High resolution means detailed and precise. Satellites can estimate the general shape of the seafloor using gravity measurements. But detailed mapping requires sonar. Sonar is a technology that sends sound waves into the water and listens for the echo. The time it takes for the echo to return reveals the depth. Mapping the entire ocean floor with sonar would take decades with current technology.

The deep ocean is hostile to human visitors. The pressure at great depths crushes most equipment. At 4,000 meters, the pressure is 400 times what we feel at the surface. At 10,000 meters, it exceeds 1,000 times surface pressure. Building vehicles that survive these conditions is expensive and difficult.

Only three people have reached the bottom of the Challenger Deep. In 1960, Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh descended in a vessel called the Trieste. Their dive took nearly five hours. They spent only 20 minutes on the bottom. In 2012, filmmaker James Cameron made the descent alone in a specially built submersible. A submersible is a small vehicle designed to operate underwater. Each dive was a monumental engineering achievement.

Remotely operated vehicles, called ROVs, explore where humans cannot easily go. An ROV is an unmanned vehicle controlled from the surface through a cable. ROVs carry cameras, sensors, and robotic arms. They have discovered hydrothermal vents, new species, and shipwrecks. Autonomous underwater vehicles, called AUVs, can operate without a cable. They follow pre-programmed routes and collect data independently.

Every expedition to the deep ocean discovers something new. Scientists have found organisms that thrive in boiling water near hydrothermal vents. They have found bacteria living inside rocks beneath the seafloor. They have found fish at depths once thought to be lifeless. The census of ocean life is far from complete. Scientists estimate that millions of marine species remain undiscovered.

The ocean holds information about Earth's history. Sediment layers on the ocean floor record millions of years of climate changes. Sediment cores, which are long cylinders of material drilled from the seafloor, reveal ancient temperatures, volcanic eruptions, and the rise and fall of ice ages.

The ocean also holds practical resources. Mineral deposits on the abyssal plain contain manganese, cobalt, and rare earth elements. Hydrothermal vents create deposits rich in copper, zinc, and gold. Whether to mine these deposits is a difficult question. Mining could damage ecosystems that we barely understand.

The ocean we have not seen is vast. It is dark and cold and under immense pressure. It is also full of life, history, and resources we are only beginning to discover. The greatest exploration on Earth is not behind us. It is below us.
