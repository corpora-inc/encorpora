# Currents

The ocean is always moving. Water flows in continuous streams called currents. A current is a large body of water moving in a specific direction. Some currents flow at the surface. Others flow deep below. Together, they form a global circulation system that moves heat, nutrients, and organisms around the planet.

Surface currents are driven by wind. The wind pushes the top layer of the ocean. The Coriolis effect bends these currents. The Coriolis effect is the deflection of moving objects caused by Earth's rotation. In the Northern Hemisphere, currents curve to the right. In the Southern Hemisphere, they curve to the left. This creates large circular patterns called gyres. A gyre is a system of rotating currents that forms a loop.

There are five major gyres. The North Atlantic Gyre includes the Gulf Stream. The Gulf Stream is a powerful, warm current that flows from the Gulf of Mexico up the east coast of North America and across the Atlantic toward Europe. It carries warm water northward. This warmth helps keep Western Europe's climate milder than it would otherwise be. Without the Gulf Stream, winters in London and Paris would be much colder.

The ocean also has deep currents. Deep currents are not driven by wind. They are driven by differences in water density. Cold water is denser than warm water. Salty water is denser than less salty water. When surface water near the poles gets very cold and salty, it becomes dense and sinks. This sinking water flows along the ocean floor toward the equator. Warmer water flows at the surface to replace it. This global pattern is called thermohaline circulation. Thermo means heat. Haline means salt.

Thermohaline circulation works like a giant conveyor belt. Water sinks in the North Atlantic near Greenland. It flows south along the ocean floor. It travels around Antarctica. It rises in the Indian and Pacific Oceans. It flows back along the surface toward the Atlantic. One complete loop takes about 1,000 years.

Upwelling is another important type of current. Upwelling happens when wind pushes surface water away from a coast. Deep water rises to replace it. This deep water is cold and rich in nutrients. Nutrients are substances that organisms need to grow. Upwelling zones are among the most productive areas in the ocean. They support large populations of fish.

Currents also transport floating objects, including plastic waste. Ocean currents concentrate debris in the centers of gyres. The Great Pacific Garbage Patch is a zone of accumulated plastic in the North Pacific Gyre. It exists because currents carry plastic to the gyre's center and trap it there.

Currents connect every part of the ocean. They make the ocean a single, dynamic system. A change in one region affects regions thousands of kilometers away.
