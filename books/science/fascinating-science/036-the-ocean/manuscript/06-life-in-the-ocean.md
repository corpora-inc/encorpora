# Life in the Ocean

Life began in the ocean. The oldest fossils of living organisms come from the sea. For billions of years, the ocean was the only place life existed. Today, the ocean contains an astonishing variety of life.

At the base of the ocean food web are phytoplankton. Phytoplankton are microscopic organisms that use sunlight to produce energy through photosynthesis. They are so small that millions can fit in a single liter of water. Despite their size, they produce roughly half of the oxygen in the atmosphere. They also form the foundation of the marine food web. A food web is the network of feeding relationships among organisms.

Zooplankton eat phytoplankton. Zooplankton are tiny animals that drift in the water. Some are the larvae of larger animals. A larva is an early, immature form of an organism. Others, like krill, spend their entire lives as plankton. Krill are small, shrimp-like creatures. They exist in enormous numbers. A single swarm of krill can contain millions of individuals.

Small fish eat zooplankton. Larger fish eat the small fish. Sharks, tuna, and swordfish sit near the top of the food web. Marine mammals like dolphins, seals, and whales also feed on fish and other ocean animals. The blue whale is the largest animal that has ever lived on Earth. It can reach 30 meters in length. Despite its size, it feeds almost entirely on krill.

The ocean floor supports a different kind of life. Organisms called benthos live on or in the seafloor. Benthos include crabs, sea stars, worms, and clams. In the deep ocean, communities of organisms cluster around hydrothermal vents. These vent communities do not depend on sunlight. Instead, bacteria at the vents use chemicals from the hot water to produce energy. This process is called chemosynthesis. Chemosynthesis is the production of energy from chemical reactions rather than sunlight. Giant tube worms, blind shrimp, and pale crabs thrive near these vents.

Coral reefs are among the most diverse ecosystems in the ocean. An ecosystem is a community of organisms and their physical environment. Coral reefs are built by tiny animals called coral polyps. A polyp is a small, soft-bodied organism. Polyps secrete calcium carbonate, which forms a hard skeleton. Over thousands of years, these skeletons build up into reefs. Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, but they support about 25 percent of all marine species.

The open ocean, far from shore and far from the bottom, is the largest habitat on Earth. It appears empty, but it is not. Jellyfish, squid, and fish travel through this vast space. Many organisms here are transparent. Transparency is a form of camouflage. In open water with nowhere to hide, being invisible is the best defense.

Every part of the ocean holds life. Life adapts to crushing pressure, total darkness, extreme cold, and boiling heat. The ocean proves that life finds a way in almost any condition.
