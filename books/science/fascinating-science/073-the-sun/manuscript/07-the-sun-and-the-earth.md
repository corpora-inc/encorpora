# The Sun and the Earth

The Sun and Earth have a relationship that makes life possible. The Sun provides energy. Earth is at just the right distance to use that energy without being destroyed by it.

Earth orbits the Sun at an average distance of about 93 million miles. This distance is called one astronomical unit. Earth's orbit is not a perfect circle. It is an ellipse. An ellipse is a slightly flattened circle. Earth is a little closer to the Sun in January and a little farther in July. This small difference does not cause the seasons.

The seasons happen because Earth is tilted. Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees from vertical. An axis is an imaginary line through the center of a spinning object. When the Northern Hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, it receives more direct sunlight. This is summer in the north. When the Northern Hemisphere tilts away from the Sun, it receives less direct sunlight. This is winter in the north. The Southern Hemisphere has opposite seasons.

The Sun heats Earth's surface unevenly. The equator gets more direct sunlight than the poles. This uneven heating drives global weather patterns. Warm air rises at the equator and moves toward the poles. Cool air sinks at the poles and moves toward the equator. These air movements create winds. Winds drive ocean currents. Ocean currents move heat around the planet.

The Sun also drives the water cycle. It heats water in oceans, lakes, and rivers. Some of that water evaporates. Evaporation is the process of liquid turning into gas. Water vapor rises into the atmosphere and forms clouds. Water falls back to the ground as rain or snow. This cycle provides fresh water to every ecosystem on Earth. An ecosystem is a community of living things and their environment.

Solar wind constantly flows from the Sun toward Earth. The solar wind is a stream of charged particles that escapes the Sun's corona. These particles travel at about one million miles per hour. Earth's magnetic field deflects most of the solar wind. Deflect means to push aside. Without this magnetic shield, the solar wind could strip away Earth's atmosphere.

When the solar wind interacts with Earth's magnetic field, it can create auroras. An aurora is a display of colored light in the sky near the poles. The aurora borealis appears near the North Pole. The aurora australis appears near the South Pole. Charged particles from the Sun excite gases in Earth's atmosphere. The gases glow green, red, purple, and blue.

Sometimes the Sun sends bursts of plasma toward Earth. These are called coronal mass ejections. A coronal mass ejection is a huge cloud of charged particles. If one hits Earth, it can disrupt satellites, power grids, and communications. Scientists monitor the Sun to predict these events.

The Sun and Earth are partners in a cosmic dance. The Sun gives energy. Earth transforms that energy into wind, rain, warmth, and life.
