# Decomposers

Decomposers are organisms that break down dead material. They feed on dead plants, dead animals, and waste products. Decomposers are essential to every ecosystem. Without them, dead material would pile up and nutrients would be locked away forever.

Fungi are among the most important decomposers. A fungus is an organism that absorbs nutrients from dead or decaying material. Mushrooms are the visible part of some fungi. The main body of a fungus is a network of tiny threads called hyphae. Hyphae spread through soil, wood, and dead leaves. They release enzymes that break down complex molecules into simpler ones. An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction. The fungus then absorbs the simple molecules as food.

Bacteria are the other major group of decomposers. Bacteria are single-celled organisms. They are found everywhere — in soil, water, air, and inside other organisms. Many types of bacteria specialize in breaking down specific materials. Some break down proteins. Others break down fats. Still others break down cellulose, which is the tough material in plant cell walls.

Decomposers recycle nutrients. When a tree falls in the forest, decomposers go to work. Fungi and bacteria break down the wood, bark, and leaves. They release carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and other elements back into the soil. Carbon is an element found in all living things. Nitrogen is an element that plants need for growth. Phosphorus is an element important for energy transfer in cells. These nutrients become available for plants to absorb through their roots. The nutrients that were locked in the dead tree return to the food chain.

The process of decomposition releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. Carbon dioxide is a gas made of one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms. Plants absorb carbon dioxide during photosynthesis. This creates a cycle. Plants take in carbon dioxide and build it into their bodies. Decomposers break down dead plants and release carbon dioxide. The carbon moves in a circle from air to plants to decomposers and back to the air. This is part of the carbon cycle.

Decomposers also break down animal waste. Animal droppings contain undigested food and nutrients. Dung beetles, earthworms, and bacteria break down this waste. They return the nutrients to the soil. Without this process, soils would become depleted. Depleted means used up or emptied.

Earthworms deserve special mention. An earthworm eats soil and dead plant material. It digests some of the material and excretes the rest. Excrete means to release waste from the body. Earthworm excretions, called castings, are rich in nutrients. Castings improve soil quality. Charles Darwin spent years studying earthworms. He concluded that earthworms are among the most important organisms on Earth because of their role in maintaining healthy soil.

Decomposers are often overlooked because they are small or hidden underground. But they are just as important as producers and consumers. They close the loop in the food chain. Without decomposers, nutrients would not recycle. Plants would run out of raw materials. Food chains would collapse. Decomposers keep the system running.
