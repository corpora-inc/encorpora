# What Happens When a Link Breaks

A food chain is only as strong as its weakest link. When one species in a food chain disappears or declines sharply, the effects ripple through the entire ecosystem. Scientists call this a trophic cascade. A cascade is a chain of events where one thing causes the next.

When a predator disappears, the animals it used to eat increase in number. Their population grows because nothing is controlling it. These animals then eat more of the organisms below them. The organisms below decline. This is called a top-down cascade because the effect starts at the top and moves downward.

Yellowstone National Park provides a famous example. Wolves were removed from Yellowstone in the 1920s. Without wolves, elk populations grew rapidly. Elk are large herbivores. The elk overgrazed the land. They ate young willow and aspen trees before the trees could grow. Without trees along riverbanks, the banks eroded. Erode means to wear away. The rivers became wider and shallower. Beavers lost their food supply and disappeared from the park.

In 1995, wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone. The wolves hunted elk. Elk populations decreased. Elk also changed their behavior. They avoided open areas near rivers where wolves could easily catch them. Young trees began to grow again along the riverbanks. The roots of these trees stabilized the soil. The rivers narrowed. Beavers returned and built dams. The dams created ponds that attracted fish, frogs, and birds. The entire ecosystem recovered because one predator was restored.

When a producer disappears, everything above it suffers. If a disease kills a major plant species, the herbivores that depend on it lose their food source. They decline in number. The predators that eat those herbivores also decline. This is called a bottom-up cascade because the effect starts at the base.

Human activities break links in food chains every day. Overfishing removes predators from ocean food webs. Deforestation destroys producers. Deforestation is the clearing of forests. Pollution kills organisms at every level. Pesticides are chemicals used to kill pests. Pesticides can kill insects that are important food sources for birds and frogs. When the insects decline, the birds and frogs decline too.

Invasive species can also break food chains. An invasive species is an organism that is introduced to a new environment where it does not naturally belong. Invasive species often have no predators in their new environment. They compete with native species for food and space. They can drive native species to extinction. Extinction means the complete disappearance of a species.

The introduction of the brown tree snake to Guam is a devastating example. This snake arrived on the island accidentally in the 1950s. Guam had no native snakes and no predators that could control the brown tree snake. The snake ate birds, lizards, and small mammals. It drove most of Guam's native bird species to extinction. Without birds to eat insects and spread seeds, the entire ecosystem changed.

Broken links in food chains show how interconnected life is. Removing one species can transform an entire landscape. Protecting food chains means protecting every link, from the smallest producer to the largest predator.
