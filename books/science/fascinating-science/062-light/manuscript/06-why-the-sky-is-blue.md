# Why the Sky Is Blue

The sky is blue because of the way sunlight interacts with the atmosphere. The answer involves scattering, wavelength, and the composition of the air above us.

Sunlight is white light. White light contains all the colors of the visible spectrum. When sunlight enters the atmosphere, it collides with gas molecules. The atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen. These molecules are much smaller than the wavelength of visible light.

When light strikes a particle much smaller than its wavelength, it scatters. Scattering is the redirection of light in many directions. The type of scattering caused by particles smaller than the wavelength of light is called Rayleigh scattering. Lord Rayleigh, a British physicist, described this process in the 1870s.

Rayleigh scattering depends strongly on wavelength. Shorter wavelengths scatter much more than longer wavelengths. The relationship follows an inverse fourth-power law. This means that if one wavelength is half as long as another, it scatters 16 times as much. Violet and blue light have the shortest wavelengths in the visible spectrum. They scatter far more than red, orange, or yellow light.

Violet light actually scatters more than blue. So why does the sky look blue instead of violet? Two reasons. The sun emits more blue light than violet. And the human eye is more sensitive to blue than to violet. Our cone cells respond more strongly to blue wavelengths. The combination makes the sky appear blue.

At sunrise and sunset, the sky turns red and orange. Sunlight travels through a much thicker layer of atmosphere when the sun is near the horizon. The shorter wavelengths scatter away before the light reaches you. What remains is red, orange, and yellow. These colors paint the sky at dawn and dusk.

Dust, pollution, and water vapor in the atmosphere change the sky's color. After a volcanic eruption, fine ash in the stratosphere can produce vivid red and purple sunsets for months. On clear days, the sky is a deep blue. On hazy days, extra particles make the sky appear paler.

The sky on other planets is different. Mars has a thin atmosphere filled with fine iron-oxide dust. Iron oxide is the compound that makes rust. The dust scatters red and orange light, so the Martian sky appears butterscotch during the day. Sunsets on Mars appear bluish because of how the dust scatters forward-directed light.

On the moon, there is no atmosphere. Without gas molecules to scatter light, there is no sky color. The sky on the moon is black, even during the day. Stars are visible at all times. The sun is a brilliant disk against pure darkness.

The blue sky is not a simple backdrop. It is a physics lesson displayed above us every clear day. It is the visible result of tiny molecules redirecting tiny waves of light.
