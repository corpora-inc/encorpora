# Shadows

A shadow forms when an object blocks light. Light travels in straight lines. When an opaque object stands in the path of light, it prevents the light from reaching the surface behind it. Opaque means not allowing light to pass through. The dark area behind the object is the shadow.

The shape of a shadow depends on the shape of the object and the angle of the light. Hold your hand in front of a flashlight and you see its shadow on the wall. Move your hand closer to the light and the shadow grows. Move it closer to the wall and the shadow shrinks. Light rays spread from a source. An object closer to the source blocks a wider angle of rays.

Shadows have distinct parts when the light source has size. A point source of light creates a sharp-edged shadow. A point source is a light that appears to come from a single, tiny spot. But most light sources have width.

When a light source has size, the shadow has two regions. The darkest central region is the umbra. The umbra is the area where the object blocks all light from the source. Surrounding the umbra is the penumbra. The penumbra is the area where the object blocks some but not all of the light.

Solar eclipses are shadows. A solar eclipse occurs when the moon passes between Earth and the sun. The moon casts a shadow on Earth. If you stand in the umbra, you see a total solar eclipse. The sky goes dark. Stars become visible. The sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, glows around the moon's silhouette. If you stand in the penumbra, you see a partial eclipse.

The moon is about 400 times smaller than the sun. The sun is about 400 times farther from Earth than the moon. This coincidence means they appear almost the same size in the sky. This is why the moon can just barely cover the sun during a total eclipse.

Lunar eclipses are also shadow events. A lunar eclipse occurs when Earth passes between the sun and the moon. Earth's shadow falls on the moon. During a total lunar eclipse, the moon turns deep red. Earth's atmosphere bends some sunlight into the shadow, scattering away shorter wavelengths and letting red light through. The same physics that makes sunsets red makes the eclipsed moon red.

Shadows reveal information. The length of a shadow tells you the angle of the light source. Around 240 BCE, Eratosthenes used shadows to estimate Earth's circumference. He measured the shadow of a stick in Alexandria at noon on the summer solstice. In Syene, far to the south, the sun was directly overhead and cast no shadow. From the angle difference and the distance between the cities, he calculated Earth's circumference with remarkable accuracy.

Shadows are the absence of light. They are simple in principle and useful in practice. They have helped measure the Earth, predict eclipses, and tell time for thousands of years.
