# Refraction

Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one material to another. Light travels at different speeds in different materials. When light enters a new material at an angle, the change in speed causes it to change direction. This bending is refraction.

Light travels fastest in a vacuum. It slows down when it enters a denser material. In air, light travels almost as fast as in a vacuum. In water, it travels about 25 percent slower. In glass, it travels about 33 percent slower. In diamond, it travels about 59 percent slower.

The amount of bending depends on how much the speed changes. Scientists describe this with the refractive index. The refractive index is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to its speed in the material. A vacuum has a refractive index of 1. Water is about 1.33. Glass is about 1.5. Diamond is about 2.42. A higher refractive index means more bending.

You see refraction every day. A straw in a glass of water looks bent at the surface. It is not actually bent. Light from the submerged part changes direction as it leaves the water and enters the air. Your brain assumes light travels in straight lines, so it misinterprets the straw's position.

A pool of water appears shallower than it really is. Light from the bottom bends when it leaves the water. The bending makes the bottom look closer to the surface. This is why wading into water can surprise you. The water is always deeper than it looks.

Lenses use refraction to focus light. A lens is a piece of transparent material with curved surfaces. A convex lens is thicker in the middle. It bends light inward to a point. Convex lenses are used in magnifying glasses, cameras, and the human eye. A concave lens is thinner in the middle. It spreads light outward. Concave lenses correct nearsightedness.

The human eye is a natural lens system. Light enters through the cornea, a transparent cover at the front. It passes through the pupil, which controls how much light enters. The lens behind the pupil focuses light onto the retina. The retina is a layer of light-sensitive cells at the back of the eye. When the image is sharp, you see clearly. When the lens cannot focus properly, you need corrective lenses.

Prisms demonstrate refraction beautifully. Different wavelengths refract by different amounts. Red light bends the least. Violet bends the most. This is why a prism separates white light into a rainbow. The separation of light into its component colors is called dispersion. Dispersion creates rainbows in the sky. Sunlight enters a raindrop, refracts, reflects off the back, and refracts again as it exits. Each wavelength exits at a slightly different angle. The result is a spectrum spread across the sky.

Refraction shapes how we see the world. It makes lenses possible. It creates rainbows. It explains why pools deceive and straws appear broken. It is the simple consequence of light changing speed.
