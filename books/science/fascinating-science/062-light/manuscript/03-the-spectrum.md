# The Spectrum

White light is not a single thing. It is a mixture of many colors. When you split white light into its components, you see a spectrum. A spectrum is the range of colors that make up light, arranged by wavelength.

In 1666, Isaac Newton performed a famous experiment. He directed a beam of sunlight through a glass prism. A prism is a transparent solid with flat surfaces that bends light. The prism separated the white light into a band of colors: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, and violet. Newton called this band a spectrum. He showed that the prism did not add color to the light. The colors were already there. The prism simply separated them.

Each color corresponds to a different wavelength. Red light has the longest wavelength in the visible spectrum, about 700 nanometers. A nanometer is one billionth of a meter. Violet has the shortest, about 380 nanometers. Orange is about 620 nanometers. Yellow is about 580. Green is about 530. Blue is about 470.

The visible spectrum is the range of wavelengths the human eye can detect. But visible light is only a small fraction of the electromagnetic spectrum. The electromagnetic spectrum is the full range of electromagnetic radiation, from the longest wavelengths to the shortest.

Below red light are infrared waves. Infrared means below red. Infrared has longer wavelengths than red light. You cannot see it, but you feel it as heat. Your body emits infrared radiation. Thermal cameras detect infrared and convert it into visible images.

Below infrared are microwaves. Microwave ovens use them to heat food. Cell towers use them to carry signals. Below microwaves are radio waves. Radio waves have the longest wavelengths. Some are longer than a football field. Radio stations, television, and Wi-Fi all use radio waves.

Above violet are ultraviolet waves. Ultraviolet means beyond violet. The sun emits ultraviolet radiation. It causes sunburn and can damage DNA. The ozone layer absorbs much of the sun's ultraviolet radiation.

Above ultraviolet are X-rays. X-rays have very short wavelengths and high energy. They pass through soft tissue but are blocked by bone. This makes them useful for medical imaging.

Above X-rays are gamma rays. Gamma rays have the shortest wavelengths and the highest energy. They are produced by nuclear reactions and by the most violent events in the universe, such as supernova explosions.

All electromagnetic radiation travels at the speed of light. The only difference between radio waves, visible light, and gamma rays is wavelength. They are all the same phenomenon at different scales. The rainbow is a tiny window into a vast spectrum.
