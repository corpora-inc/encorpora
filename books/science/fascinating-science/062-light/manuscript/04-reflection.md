# Reflection

Reflection is the bouncing of light off a surface. When light hits an object, some bounces back. This reflected light enters your eyes. Reflection is how you see most of the world.

The law of reflection is simple. The angle at which light hits a surface equals the angle at which it bounces off. Scientists call the incoming light the incident ray. They call the outgoing light the reflected ray. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. This law holds for all reflective surfaces.

Smooth surfaces produce specular reflection. Specular means mirror-like. When light hits a smooth surface, all the rays bounce in the same direction. The result is a clear, sharp image. Mirrors work this way. A flat mirror is a sheet of glass with a thin coating of metal on the back. Light passes through the glass, bounces off the metal, and returns. The reflected light preserves the arrangement of the original image, so you see a copy of whatever is in front of the mirror.

Rough surfaces produce diffuse reflection. Diffuse means scattered in many directions. When light hits a rough surface, tiny irregularities send each ray in a different direction. You see the object, but not a mirror image. Most objects produce diffuse reflection. Paper, wood, cloth, and skin all scatter light. This is why you can see a wall from any angle.

The color of an object depends on reflection. A red apple appears red because its surface absorbs most wavelengths and reflects red. A green leaf reflects green and absorbs the rest. A white object reflects all visible wavelengths. A black object absorbs all and reflects very little. Color is not a property of the object itself. It is a property of the light the object reflects.

Mirrors have been used for thousands of years. Ancient Egyptians made mirrors from polished copper. Modern mirrors use glass with metal coatings. Curved mirrors have special properties. A concave mirror curves inward and focuses light to a point. Reflecting telescopes use concave mirrors to gather starlight. A convex mirror curves outward, spreading light and providing a wider field of view. Convex mirrors serve as side mirrors on vehicles.

Total internal reflection is a special case. It occurs when light traveling through a dense medium hits the boundary with a less dense medium at a steep angle. Instead of passing through, all the light reflects back. This is how fiber optic cables work. A fiber optic cable is a thin strand of glass or plastic. Light enters one end and bounces along the inside, reflecting off the walls repeatedly until it reaches the other end. Fiber optic cables carry internet signals across oceans.

Reflection is everywhere. Every image you see, every color you perceive, every mirror and every screen all depend on the simple principle of light bouncing off surfaces.
