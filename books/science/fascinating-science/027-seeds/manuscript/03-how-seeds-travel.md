# How Seeds Travel

A seed that falls directly beneath its parent plant faces a problem. It must compete with its parent for sunlight, water, and nutrients. A seed that travels away has a better chance. The movement of seeds away from the parent plant is called dispersal.

Plants cannot walk. They cannot fly. But their seeds travel in remarkable ways. Plants use wind, water, animals, and even explosions to scatter their seeds.

Wind dispersal is common. Dandelion seeds have a fluffy parachute of fine hairs. The wind catches this parachute and carries the seed long distances. Maple seeds have a wing-like blade. They spin like helicopters as they fall. The spinning slows their descent and lets the wind carry them sideways. Orchid seeds are as fine as dust. They are so light that even a gentle breeze can lift them.

Water dispersal works for plants near rivers, streams, and oceans. Coconut palms grow on tropical beaches. Their seeds are coconuts. A coconut has a thick, waterproof husk. It can float in the ocean for months. Ocean currents carry coconuts to distant shores. The coconut washes up on a beach and germinates. This is how coconut palms colonized islands across the Pacific Ocean.

Animals disperse many seeds. Some seeds have hooks or barbs. Burdock seeds have tiny hooks that cling to animal fur. They inspired the invention of Velcro. Squirrels bury acorns for winter food. They forget some of them. The forgotten acorns sprout into oak trees.

Many plants wrap their seeds in fruit. Fruit is sweet and nutritious. Animals eat the fruit. The seeds pass through the animal's digestive system unharmed. The animal deposits the seeds in a new location, along with a pile of natural fertilizer. Fertilizer is a substance that provides nutrients for plant growth.

Birds are excellent seed dispersers. A bird eats a berry in one place and deposits the seed kilometers away. Some plants depend entirely on one species of bird for dispersal.

Some plants use explosive dispersal. The touch-me-not plant has seed pods that burst open when touched. The seeds fly outward in all directions. The sandbox tree launches seeds at speeds of up to 70 meters per second. That is faster than most cars on a highway.

Ants disperse seeds too. Some seeds have a small, oily attachment called an elaiosome. Ants carry these seeds to their nests. They eat the elaiosome and discard the seed underground. The seed germinates in the nutrient-rich soil of the ant nest.

Seeds are travelers. Each species has its own strategy for reaching new ground.
