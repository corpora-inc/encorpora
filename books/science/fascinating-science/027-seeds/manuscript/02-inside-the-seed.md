# Inside the Seed

A seed has three main parts: the embryo, the food supply, and the seed coat. Each part plays a critical role.

The embryo is the baby plant. It is alive but dormant. Dormant means inactive, waiting for the right conditions. The embryo is tiny. In some seeds, you need a magnifying glass to see it. In others, like a bean, you can see it clearly if you split the seed open.

The embryo has basic structures already formed. It has a tiny root called the radicle. The radicle will become the first root of the new plant. It has a tiny shoot called the plumule. The plumule will become the first stem and leaves. Between the radicle and the plumule is a short axis called the hypocotyl. The hypocotyl connects root to shoot.

The embryo also has one or two seed leaves called cotyledons. A cotyledon is the first leaf that emerges from a seed. Plants are classified by how many cotyledons they have. Monocots have one cotyledon. Mono means one. Grasses, lilies, and orchids are monocots. Dicots have two cotyledons. Di means two. Beans, roses, and oak trees are dicots.

In many dicots, the cotyledons store food for the embryo. Split a bean in half. The two fleshy halves are the cotyledons. They are packed with starch and protein. The embryo uses this food to fuel its first growth.

In many monocots and some dicots, the food is stored in a separate tissue called the endosperm. Endosperm means "within the seed." It surrounds the embryo. It is rich in starch, oils, and proteins. When you eat white rice, you are eating endosperm. When you eat wheat flour, you are eating ground endosperm.

The coconut is an interesting case. The white flesh of a coconut is solid endosperm. The coconut water inside is liquid endosperm. Both nourish the embryo as it grows.

The outer layer of the seed is the seed coat. The seed coat is called the testa. The testa protects the embryo from physical damage. It shields it from disease. It prevents water from entering too soon. The seed coat can be thin and papery, like on a peanut. It can be thick and hard, like on a peach pit.

Some seed coats are so tough that they can survive being eaten by animals and passing through their digestive systems. This toughness is an adaptation. An adaptation is a feature that helps an organism survive.

Three parts. One purpose. Protect the embryo and give it the resources to start a new life.
