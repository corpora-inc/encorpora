## The Global Minimum Compliance Checklist

Most countries set homeschooling expectations that, while different in detail, share a recognizable core. Once you get familiar with these basics, adapting to your local rules becomes much less intimidating. Instead of picturing compliance as a mountain, think of it as learning a basic workout routine: master a few fundamentals, then make small adjustments.

### The pattern most places follow

Across borders, the same checkpoints show up again and again. In plain language, authorities usually want evidence that a real education is happening at home, consistently, in good faith.

Here’s the global “minimum viable” checklist to look for in your country or region:

- **Notify** the relevant authority that you intend to homeschool (sometimes before you start, sometimes annually).
- **Describe a plan** for the year (often a simple outline of subjects, resources, and goals).
- **Provide regular instruction** (some places specify hours or days, others just expect a steady routine).
- **Keep records** that show progress over time.
- **Complete required reviews or assessments** if your area uses them.

### Think in evidence, not paperwork

You usually do not need an elaborate curriculum document or a binder that could qualify as a piece of furniture. Most officials are not asking you to be a school. They are asking you to be a responsible adult who can show what you’re doing.

> **Compliance evidence** is simply proof that learning is planned, happening, and being checked in a reasonable way.

A brief written plan, a predictable weekly rhythm, and a modest set of work samples is often enough. Think of it like keeping a workout log: track the work and the progress just enough to show consistent effort.

### A few habits that make this easy all year

When families get stressed about compliance, it’s rarely because the requirements are impossible. It’s because the information is scattered, the deadlines sneak up, or recordkeeping turns into a weekend-eating project.

A simple setup prevents all three:

- **One home for documents:** a single folder on your computer or a physical binder for official letters, forms, and notes.
- **One page of local requirements:** your country’s checklist with deadlines and renewal dates.
- **A once-a-year refresh:** a quick look at current rules each year (even if they rarely change), so you are never surprised.

## Attendance and Instructional Time: Tracking Simply

Most parents find they do not need to record every minute or log every activity in detail. The main thing officials look for is regular, honest evidence that learning is happening.

Think **consistent, not constant**. A steady pattern matters more than a minute-by-minute account.

### Pick a method you will actually keep

Choose a recordkeeping method that fits your real life. A log stuffed with details is useless if you abandon it by October.

Here are a few low-stress options that work in the real world:

- **Daily checkmark log:** One line per school day. Check the box if you did learning that day, and jot one sentence if something stood out.
- **Weekly summary:** Once a week, write three to five sentences about the main activities plus a rough sense of time.
- **Lightweight category log:** Track a few broad buckets (language arts, math, science, social studies, electives). Note what you did, not how perfectly you did it.

If you are torn, start with the daily checkmark. It is the smallest habit that still produces a clear record.

### What counts as instructional time

Instructional time stretches well beyond traditional lessons. Any time your child is intentionally learning, it counts: reading quietly, discussing questions at the kitchen table, working through math puzzles, science walks, nature observation, helping in the kitchen with measurements, art projects, documentaries, building things, library visits, even focused play.

> If your child was engaged and you can describe the point of the activity in one sentence, it is valid instructional time.

In practice, **compound activities** (one project that covers multiple subjects) are your best friend. They create more learning per unit of parent energy. Researching how to roast a pork shoulder? That can cover reading, science, math, and writing in one.

### Make it hard to forget

To make your system reliable, keep your log where learning usually happens, or in a spot you will see every day. Update it daily. Do not save up a week’s entries and try to catch up all at once.

The aim here is not perfection. It is a running log that reassures both you and any official who checks your records: **clear, steady, and easy to keep up with**.


## Portfolios and Work Samples: What to Keep

A good portfolio is less like a scrapbook and more like a growth chart. It documents steady progress and effort over time. The goal is not to collect everything or curate only “best work.” It’s to keep a clear, honest record that shows learning happened, and that skills grew.

If your records are ever reviewed, most authorities are looking for something simple: evidence of routine instruction and a reasonable spread of work across the year. You do not need a complicated system for that.

### The minimum that usually satisfies “proof of learning”

Think in **dated samples + a short paper trail**.

- **Monthly:** 1 to 2 dated samples per major subject
- **Quarterly:** a short parent note about progress, challenges, and what you’ll adjust
- **Year-long:** a running list of books read and major projects completed

> **A “sample” is anything dated that shows your child doing real work.** A worksheet counts. A paragraph counts. A math page with mistakes and corrections counts.

### What counts as a work sample

Keep typical work, not just highlight reels. A balanced portfolio usually includes a mix like this:

- **Writing:** a journal entry, a story draft with revisions, a short report
- **Math:** a page showing the current topic, including corrections
- **Reading:** a book list, a narration summary, a photo of a reading log
- **Science and history:** a notebook page, a map, a timeline entry, a photo of a simple experiment
- **Art and hands-on work:** a photo with a one-sentence caption

For anything that doesn’t produce paper, take a quick photo and label it with a date and a short note (“Built a lever, tested three weights” is plenty).

### A low-stress system you will actually maintain

Pick one home for everything: a binder, an accordion folder, or a single digital folder. The win is not perfection. The win is **consistency**.

Here’s a practical rhythm that stays small:

- Put work in a “to-file” stack during the month.
- At month’s end, choose the **most typical** one or two pieces per subject and file them.
- Add your quarterly note when you change seasons, finish a unit, or simply notice a shift.

The real payoff is twofold: you have solid evidence that instruction is happening, and you can see growth that’s easy to miss day to day. When you flip back a few months and notice handwriting smoothing out, sentences getting longer, or math work getting more independent, you’ll know where to focus next and you’ll have proof to match.




## Evaluations and Testing: When and Why

Evaluations are not a verdict on your parenting or your child’s worth. Their job is simpler: **confirm progress**, **document learning when you need to**, and **catch learning needs early**, when help is most effective.

If you keep the stakes low, assessments become a normal part of your rhythm, like checking your form during a workout. You are not looking for perfection. You are looking for useful feedback.

> **Assessment** is any intentional check of learning that helps you decide what to keep, what to change, and what to document.

### The three main reasons to assess

Assessments tend to earn their keep in three situations.

- **Reassurance:** You want to know your approach is working and your child is making steady gains.
- **Compliance:** You need clear evidence for local authorities, a school district, or a future enrollment step.
- **Early support:** Something feels “sticky” and you want to respond quickly and confidently (reading, writing, math facts, attention, fatigue, frustration tolerance).

### Common types you might encounter

Different regions use different systems, but most requirements land in a familiar set.

- **Parent evaluation:** A short written summary of growth in the core areas. This is often enough, and it helps you spot patterns over time.
- **Teacher or third-party review:** A licensed teacher or approved reviewer looks through your child’s work, usually as a portfolio, and gives brief written feedback. This can be required, or simply reassuring.
- **Standardized testing:** Sometimes required every few years, usually focusing on reading and math (and occasionally science or social studies). Treat it as one data point, not “the truth.”

### Timing: think “light, often” plus “formal, when required”

You do not need constant measuring. A simple cadence is usually plenty.

- **Weekly or biweekly:** A quick look through recent work to notice progress and pick one small next step.
- **Quarterly (every 9 to 12 weeks):** A slightly longer review of samples, reading level, writing pieces, and math comfort.
- **Annually (or as required):** Your official evaluation, portfolio review, or standardized test window.

If you are seeing real struggle, assess sooner. Early support is cheaper than later rescue, in both time and stress.

### Keeping it low-stress

The easiest way to reduce pressure is to make assessment feel like normal life: “Let’s look at what you’ve been doing and what’s getting easier.” Sit with your child, notice a few wins, name one strength, and choose **one** next step.

If standardized testing is required, practice the *process* more than the content. Do a short timed session so the timer stops being scary. Set up a quiet work space. Break tasks into single questions. Keep your tone neutral and supportive: “This is a chance to show what you know,” works far better than, “This determines your future.”

The stakes with routine assessments are low, and improvement is always possible next time. Done well, evaluations help you spot what’s working, address what’s not, and keep your homeschool compounding, as small gains in reading, writing, and math stack into a more capable, confident learner over time.


## Georgia Example

Georgia is a clear, practical example of how homeschool rules often work in the U.S. Think of it less as a script to follow and more as a template for making sense of compliance wherever you are. The routines Georgia requires hint at something universal: what most governments are really checking for, even when their wording differs.

### What Georgia typically asks for

Georgia homeschoolers generally handle a small set of recurring tasks: a yearly notice, a basic school-year expectation, a few records, and an occasional outside check.

- File an annual **Declaration of Intent**.
- Teach the usual **core subjects** (reading and language arts, math, social studies, science).
- Meet a **time expectation** (commonly described as 180 instructional days, averaging about 4.5 hours).
- Keep **attendance records**.
- Write a simple **annual progress report**.
- Arrange a **standardized achievement test every three years**, beginning in third grade.

If that list feels like “a lot,” remember: most of it is light documentation of what you are already doing. You do not need complex paperwork. With a few minutes of steady weekly attention, compliance starts to feel like keeping a workout log: simple, consistent, and easy to reference if you ever need it.

### The universal pattern hiding underneath

Even if you do not live in Georgia, the *shape* of the requirements is familiar in many places.

> **Compliance is usually proof of three things:** you notified someone, your child is receiving regular instruction in core skills, and you can show basic evidence of progress.

That is it. The details vary (which form, which subjects, which test), but the intent is usually the same: steady effort, broad academic coverage, and a paper trail that does not require you to become a part-time administrator.

### How to translate your local rules into a simple system

When you look up your own regulations, sort them into a few buckets. This helps you avoid rereading the same legal language all year.

- **Notification:** What do I file, with whom, and when?
- **Time and attendance:** Do days or hours matter, and how is “instruction” defined?
- **Subjects:** What must be taught (and how strict is that list)?
- **Documentation:** What records are required, and for how long?
- **Assessment:** Is there testing, evaluation, portfolio review, or another check-in?

If something is unclear, ask what the rule is trying to ensure, then call or email the relevant office with direct questions until you can explain the requirement in plain language. Once you can say, “Here is what they want, here is how we will do it,” you are basically done.

Think of compliance as a light warm-up before the main workout: repeatable, low-drama, and meant to support your real job, not replace it. When your documentation routine is small enough to maintain week to week, you stay both compliant and sane.
