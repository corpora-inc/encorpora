# Sling angle and load per leg

Skills: `rigging.sling-angle`. Exam domain: Operations (rigging questions), Technical Knowledge. Rule cited for the general duty: 29 CFR 1910.184(c)(4), "Slings shall not be loaded in excess of their rated capacities." The angle arithmetic is physics, not a rule.

## The idea

A sling leg pulls along its own line. Only the upward part of that pull holds the load. Lean the leg over and more of its pull goes sideways, squeezing the load, so the leg must pull harder to hold the same share up.

Tension per leg = (load ÷ number of legs) × (L ÷ H), where L is the leg length and H the height from the hook down to the attachment level. L ÷ H is the load angle factor. Measure the angle from the horizontal: at 90° the factor is 1, at 60° about 1.15, at 45° about 1.41, at 30° exactly 2.

No calculator: measure L and H. Legs 10 ft, height 5 ft: factor 2.

## The moment

The hook lowers, the legs flatten, the angle arc closes and the tension readout climbs, slowly at first and then steeply. At 30° each leg of a two-leg bridle carries the whole load.

## Cases (three)

1. **Straight up.** 4,000 lb, two vertical legs: 2,000 lb each.
2. **60°.** Legs 10 ft, height 8.66 ft: factor about 1.15, about 2,309 lb each.
3. **30°.** Legs 10 ft, height 5 ft: factor 2, 4,000 lb each: each leg as heavy as the whole load. Common rigging practice avoids angles under 30°.

## Close

Share per leg times L over H. Angle from the horizontal. Flatter legs, more tension, and fast below 45°.
